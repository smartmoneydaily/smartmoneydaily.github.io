The card is declined at the pharmacy counter. The balance the app showed that morning was $1,900, and nothing had been spent since. Two business days earlier, someone at the bank opened a court order from a firm that bought a medical debt in 2019, and the account went cold.

Part of that $1,900 was never legally freezable. The bank was supposed to work out how much before the card ever stopped working.

## A review runs before you hear about any of it

A creditor cannot simply reach into a deposit account. The [CFPB](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/can-a-debt-collector-garnish-my-bank-account-or-my-wages-en-1439/) puts the threshold plainly: most creditors can only garnish wages or benefits after a court issues a judgment saying that you owe the debt. Once that judgment exists, the creditor takes it to the bank, and the bank becomes the party holding money that two people are claiming.

What happens next is governed by a Treasury rule that binds the bank rather than the creditor. Under [31 CFR 212.4](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/212.4), the institution has two business days to examine the order and see whether a Notice of Right to Garnish Federal Benefits came attached to it. If one did, the bank follows its otherwise customary procedures. If none did, which is the ordinary case for a private debt, the bank has to run an account review before it touches the balance.

[Section 212.5](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/212.5) sets that deadline at no later than two business days following receipt of the order and enough information to identify the account holder. The review is narrow. The bank looks for one thing: whether a benefit agency deposited a benefit payment into that account during the lookback period. [Section 212.3](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/212.3) defines the period as the two month period that begins on the date preceding the date of account review and ends on the corresponding date of the month two months earlier.

Out of that comes a figure the rule calls the protected amount: the lesser of the sum of all benefit payments posted to the account during that window, or the balance in the account when the account review is performed.

Two monthly Social Security deposits of $1,750 put $3,500 on one side of that comparison. A balance of $1,900 sits on the other. The protected amount is $1,900 and the creditor gets nothing from this account. Raise the balance to $6,000 and the protected amount is still $3,500, leaving $2,500 exposed.

| What the review finds | What happens to the balance |
|---|---|
| Benefit payments posted in the lookback, balance above zero | The protected amount stays fully available and the bank may not freeze it |
| Balance above two months of benefits | The excess follows the bank's ordinary garnishment procedure |
| No benefit payment in the lookback | No protected amount is set, and the whole balance follows ordinary procedure |
| A Notice of Right to Garnish Federal Benefits came with the order | Sections 212.5 and 212.6 do not run at all |

The review also ignores several things people assume would matter. It is performed without consideration for the presence of other funds, from whatever source, that may be commingled in the account. Where a debtor holds more than one account, the bank runs the review separately for each and does not trace the movement of funds between accounts.

## Protected is not the same as arguable

[Section 212.6](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/212.6) is unusually blunt about what the bank owes the customer. The institution has to ensure that the account holder has full and customary access to the protected amount, which it may not freeze in response to the garnishment order. That amount is conclusively considered to be exempt from garnishment under law, and the account holder does not have to assert any right of garnishment exemption before reaching it.

That removes the step most people brace for. No form, no hearing, no negotiation with the collections firm to get at that money. It should be spendable the same afternoon.

The fee rule follows the same logic. A bank may not charge or collect a garnishment fee against a protected amount, so the processing charge cannot be taken quietly out of the two months of benefits the rule just shielded. Any fee the bank does collect in the five business days after the review has to come from funds that are not part of the protected amount.

One more provision in 212.6 gets mishandled often enough to be worth memorizing. The bank performs the account review only one time, on the first service of a given order, and it may not continually garnish amounts deposited or credited to the account following the date of account review. Next month's benefit payment is not captured by last month's order. If new deposits are still being held, something has gone wrong at the bank, and waiting it out is the wrong response.

## The protection rides on the payment file, not on the money

Here is the mechanical detail that decides who gets the automatic version of all this. Section 212.3 defines a benefit payment as a federal payment made by direct deposit carrying the characters XX in positions 54 and 55 of a specific field in the payment record. The tag travels inside the file the government sends through the ACH network. The bank's review is a search for that tag.

So the protection attaches to how the money arrived, not to where it originally came from. The [CFPB](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/) states the consequence without the plumbing: if you receive Social Security or VA benefits by check and then deposit the check into your bank account, the bank does not have to protect two months' worth.

Now apply that to a habit this site encourages constantly. Benefits land in a checking account at one bank. Every month the balance gets swept into a high-yield savings account somewhere else, because the rate there is better by a point and a half. The money reaching the second bank arrives as an ordinary transfer. No tag. And under 212.5 that second bank reviews its own account on its own terms, with no duty to trace where the funds came from.

The transfer that earns the extra yield also strips the automatic protection off the balance. On $5,000, a point and a half of additional APY is roughly $75 a year. Whether that is a sensible trade depends entirely on whether a judgment against you is a live possibility or a distant one. For most households it is distant and the yield wins outright. For someone already served with a lawsuit, or behind on a debt that has been sold twice, the calculation is not close.

There is a version that keeps both. Leave two months of benefits sitting in the account where the direct deposit lands and sweep only what piles up above that line. The protected amount is capped at two months of payments in any case, so the balance beyond it was never going to be shielded by this rule regardless of which bank held it.

The reverse move deserves a warning of its own. Parking an unrelated $20,000 in the same account the benefits land in does not extend the protection to that money. It relocates it to the first place a creditor will look.

## Which benefits, and which orders

The automatic review covers a specific list. [Section 212.2](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/212.2) ties it to payments from the Social Security Administration, the Department of Veterans Affairs, the Railroad Retirement Board, and the Office of Personnel Management, each keyed to the statute that makes those particular payments exempt.

The CFPB's [list of protected benefits](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/can-a-debt-collector-garnish-my-federal-benefits-en-1157/) is longer, and the gap between the two lists matters. It includes federal student aid, FEMA assistance, servicemember pay, and military annuities alongside Social Security, SSI, and VA benefits. Those are protected from garnishment by law. They are not all inside the machinery that delivers the protection without you lifting a finger. A benefit outside the Part 212 account review is one you may have to claim yourself.

The larger carve-out is about who is collecting.

| Who holds the order | Does the automatic review run | What that means for benefits in the account |
|---|---|---|
| A private creditor with a judgment | Yes | Two months of directly deposited benefits stay accessible |
| The United States, collecting a federal debt | No, where a Notice of Right to Garnish Federal Benefits is attached | Ordinary procedures, and Social Security can be reached for back taxes or federal student loans |
| A state child support enforcement agency | No, on the same basis | Ordinary procedures, and support obligations can reach Social Security |
| The IRS, by levy | No | A 21-day waiting period runs before the bank sends the funds |

SSI is the exception inside that exception. The CFPB states that Supplemental Security Income is protected from garnishment even to pay a government debt or child or spousal support.

On the levy, the [IRS](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/information-about-bank-levies) says the Internal Revenue Code provides a 21-day waiting period for complying with a levy on a bank account, and that the point of the wait is to allow time to contact the agency, arrange payment of the tax, or report an error in the levy. The agency also notes that normally the levy does not affect funds added to the account after the date of the levy. Those 21 days are the whole window, and they run whether or not anyone has opened the mail.

## The notice, and the shorter clock it starts

When the review finds benefits, establishes a protected amount, and money remains above it, the bank has to write to you. [Section 212.7](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/212.7) requires that notice within three business days from the date of account review, issued directly to the account holder or to a fiduciary who administers the account.

Its content is specified rather than left to the bank's drafting. The notice has to say that a garnishment order arrived and when, give a succinct explanation of garnishment, identify the account and the protected amount, disclose the amount of any garnishment fee charged, list the federal benefits the rule covers, and set out the right to assert additional exemptions and to consult an attorney or a legal aid service. Where the bank has it, the creditor's name and contact details go in as well.

That last item is the one to act on. The protected amount is handled for you. Everything above it is governed by state law, and the CFPB notes that state exemptions may also protect some wages or property, such as money in a bank account, from garnishment. Those exemptions are generally not automatic. They are claimed on a filing with a court, and the deadline comes from the state rather than from the Treasury rule. The notice is usually the first dated document in the whole file, which makes it worth photographing rather than filing away.

## What this changes on a normal Tuesday

1. **Work out your own protected number now.** Two months of benefit deposits, or the balance, whichever is smaller. It is one line of arithmetic, and it is the figure to measure a frozen balance against instead of arguing in the dark.
2. **Leave the direct deposit where the government sends it.** The tag in the payment file is what makes the protection automatic, and it survives neither a check deposit nor a transfer between banks.
3. **Sweep the surplus, not the base.** Money above two months of benefits was never inside the protected amount, so that is the slice to move to the better rate.
4. **Keep unrelated savings out of the benefits account.** Commingling expands nothing. The bank is instructed to run its review without regard to other funds sitting in the account.
5. **Read the notice for the fee and the creditor.** A garnishment fee taken out of the protected amount is a violation of the rule, not a service charge to absorb.
6. **Complain to the right regulator.** [Section 212.11](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/212.11) places enforcement with the federal banking agencies, and a bank's primary federal regulator sits on its public [FDIC](https://www.fdic.gov/resources/deposit-insurance) record, one of the fields worth reading in [any BankFind lookup](/2026/09/09/how-to-verify-a-bank-is-actually-fdic-insured-before-you-transfer/).

Two dates decide the outcome, and the creditor sets both of them. The day the order reaches the bank fixes the review date, and the review date fixes the protected amount. Nothing deposited afterward raises that number, and nothing withdrawn beforehand can be put back to improve it.
