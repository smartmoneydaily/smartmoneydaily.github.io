Two savings accounts can hold identical money, carry identical federal insurance, and pay amounts that differ by hundreds of dollars over a year. The difference is not risk, and it is not complexity. It is one number printed in a disclosure.

This post hands you the arithmetic to find your own gap. Not a generic illustration, but the dollars your specific balance is or is not earning, plus the terms that quietly shrink that figure, and a way to decide whether to move all of it, split it, or leave it alone and stop revisiting the question.

One thing you will not find below is a high-yield APY presented as today's market rate. Any number like that is a single bank's offer on a single day, and it would be stale before you finished reading. What does not go stale is the method, and the FDIC's published national averages, which give you a fixed line to measure against.

## The one difference that matters

Both accounts are deposit accounts at an insured institution. Both hold cash rather than investments. The functional difference is the APY the bank chooses to pay and the conditions attached to earning it.

"High-yield" is a marketing phrase rather than a defined product category. The label commits a bank to nothing. The disclosure does, and it is where the four answers live: the APY, whether that APY applies to your whole balance or only part of it, the minimum required to earn it, and the fees.

Compare APY to APY. APY includes compounding, a stated interest rate does not. Two accounts advertising the same interest rate can pay different amounts if they compound on different schedules, and the APY is the figure that already settles that difference.

## The national averages, as of August 17, 2026

The FDIC publishes national deposit rate averages across insured institutions. They are not offers you can open, and they are not the best rates available. They are a reference line.

| Product | National average APY |
|---|---|
| Savings | 0.38% |
| Interest checking | 0.07% |
| Money market | 0.63% |
| 6 month CD | 1.41% |
| 12 month CD | 1.71% |

If your account pays near the savings average, you now know where it sits relative to insured institutions overall. If it pays noticeably less, that is worth a look at your bank's current rate sheet.

## The dollar gap, worked out

One formula does all of the work:

**annual gap = balance x (new APY - current APY)**

Worked example. Balance of $15,000. The current account pays 0.38%, the FDIC national average for savings as of August 17, 2026. The offer in front of you quotes 3.00% APY. Treat that 3.00% strictly as a placeholder for the arithmetic and substitute whatever your actual offer states.

- current account: 15,000 x 0.0038 = **$57.00** a year
- the offer: 15,000 x 0.0300 = **$450.00** a year
- the gap: 450.00 - 57.00 = **$393.00**

Shortcut, same answer in one step: 15,000 x (0.0300 - 0.0038) = 15,000 x 0.0262 = $393.00.

Per month, 393.00 / 12 = **$32.75**.

Balance times APY is the correct one-year figure for a balance that sits still, since APY already contains a year of compounding. If you add to the account through the year, the real total comes in higher, so the simple version is a conservative floor rather than an overstatement.

Now scale it down, because scale changes the answer. On a $2,000 balance the same 2.62 percentage-point spread produces 2,000 x 0.0262 = $52.40 a year. Identical spread, $52.40 instead of $393.00. Work out which of those two numbers you are actually deciding about before you set aside an evening for the switch.

## Terms that eat into the gap

The gap you calculated is a ceiling, not a promise. Four things in the disclosure can reduce it, and each is checkable before you open anything.

- **Monthly maintenance fees.** A $5 monthly fee costs $60.00 a year. Applied to the example above: 393.00 - 60.00 = **$333.00** left. Whether that fee can be waived, and on what conditions, is stated in the fee schedule.
- **Tiered or conditional APY.** The headline rate may apply only above a minimum balance, only up to a balance cap, or only when you meet a monthly requirement. The disclosure states which.
- **Transfer limits.** Regulation D's six-transfer limit was suspended in 2020, though many banks still set their own limits. Your deposit agreement lists yours and the fee for exceeding them.
- **Transfer timing.** External transfers move on the bank's stated schedule, in business days. If the money is your emergency cash, read that section before, not during, the emergency.

One more: the APY on a savings account is variable. The bank can change it, and your agreement states how you will be notified. A gap you calculate today is a snapshot, which is an argument for rechecking once or twice a year, not for skipping the calculation.

## Insurance on both sides

The insurance question has a short answer, and it is the same answer on both sides of the comparison.

FDIC standard coverage is $250,000 per depositor, per insured bank, per ownership category. Credit unions carry the same standard limit through the NCUA. Ownership categories, including single, joint, certain retirement accounts, and revocable trusts, are insured separately, so a couple's joint account is covered apart from each person's individual account.

Three details worth knowing. Two consumer brands can operate on a single bank charter, and deposits at both count against one $250,000 limit for that charter. The FDIC's BankFind tool shows which brands share a charter. Trust account coverage rules were revised effective April 1, 2024, so if a trust is in the picture, run your actual setup through the FDIC's EDIE estimator instead of applying an older formula. And deposits at a failed bank are insured, while investments are not. A money market deposit account at a bank is a deposit. A money market mutual fund is an investment product and carries no FDIC insurance. The names sit close together; the protection does not.

## All, split, or none

Splitting is a legitimate third answer, and it costs something specific. Using the same $15,000 and the same placeholder rates:

- $3,000 stays put at 0.38%: 3,000 x 0.0038 = **$11.40**
- $12,000 moves at 3.00%: 12,000 x 0.0300 = **$360.00**
- combined: 11.40 + 360.00 = **$371.40**

Against leaving everything where it is at $57.00, the split earns 371.40 - 57.00 = **$314.40** more. Against moving the entire balance at $450.00, the split gives up 450.00 - 371.40 = **$78.60**.

That $78.60 is the price of keeping same-day branch access to $3,000. Whether that is a good purchase depends on facts about your life rather than facts about banking: how quickly you have needed cash in the past, what your card and overdraft terms look like, and how long a transfer between the two institutions actually takes when you test it.

A rough guide:

- **Move all of it** when the balance is genuinely idle, you already bank online comfortably, and nothing about the money needs a teller.
- **Split it** when part of the balance is your near-term buffer and you want it reachable the same day.
- **Stay put** when the calculated gap is small enough that you would not notice it, or when a branch relationship is doing something for you that a rate cannot replace.

## A switch checklist

If you decide to move, the work is short and mostly reading.

1. Confirm the institution is FDIC insured, or NCUA insured for a credit union, using the FDIC's BankFind tool or the credit union's regulator listing.
2. Read the fee schedule and the rate disclosure before funding, specifically the minimum to earn the APY and any monthly fee.
3. Open and fund with a small test transfer first, then confirm the timing you actually experienced.
4. Move the rest, keeping enough in your existing account to cover any auto-payments still pointed there.
5. Recheck your APY against the FDIC national average twice a year, since both numbers move.

## The gap in one line

Balance times the spread between the two APYs, minus twelve months of fees. That is the entire calculation, and it takes under a minute once you have both rates in front of you.

Run it with your real balance and your real quoted rate. If the answer is a few hundred dollars, you have a reason to spend an evening on it. If the answer is a few dollars, you have permission to close the tab and think about something else. Either way you will have replaced a vague suspicion that you are leaving money behind with a number you calculated yourself.
