![Is Your LLC's Bank Account FDIC Insured Separately From Your Own Money?](/assets/pin-images/2026-10-01-is-your-llcs-bank-account-fdic-insured-separately-from-your-own-money.png)

Yes, with two conditions attached. A limited liability company, corporation or partnership that has been validly formed under state law and is run as a real business gets its own $250,000 of [FDIC](https://www.fdic.gov/resources/deposit-insurance) coverage at a bank, and that $250,000 is counted separately from the personal accounts of the people who own it. The FDIC's [Financial Institution Employee's Guide to Deposit Insurance](https://www.fdic.gov/financial-institution-employees-guide-deposit-insurance/corporation-partnership-and-unincorporated) states the result in one line: "Deposits held in the name of a validly formed corporation, partnership, or unincorporated association are insured separately from the personal deposits of the owners or officials of the organization." A sole proprietorship, including any account opened under a "doing business as" name, gets nothing of the kind. Its balance is added to the owner's personal accounts and insured with them, once, up to the same $250,000.

The two conditions are the whole subject. The first is formation: the entity has to exist as a matter of state law, not as a name on a check. The second is what the regulation calls an "independent activity," a test aimed at entities that exist only to increase deposit insurance. Pass both and the entity is its own depositor. Fail either and the FDIC looks straight through the entity to the people behind it, adds each person's share of the business balance to that person's own accounts, and applies a single $250,000 cap to the total.

Inside the category, what matters to an owner with several accounts is the arithmetic. Every deposit one entity holds at one bank is added together against one cap, however the accounts are labeled and however many people can sign on them. The guide's own example is a five-partner firm that put a $1,250,000 certificate of deposit in the partnership's name at one bank and found $1,000,000 of it uninsured. Five partners meant one cap.

## What the regulation says

The rule is [12 CFR 330.11](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/12/330.11), and it is short. Paragraph (a)(1) covers corporations: "The deposit accounts of a corporation engaged in any 'independent activity' (as defined in § 330.1(g)) shall be added together and insured up to the SMDIA in the aggregate. If a corporation has divisions or units which are not separately incorporated, the deposit accounts of those divisions or units shall be added to any other deposit accounts of the corporation." The SMDIA is the standard maximum deposit insurance amount, which [12 CFR 330.1](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/12/330.1) sets at $250,000.

Paragraph (b) does the same for partnerships and adds the sentence that makes the category worth having: "The deposit accounts of a partnership engaged in any 'independent activity' (as defined in § 330.1(g)) shall be added together and insured up to the SMDIA in the aggregate. Such insurance coverage shall be separate from any insurance provided for individually owned (single ownership) accounts maintained by the individual partners." The paragraph also says what a partnership is for this purpose: "an association of two or more persons or entities formed to carry on, as co-owners, an unincorporated business for profit."

Paragraph (c) extends the same treatment to an unincorporated association, defined as "an association of two or more persons formed for some religious, educational, charitable, social or other noncommercial purpose," with its deposits insured "separately from the accounts of the person(s) or entity(ies) comprising the unincorporated association."

Then paragraph (d), which is the one to read twice. Under the heading "Non-qualifying entities," it provides: "The deposit accounts of an entity which is not engaged in an 'independent activity' (as defined in § 330.1(g)) shall be deemed to be owned by the person or persons owning the corporation or comprising the partnership or unincorporated association, and, for deposit insurance purposes, the interest of each person in such a deposit account shall be added to any other deposit accounts individually owned by that person and insured up to the SMDIA in the aggregate."

The definition everything hangs on is in 12 CFR 330.1(g): "A corporation, partnership or unincorporated association shall be deemed to be engaged in an 'independent activity' if the entity is operated primarily for some purpose other than to increase deposit insurance." The FDIC's guide for bank employees restates it from the other direction: "The term 'independent activity' means that the entity is operated primarily for a legitimate business purpose and not solely to increase deposit insurance coverage. If a corporation, partnership, or unincorporated association is not engaged in an 'independent activity', the FDIC will consider its deposits to be owned by the person or persons who established the account or who own or control the corporation, partnership, or unincorporated association."

Neither text sets a revenue floor, an employee count or an age for the entity. The test is purpose. A one-person consulting LLC that invoices clients and pays its expenses from the account is operated for a business purpose. An LLC formed last week whose only activity is holding a CD that would otherwise have pushed its owner over the limit is the case the definition was written for.

## Who is inside the category, and who is not

The FDIC guide lists the entities the category covers. A corporation "is defined as an organization that is incorporated under the laws of the state in which it is located. This definition includes both for-profit and not-for-profit corporations, as well as 'Subchapter S,' 'Limited Liability (LLC)' and 'Professional (PC)' corporations." A partnership and an unincorporated association are defined as in the regulation. The FDIC's consumer brochure [Your Insured Deposits](https://www.fdic.gov/resources/deposit-insurance/brochures/insured-deposits) puts the coverage rule in one sentence: "All deposits owned by a corporation, partnership, or unincorporated association at the same bank are combined and insured up to $250,000."

Formation is a precondition, not a formality. The guide: "Corporations and partnerships must be validly formed under the applicable state law to receive insurance coverage that is separate from any coverage that the owners, officers, or partners of the business might otherwise receive." What "validly formed" requires is a question of state law, not of what the bank prints on a statement.

The entity outside the category is the one most side businesses are. The guide is direct: "Accounts of a sole proprietorship or a DBA are not insured under this account category. Sole proprietorship accounts are insured as the single accounts of the owner. As discussed in the section on single account coverage, DBA or sole proprietorship accounts are added with any other single accounts of the sole proprietor and insured for up to the SMDIA." The FDIC's [single accounts chapter](https://www.fdic.gov/financial-institution-employees-guide-deposit-insurance/single-accounts) gives the reason: "For deposit insurance purposes, a sole proprietorship has no separate legal existence or identity beyond that of the sole proprietor." The consumer brochure lists "An account held in the name of a business that is a sole proprietorship (e.g., a 'Doing Business As' [or DBA] account)" among the things that count as a single account.

The dividing line is not whether the account carries a business name or whether the bank calls it business checking. It is whether a separate legal person exists under state law to own the deposit. An LLC is such a person. A DBA is a nickname.

The single accounts chapter has a worked example that puts the DBA rule in dollars. Marci Jones holds a $55,000 savings account in her own name, a $25,000 demand deposit account titled "Marci Jones DBA Marci's Cakes," and a $200,000 CD placed at the same bank through a brokerage as her agent. The three are added together as her single accounts: $280,000 in balances, $250,000 insured, $30,000 uninsured. The business account did nothing to raise the ceiling. How the seven ownership categories relate to each other at one bank, and why single, joint and retirement balances get separate caps while a DBA does not, is laid out in [How FDIC Ownership Categories Stack Coverage at One Bank](https://smartmoneydaily.github.io/2026/08/21/how-fdic-ownership-categories-stack-coverage-at-one-bank/).

## One entity, one bank, one cap

The rule that catches established businesses is aggregation. Under 330.11(a)(1), a corporation's accounts "shall be added together," and the guide spells out three things that do not change the cap.

Labels do not help. "If a corporation has divisions that are not separately incorporated, deposits in the names of those divisions are not separately insured. Additionally, deposit accounts designated for different purposes but held by the same corporation also are not separately insured." A payroll account, a tax reserve account and an operating account belonging to the same LLC at the same bank are one pool of $250,000.

Signers do not help. "The number of signatories on the account or the number of officers does not affect insurance coverage." Adding a second member as a signer changes who can move the money and nothing about how much of it is insured.

Partners do not help either, and this is where the biggest balances go wrong. "The maximum deposit insurance coverage for a partnership's deposit accounts at one IDI is $250,000, regardless of the number of partners or signatories on the account." IDI is the guide's abbreviation for an insured depository institution.

Two of the guide's examples show the aggregation rule with round numbers. In Example 18, EFG, Inc. keeps $250,000 in an account for its Operations division and $300,000 in an account for its Research and Development division at the same bank. The answer: "Although the accounts are designated for different divisions, one for Operations and another for Research and Development, the deposits will be aggregated and insured for up to $250,000 as the funds of EFG, Inc. Since only $250,000 would be insured, the remaining $300,000 would be uninsured." In Example 19, a nonprofit called Good Deeds holds a $500,000 operating account and a $300,000 building fund at one bank; the result is $250,000 insured and $550,000 uninsured, because "the same non-profit corporation owns all the deposits."

The cap is per bank, and that is the only lever the rule leaves. The same entity with the same $550,000 split across three insured banks, none holding more than $250,000, is fully covered. That is the ordinary answer for a business whose cash exceeds the cap, along with the deposit-network arrangements and Treasury alternatives described in [Insuring More Than $250,000 Without Opening Ten Accounts](https://smartmoneydaily.github.io/2026/09/02/insuring-more-than-250000-without-opening-ten-accounts/).

## Three owners, three results

Take one bank and three people who each run a business from it. Every figure below is invented for the example; only the rule comes from the FDIC.

Dana runs a design business through a single-member LLC, validly formed and operating for years. At one bank the LLC has $180,000 in checking and $120,000 in savings. Dana also has $210,000 in personal accounts at the same bank. The LLC's two accounts are added together to $300,000 and insured to $250,000, leaving $50,000 uninsured. Dana's personal $210,000 is a separate depositor's money and is fully insured. Total exposure at the bank: $50,000.

Now suppose Dana had never formed the LLC and had opened the same two business accounts under a DBA. The $300,000 of business money and the $210,000 of personal money are all Dana's single accounts. Added together they come to $510,000, of which $250,000 is insured. Exposure: $260,000, on the same balances at the same bank.

Jordan and Lee are the two members of a general partnership that keeps $400,000 in one operating account. Jordan has $100,000 in a personal account at the bank; Lee has $20,000. If the partnership is validly formed and engaged in an independent activity, the $400,000 is the partnership's deposit, insured to $250,000, and $150,000 is exposed. Jordan's and Lee's personal accounts are fully covered on their own.

Change one fact. Suppose the partnership was set up only to hold that cash and does nothing else, so that a claims examiner applies 330.11(d) and treats the deposit as owned by the partners. Assume the two hold equal interests. Jordan's $200,000 share is added to Jordan's personal $100,000: $300,000 in single accounts, $250,000 insured, $50,000 exposed. Lee's $200,000 share is added to Lee's $20,000: $220,000, fully insured. Exposure across the two partners is $50,000, less than the $150,000 exposed when the partnership qualified. That is not a strategy. The non-qualifying rule does not zero out coverage; it reroutes the money through the owners' personal caps, and the result depends on how full those caps already are.

| Depositor | Structure | Business balance | Personal balance at same bank | Insured | Uninsured |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Dana | Single-member LLC, qualifying | $300,000 | $210,000 | $460,000 | $50,000 |
| Dana | Same accounts as a DBA | $300,000 | $210,000 | $250,000 | $260,000 |
| Jordan and Lee | Partnership, qualifying | $400,000 | $100,000 and $20,000 | $370,000 | $150,000 |
| Jordan and Lee | Partnership, non-qualifying, equal interests | $400,000 | $100,000 and $20,000 | $470,000 | $50,000 |

The first two rows are the practical point for anyone running a side business. Forming the LLC did not change what Dana earns or owes. It changed which depositor owns $300,000 of cash, and at this bank that is worth $210,000 of additional coverage.

## The partnership CD

The guide's Example 20 deserves its own reading, because the mistake it describes is an easy one to make. Mary Rodriguez and Associates is a financial services partnership established by five financial planners. At the local bank, one partner "purchased a CD for $1,250,000 in the name of the partnership." The question posed is the maximum insurance on the CD.

The rule stated in the example: "Deposits held by a partnership are insured for up to $250,000 irrespective of the number of partners or the different purposes for which the accounts have been designated." And the answer: "The partnership's deposits are not insured as the partners' single or joint accounts since the deposits are owned by the partnership (a legal entity), not the partners as individuals. Accounts owned by the partnership are added together and insured for up to $250,000, with $1,000,000 in uninsured funds."

The intuition the example corrects is the joint-account one. Five people who co-owned a personal joint account would each have a share insured under the joint category. Five people who are partners in an entity bring nothing individually, because the entity owns the deposit and they do not; the consumer brochure says outright that legal entities such as partnerships "are not eligible for joint account coverage." The same logic protects the partners in the other direction: the guide's Example 17 shows Ann Johnson, president and authorized signer of XYZ Corporation, holding a $250,000 corporate account, a $500,000 joint account with her husband and a $250,000 single account at the same bank, all $1,000,000 insured, because her signing authority over the corporate account does not make its balance hers.

| Account title | Owner | Balance | Insured | Uninsured |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| XYZ Corporation Operating Account | XYZ Corporation | $250,000 | $250,000 | $0 |
| Dave Johnson and Ann Johnson | Dave Johnson, Ann Johnson | $500,000 | $500,000 | $0 |
| Ann Johnson | Ann Johnson | $250,000 | $250,000 | $0 |
| Mary Rodriguez and Associates, LP | The partnership | $1,250,000 | $250,000 | $1,000,000 |

The first three rows are Example 17 and the last is Example 20. Together they show the category doing both of its jobs: keeping an owner's personal coverage intact whatever her role at the company, and refusing to let the company's coverage grow with the number of people attached to it.

## Exceptions and edge cases

Separately incorporated subsidiaries get separate caps. The guide: "Separately incorporated subsidiaries engaged in an 'independent activity' are separately insured from each other and from the parent company." A holding LLC with two operating subsidiaries, each a validly formed entity running a real business, is three depositors at one bank. Two divisions of one LLC are one depositor. The distinction is whether each is separately incorporated.

Fiduciary balances are not the entity's balances. Under 330.11(a)(1), "If a corporation maintains deposit accounts in a representative or fiduciary capacity, such accounts shall not be treated as the deposit accounts of the corporation but shall be treated as fiduciary accounts and insured in accordance with the provisions of § 330.7." An LLC that holds clients' money as their agent does not spend its own $250,000 on that money; under [12 CFR 330.7(a)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/12/330.7), funds deposited in the name of an agent, custodian or nominee "shall be insured to the same extent as if deposited in the name of the principal(s)."

Unincorporated associations have a titling trap. The guide: "In order for the account to receive separate coverage, the title of the account must include the name of the unincorporated association. In other words, if the accounts are titled using the names of the officers of the organization, the accounts may be insured as the personal deposits of the individuals and not as the funds of the organization." A club treasurer who opens the account in his own name with the club's money has, for insurance purposes, opened a personal account, and its balance will be added to his other single accounts at that bank. The fix is in the title.

The non-qualifying rule has no bright line. Neither the regulation nor the guide says how much activity is enough. The definition asks what the entity is "operated primarily" for. An entity that has been running a business for years and an entity formed the week before a large deposit, with no other purpose, sit at opposite ends of that question. The call belongs to the FDIC: in the guide's words, if the entity is not engaged in an independent activity, "the FDIC will consider its deposits to be owned by the person or persons who established the account or who own or control" it.

## What to do

For an LLC or corporation, three things settle it. Confirm the entity is validly formed under state law, since the FDIC's separate coverage depends on it. Title every business account in the entity's exact legal name, so that the bank's records show the entity, and not a person, as the owner. Then add up every account the entity holds at that bank and compare the total with $250,000; that sum, not any single account, is what the cap applies to.

For a partnership, do the same three things, and drop the assumption that the number of partners matters. A firm whose cash regularly exceeds $250,000 at one bank has to move the excess to another insured institution, into a deposit-placement network or into instruments that are not deposits at all. There is no titling arrangement inside one bank that expands a partnership's cap.

For a sole proprietorship or DBA, the question changes. The business account is a personal single account. Add its balance to every other single account the owner holds at that bank, including any CD a broker placed there, and compare that total with $250,000. If the sum is over, the options are a second bank or a change in the business's legal structure, and the second is a decision far larger than deposit insurance.

The FDIC's [Electronic Deposit Insurance Estimator](https://edie.fdic.gov/) covers, in its own words, "Business Accounts—deposits held by corporations, partnerships, and organizations, both for-profit and not-for-profit," and runs the aggregation for a given bank.

### Does a single-member LLC count, or does the FDIC treat it like a sole proprietorship?

It counts, if it is validly formed under state law and operated for a business purpose. The FDIC guide's definition of a corporation for this category expressly includes "Limited Liability (LLC)" corporations and does not set a minimum number of members. What matters is the state filing and the independent-activity test, not how the LLC is taxed. A sole proprietorship is different because, in the guide's words, it "has no separate legal existence or identity beyond that of the sole proprietor."

### Can a business get more than $250,000 at one bank by opening a second account for a different purpose?

No. "Deposit accounts designated for different purposes but held by the same corporation also are not separately insured," and the same aggregation applies to partnerships and unincorporated associations. The guide's Example 19 shows a nonprofit with an operating account and a building fund at one bank insured to a single $250,000. Separate coverage at one bank requires a separately formed entity engaged in its own independent activity, not a separate account.

### Does an owner's personal money get counted against the business's $250,000?

Not when the business qualifies. Under 330.11(b) partnership coverage "shall be separate from any insurance provided for individually owned (single ownership) accounts maintained by the individual partners," and the guide's Example 17 shows a corporate president's single and joint accounts insured in full alongside the corporation's account at one bank. The exception is a non-qualifying entity under 330.11(d), where each owner's interest in the business deposit is added to that owner's own single accounts.

The category is simple once the two conditions are met and unforgiving before that. A real entity, validly formed and running a business, is a depositor in its own right with its own $250,000 at each bank, and its owners keep theirs. Everything else a business might do with its accounts, from labeling them to adding signers to adding partners, changes nothing about the cap. And a DBA, whatever the bank calls the account, was never an entity at all.
