The banner on the bank's home page says $300, in type larger than the rate. The small link underneath opens the terms: open a new savings account, bring $15,000 in new money within 30 days, keep it there for 90 days, and the bonus is credited within 30 days after that. The saver reading it already has $15,000 in a high-yield account at another bank, and three questions come up before the application does. Is $300 more than the interest given up by moving? What happens if some of that money is needed in week six? And does $300 from a bank belong on a tax return?

The answers come from two sets of federal rules, and they do not agree on what the $300 is.

A savings account bonus is taxable income in the year it is credited, even though federal deposit rules say it is not interest. Under Truth in Savings, a bonus is kept out of the APY, and the advertisement and account disclosures have to state its conditions separately: when it is paid, the minimum balance, and how long the money has to stay. The IRS works from the other direction. Noncash gifts for opening an account are reported as interest above small thresholds, and a cash bonus arrives either as interest on Form 1099-INT or, when a bank treats it as other income, on Form 1099-MISC, where the filing threshold for 2026 payments is $2,000. A bonus under that line can arrive with no form and still has to be reported. Before opening, check the interest you give up over the whole hold, the fee and penalty lines that can take the bonus back, and which tax form the bank uses.

## Why a bonus is not interest under Truth in Savings

Regulation DD, the [Consumer Financial Protection Bureau](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/)'s rule implementing the Truth in Savings Act, defines the term at [12 CFR 1030.2(f)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-12/section-1030.2): "Bonus means a premium, gift, award, or other consideration worth more than $10 (whether in the form of cash, credit, merchandise, or any equivalent) given or offered to a consumer during a year in exchange for opening, maintaining, renewing, or increasing an account balance. The term does not include interest, other consideration worth $10 or less given during a year, the waiver or reduction of a fee, or the absorption of expenses."

Interest, defined in the same section as "any payment to a consumer or to an account for the use of funds in an account, calculated by application of a periodic rate to the balance," likewise "does not include the payment of a bonus." The official commentary printed with [the regulation](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-12/chapter-X/part-1030) puts it in one sentence: "Bonuses are not interest for purposes of this part."

That split decides what the advertised rate includes. [Appendix A to Part 1030](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-12/appendix-Appendix%20A%20to%20Part%201030), which sets out the APY math, says "The annual percentage yield reflects only interest and does not include the value of any bonus (or other consideration worth $10 or less) that may be provided to the consumer to open, maintain, increase or renew an account." An account paying a $300 bonus and one paying nothing extra can advertise the same APY. APY is the figure meant for comparing banks, and it leaves the bonus out, so the bonus terms have to be read on their own. The regulation gives them separate disclosure rules.

The commentary also spells out what does not count:

- **Small items.** "Items with a de minimis value of $10 or less are not bonuses," and "Institutions may rely on the valuation standard used by the Internal Revenue Service to determine if the value of the item is de minimis."
- **Fee waivers.** "Bonuses do not include value that consumers receive through the waiver or reduction of fees (even if the fees waived exceed $10)."
- **Coupons.** "Items that are not a bonus include discount coupons for goods or services at restaurants or stores."

A waived monthly fee therefore triggers none of the bonus disclosures, even when it is worth more over a year than a small cash offer.

## What the advertisement has to tell you

[12 CFR 1030.8(d)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-12/section-1030.8) sets the rule: "if a bonus is stated in an advertisement, the advertisement shall state the following information, to the extent applicable, clearly and conspicuously:"

1. "The 'annual percentage yield,' using that term;"
2. "The time requirement to obtain the bonus;"
3. "The minimum balance required to obtain the bonus;"
4. "The minimum balance required to open the account, if it is greater than the minimum balance necessary to obtain the bonus; and"
5. "When the bonus will be provided."

Vague language does not trigger the list: "General statements such as 'bonus checking' or 'get a bonus when you open a checking account' do not trigger the bonus disclosures." An ad that states the bonus itself, such as a dollar amount, is covered.

Online ads do not get the exemption that lets television, radio, and billboards skip some items. The commentary says "The exemption for advertisements made through broadcast or electronic media does not extend to advertisements posted on the Internet or sent by email." A web banner can keep the details on another page, as long as it points there: an electronic ad that displays "a triggering term (such as a bonus or annual percentage yield)" must "clearly refer the consumer to the location where the additional required information begins." The small link under the $300 banner is that reference.

A poster in a branch lobby is treated differently: "Signs inside the premises of a depository institution (or the premises of a deposit broker) are not subject to paragraphs (b), (c), (d) or (e)(1) of this section." It can show a bonus amount without the five items, though every ad remains barred from being "misleading or inaccurate."

## The account disclosures are the document to read

The terms that bind the bank are in the account disclosures, and [12 CFR 1030.4](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-12/section-1030.4) sets the timing: "A depository institution shall provide account disclosures to a consumer before an account is opened or a service is provided, whichever is earlier." For an online application, the disclosures "must be provided before the account is opened or the service is provided." They are also available "upon request," so they can be read before applying.

The bonus line, at 1030.4(b)(7), requires "The amount or type of any bonus, when the bonus will be provided, and any minimum balance and time requirements to obtain the bonus." Appendix B's model clause shows the shape it can take:

> You will [be paid/receive] [$\_\_\_\_/(description of item)] as a bonus [when you open the account/on (date) \_\_\_\_]. You must maintain a minimum [daily balance/average daily balance] of $\_\_\_\_ to obtain the bonus. To earn the bonus, [$\_\_\_\_/your entire principal] must remain on deposit [for (time period)/until (date)\_\_\_\_].

The bank picks one option in each bracket, and the pick matters. A minimum daily balance can be missed on a single day; an average daily balance tolerates a dip that the rest of the period makes up. "Your entire principal" means a partial withdrawal can cost the whole bonus. The same list requires "The amount of any fee that may be imposed in connection with the account (or an explanation of how the fee will be determined) and the conditions under which the fee may be imposed," which is where a charge for closing an account early belongs. Other lines worth reading are covered in [Six Lines in a Bank Disclosure That Decide What You Earn](/2026/08/24/six-lines-in-a-bank-disclosure-that-decide-what-you-earn/).

Terms can change after opening, with limits. [12 CFR 1030.5(a)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-12/section-1030.5) requires advance notice of "any change in a term required to be disclosed under § 1030.4(b) of this part if the change may reduce the annual percentage yield or adversely affect the consumer," delivered "at least 30 calendar days before the effective date of the change." Bonus terms are on that list. Missing a condition is a different matter: the commentary says an institution offering "terms that will automatically change upon the occurrence of a stated event need not send an advance notice" if it "fully describes the conditions of the change in the account opening disclosures." And no notice is required for "Changes in the interest rate and corresponding changes in the annual percentage yield in variable-rate accounts." The rate that makes the move worthwhile can drop the week after funding.

## When the bank takes the bonus back

On a CD, a clawback is a penalty by definition. Time account disclosures must include "A statement that a penalty will or may be imposed for early withdrawal, how it is calculated, and the conditions for its assessment," and the commentary's examples of early withdrawal penalties include "Reclamation of bonuses." A CD bonus that vanishes when the CD is broken early is disclosed under that line. The broader math is in [When Breaking a CD Actually Pays: Run the Break-Even](/2026/08/25/when-breaking-a-cd-actually-pays-run-the-break-even/).

On a savings account, look in two places: the time requirement in the bonus clause, and the fee schedule for any charge on closing within a set period. If the money must stay until a date and the bonus posts after that, the real holding period runs from funding to the day the bonus is credited, not just the 90 days in the headline.

## Running the numbers against the interest you give up

The gain is the bonus plus interest at the new bank, minus the interest the money would have earned where it was. A hypothetical $15,000 kept at the new bank for 120 days (a 90-day hold plus up to 30 days for posting), with the current account paying a hypothetical 4% APY:

| Scenario (hypothetical, pre-tax) | Interest | Bonus | Fee | Total |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Stay at 4% APY, 120 days | $195 | $0 | $0 | $195 |
| Move to 3% APY, 120 days | $146 | $300 | $0 | $446 |
| Move to 1% APY, 120 days | $49 | $300 | $0 | $349 |
| Move to 3% APY, close on day 45 | $55 | $0 | $25 | $30 |
| Stay at 4% APY, 45 days | $73 | $0 | $0 | $73 |

At $15,000 the bonus wins if the terms are met, even into a low-rate account; $300 spread over 120 days is roughly 6% annualized on its own. The fourth row is the risk. Close early and the bonus never posts, a hypothetical $25 closing fee comes out, and the move earns less than staying would have.

A bigger deposit requirement for the same bonus changes the answer. $50,000 at 4% APY for 120 days earns about $649. At a new bank paying 1%, it earns about $164, so the bonus brings the total to $464 and the move loses about $185. At 3%, it earns about $488 and comes out about $139 ahead. The larger the required balance, the more the new bank's APY matters.

The figures are before tax, and both the interest and the bonus are taxable.

## How the IRS taxes a bank bonus

The tax rules do not use Regulation DD's definition. [IRS Publication 550](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p550), the edition for 2025 returns, treats gifts for opening an account as interest income: "If you receive noncash gifts or services for making deposits or for opening an account in a savings institution, the value may be reported to you as interest income on Form 1099-INT and you may have to report it on your tax return. For deposits of less than $5,000, gifts or services valued at more than $10 must be reported as interest. For deposits of $5,000 or more, gifts or services valued at more than $20 must be reported as interest. The value is determined by the cost to the financial institution."

Its example: "You open a savings account at your local bank and deposit $800. The account earns $20 interest. You also receive a $15 calculator. If no other interest is credited to your account during the year, the Form 1099-INT you receive will show $35 interest for the year."

A cash bonus takes one of two reporting paths, depending on how the bank classifies it.

**As interest, on Form 1099-INT.** The [Instructions for Forms 1099-INT and 1099-OID](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i1099int) require a form for each person "To whom you paid amounts reportable in boxes 1, 3, or 8 of at least $10." For some institutions the net is wide: Box 1 includes "amounts of $10 or more, whether or not designated as interest, that are paid or credited to the person's account by savings and loan associations, mutual savings banks not having capital stock represented by shares, building and loan associations, cooperative banks, homestead associations, credit unions, or similar organizations," along with "interest on bank deposits."

**As other income, on Form 1099-MISC.** The current [Instructions for Forms 1099-MISC and 1099-NEC](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i1099mec) say "For tax years beginning after 2025, the minimum threshold amount for reporting certain payments required to be reported on certain information returns and/or perform backup withholding on those payments increased to $2,000 and may be adjusted for inflation beginning in calendar year 2027." A bank that treats a $300 bonus paid in 2026 as other income has no filing requirement for it.

No form does not mean no tax. Pub 550: "You must report all your interest income even if you do not receive a Form 1099-INT." [Publication 525](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p525) states the general rule: "In most cases, an amount included in your income is taxable unless it is specifically exempted by law." When a 1099-MISC does arrive, [the form's](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/f1099msc.pdf) recipient instructions say "Box 3. Generally, report this amount on the 'Other income' line of Schedule 1 (Form 1040) and identify the payment."

**Which year.** The credit date decides. Pub 550 says you receive earnings "on any deposit or account in a bank, savings and loan association, or similar financial institution" when "they are credited to your account and subject to your withdrawal." An account opened in October 2026 whose bonus is credited on January 12, 2027 produces 2027 income.

**Withholding.** Pub 550 says "Your investment income is generally not subject to regular withholding," with backup withholding as the exception: the payer of interest "must withhold income tax on these reportable payments at a rate of 24% under backup withholding" if, among other triggers, "You do not give the payer your TIN in the required manner." For new accounts, "Your payer will give you a Form W-9, or similar form, to make this certification." Skip it, and a $300 bonus reported as interest can land as $228. The rest of the tax picture on savings is in [The Tax Bite on Savings Interest: What Your Real Yield Is](/2026/08/26/the-tax-bite-on-savings-interest-what-your-real-yield-is/).

## When a reported bonus is reversed

The awkward case is a bonus credited, reported, and then reclaimed.

For CDs there is a box for forfeitures. The 1099-INT instructions describe Box 2 as "interest or principal forfeited because of an early withdrawal of time deposits, such as an early withdrawal from a certificate of deposit (CD), that is deductible from gross income by the recipient," and add "Do not reduce the amount reported in box 1 by the amount of the forfeiture." Pub 550 gives the depositor's side: "You must include in income all interest shown in Form 1099-INT, box 1. You can deduct the penalty on Schedule 1 (Form 1040), line 18."

Whether a particular bank puts a reclaimed bonus in that box is its reporting call; the IRS text quoted here does not settle every case. It does settle the remedy for a wrong form: "If you receive a Form 1099 that shows an incorrect amount (or other incorrect information), you should ask the issuer for a corrected form." If a form shows the full bonus and nothing for a clawback in the same year, ask the bank how it reported the reversal, with the statement showing it in hand.

## Credit union bonuses under NCUA rules

Regulation DD "applies to depository institutions except for credit unions." The [NCUA](https://ncua.gov/consumers/share-insurance-coverage) has a parallel rule at [12 CFR Part 707](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-12/chapter-VII/subchapter-A/part-707) that covers the same ground in credit union vocabulary.

Its definition at [12 CFR 707.2](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-12/section-707.2) matches almost line for line: "Bonus means a premium, gift, award, or other consideration worth more than $10 (whether in the form of cash, credit, merchandise, or any equivalent) given or offered to a member during a year in exchange for opening, maintaining, or renewing an account, or increasing an account balance." Dividends, in turn, do not include "the payment of a bonus," and Part 707's APY appendix says the yield "reflects only dividends and does not include the value of any bonus." The advertising rule lists the same five bonus items, and the NCUA's official staff interpretations also name "Reclamation of bonuses" as an early withdrawal penalty.

The word "dividend" does not change the tax treatment. [IRS Tax Topic 403](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc403) says "certain distributions, commonly referred to as dividends, are actually taxable interest. They include dividends on deposits or on share accounts in cooperative banks, credit unions, domestic building and loan associations, domestic federal savings and loan associations, and mutual savings banks." How share insurance compares is in [Credit Union Share Insurance: How NCUA Coverage Compares](/2026/08/31/credit-union-share-insurance-how-ncua-coverage-compares/).

## Large bonus tiers and the insurance limit

Offers that scale with the deposit can push a balance past the insurance limit. The [FDIC](https://www.fdic.gov/resources/deposit-insurance) states it on its [Understanding Deposit Insurance](https://www.fdic.gov/resources/deposit-insurance/understanding-deposit-insurance) page: "FDIC deposit insurance covers $250,000 per depositor, per FDIC-insured bank, for each account ownership category."

A saver who already holds a $75,000 CD at a bank in her own name, with no beneficiaries, and moves $200,000 into a new savings account there to reach a top tier has $275,000 in the single-account category at one bank, $25,000 of it over the limit. The bonus terms say nothing about that. Ways to title accounts for more coverage at one bank are in [How FDIC Ownership Categories Stack Coverage at One Bank](/2026/08/21/how-fdic-ownership-categories-stack-coverage-at-one-bank/).

The same page notes that "FDIC deposit insurance only covers deposits, and only if your bank is FDIC-insured." If an offer comes from an app that holds deposits at a partner bank, run [the insurance check](/2026/09/09/how-to-verify-a-bank-is-actually-fdic-insured-before-you-transfer/) before moving money. A new application can also run into deposit-account screening, covered in [Denied a Bank Account Because of ChexSystems: What to Do](/2026/09/16/denied-a-bank-account-because-of-chexsystems-what-to-do/).

## Checklist from the offer page to tax season

| When | What to check | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Reading the ad | APY, time requirement, bonus minimum balance, opening minimum if higher, payment date | 12 CFR 1030.8(d); 707.8(d) for credit unions |
| Before applying | Bonus clause and fee schedule in the account disclosures | 12 CFR 1030.4(b)(4) and (b)(7) |
| Running the math | Bonus plus new interest, minus interest given up, over funding, hold, and posting time | Appendix A: APY excludes the bonus |
| During the hold | Change-in-terms notices; rate cuts on variable-rate accounts need none | 12 CFR 1030.5(a) |
| Large deposits | Total per ownership category at that bank, existing CDs included | FDIC: $250,000 per depositor, per bank, per category |
| Bonus credited | The credit date sets the tax year | Pub 550 |
| Tax season | 1099-INT, 1099-MISC, or no form; report it either way | 1099-INT and 1099-MISC instructions; Pub 550; Pub 525 |
| Bonus reclaimed | Box 2, or a corrected form | 1099-INT instructions; Pub 550 |

For the saver looking at the $300 banner, Regulation DD makes the bank put the conditions in writing before the account opens, and the IRS expects the money on a return whether or not a form shows up. The $300 comes through if the balance stays where the clause says, and it is income in the year it posts.
