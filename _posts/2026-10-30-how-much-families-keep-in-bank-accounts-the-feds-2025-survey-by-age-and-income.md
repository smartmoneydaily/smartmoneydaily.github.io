On Oct. 9, the [Federal Reserve Board released](https://www.federalreserve.gov/newsevents/pressreleases/other20261009a.htm) the results of its 2025 Survey of Consumer Finances, the survey it runs every three years on what American families own and owe. For savers, the most useful part is what it found in families' cash accounts. In 2025, 98.7% of families had at least one transaction account, the survey's category for checking, savings and money market accounts, money market funds, brokerage cash and prepaid cards. Among those families, the median balance across all of those accounts combined was $8,200, down 6% from 2022 after adjusting for inflation. The mean was $62,900, down 8%.

Ownership of two other savings products changed more. The share of families with certificates of deposit jumped from 6.5% in 2022 to 10.7% in 2025, the highest share in the survey since 2010, and the Fed's report says the increase "likely reflects the increased returns to holding such an asset in 2025, compared with 2022." Savings bonds went the other way. Only 5.4% of families held them, the lowest share in any survey back to 1989, and the median holding fell from $2,200 to $1,000. Fewer families said they had saved at all over the past year: 53.4%, down from 56.5% in 2022.

The sections below break those numbers out by age, income and family type. They also explain which accounts the survey counts, how its medians work and how to compare your own balances with them. Dollar figures are in 2025 dollars unless otherwise noted.

This article is based on the Fed's report, [Changes in U.S. Family Finances from 2022 to 2025](https://www.federalreserve.gov/publications/files/scf26.pdf); the [historic tables](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/scfindex.htm) published with it, in [2025 dollars](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/files/scf2025_tables_internal_real_historical.xlsx) and [nominal dollars](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/files/scf2025_tables_internal_nominal_historical.xlsx), and the [variable definitions](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/files/bulletin.macro.txt) behind them; two of the three FEDS Notes released the same day, on [spending out of a windfall](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/notes/feds-notes/heterogeneity-in-the-marginal-propensity-to-consume-among-u-s-households-20261009.html) and on [informal support networks](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/notes/feds-notes/informal-support-networks-in-the-survey-of-consumer-finances-20261009.html); ## What the Fed released on Oct. 9

The release has three parts. The report summarizes changes in income, net worth, assets, debt and what it calls financial vulnerability. The historic tables repeat the main breakdowns for every survey back to 1989, in nominal dollars and in 2025 dollars. Three FEDS Notes, short papers by the same staff, look at questions that were new in 2025: total spending, what families would do with unexpected money, and help from friends and relatives. The Fed's press release describes the data as "a representative picture of what Americans own, how and how much they borrow, and how they bank."

The survey is conducted for the Fed by NORC at the University of Chicago. According to the release, participants "are chosen at random from 119 geographic areas, including metropolitan areas and rural counties across the United States." A second, supplemental sample, the "list sample," is drawn from statistical records derived from tax returns to include more wealthy families, who hold a large share of some assets. In 2025, 4,367 families were interviewed, compared with 4,602 in 2022. Weighted, the 2025 results represent 135.0 million families.

The timing affects how to read every number. The report says the majority of the data are collected between April and December of the survey year, and "for the 2025 SCF, about one-seventh of the interviews were conducted between January and May 2026." Balances are measured as of the interview. Income is for the calendar year before the survey, so the 2025 survey's income figures describe 2024. The report adjusts dollar amounts to 2025 dollars using the "current methods" version of the consumer price index for all urban consumers.

The headline results were mixed. Real median family income rose 7% between the 2022 and 2025 surveys to $82,200, and real median net worth rose 2% to $215,900. The report also found more families falling behind on loan payments, covered further below.

## What counts as a transaction account

The survey doesn't report savings accounts by themselves. Its basic cash category is the transaction account, and the definition in the report's appendix is broad:

> "Transaction accounts include checking, savings, and money market deposit accounts; money market funds (MMFs); call or cash accounts at brokerages; and prepaid debit cards."

Three details in the appendix change what the $8,200 median covers.

- **Some tax-advantaged accounts count as savings.** "The savings account category includes tax-preferred accounts such as medical or health savings accounts and Coverdell or 529 education accounts." A health savings account, for instance, is counted as savings. Our guide to [2027 HSA limits and where HSA cash is insured](/2026/10/03/2027-hsa-limits-4500-and-9000-and-where-hsa-cash-is-insured/) covers those accounts.
- **Brokerage cash counts.** Call accounts "include those that hold money received from the sale of securities until the money is reinvested."
- **Some apps count.** Prepaid debit cards, "collected in the SCF for the first time in 2016, include reloadable prepaid debit cards, government benefit cards, and some online payment services and mobile apps that include an account feature that allow families to receive and store money (such as Venmo, CashApp, Paypal, and the apps for Starbucks and Apple)."

Other cash-like holdings are separate categories and aren't in the $8,200. Certificates of deposit "are accounts held for a set period of time that must be cashed or renewed at the maturity date." Savings bonds "include only U.S. government issues; recent series include EE and I, and older bonds may be series E, H, and HH." Retirement accounts, including IRAs and 401(k) plans, are another category, held by 54.9% of families with a median of $106,000.

The survey's category isn't a deposit insurance category, and the difference matters for anyone with a large balance. [The FDIC's list of covered accounts](https://www.fdic.gov/resources/deposit-insurance/understanding-deposit-insurance) at insured banks includes checking accounts, NOW accounts, savings accounts, money market deposit accounts and time deposits such as CDs. Its list of what isn't covered includes "mutual funds." Investor.gov lists money market funds among the main categories of mutual funds, says many investors use them "to store cash or as an alternative to bank savings vehicles," and states that "mutual funds are not guaranteed or insured by the FDIC or any other government agency." Our guide to [money market accounts vs. money market funds](/2026/08/22/money-market-account-vs-money-market-fund-whats-insured/) goes through the difference. Our guide to [where brokerage cash actually sits](/2026/08/30/where-brokerage-cash-actually-sits-and-what-insures-it/) covers sweep programs.

Apps raise a separate question. [The FDIC also says](https://www.fdic.gov/resources/deposit-insurance/understanding-deposit-insurance) its deposit insurance "doesn't cover default or bankruptcy of any non-FDIC-insured institution." Our guide to [when "FDIC insured" means a partner bank, not your app](/2026/09/08/when-fdic-insured-means-a-partner-bank-not-your-app/) explains how that plays out. Credit union accounts are covered by the [NCUA](https://ncua.gov/consumers/share-insurance-coverage)'s Share Insurance Fund, which "provides up to $250,000 of federal share insurance to millions of account holders in all federal credit unions and the overwhelming majority of state-chartered credit unions."

## The $8,200 median and the $62,900 mean

The survey's medians and means are "conditional." They describe only the families that hold the asset. With 98.7% of families holding a transaction account, that barely matters for bank balances, but it matters a great deal for CDs and savings bonds.

The report defines the median as "the value at which equal parts of the population considered have values that are larger or smaller." Half of the families with transaction accounts had less than $8,200 across all of them, and half had more. The mean adds up everyone's balances and divides by the number of families, so a relatively small number of very large balances pulls it up. In 2025 the mean of $62,900 was more than seven times the median.

The report gives a rule for reading the two together: "where a comparable median and mean are given, the gain or loss of the mean relative to the median may usually be taken as indicative of the relative change at the top of the distribution." Between 2022 and 2025, after inflation, the median fell 6% and the mean fell 8%. It also warns that "medians are not additive." Its example is that "median assets minus median liabilities will generally not equal median net worth," and for the same reason the median transaction account and the median CD can't be added to get a typical family's total cash.

The decline appears only after adjusting for inflation. In the Fed's nominal tables, the 2022 median was $8,000 in 2022 dollars. The report's factor for converting 2022 assets into 2025 dollars is 1.0944, and the inflation-adjusted table shows the same median as $8,800. The 2025 median of $8,200 was higher in nominal dollars than the 2022 median, but it bought less.

Over a longer stretch, the 2025 numbers are still high. These are the Fed's figures for all families, in 2025 dollars.

| Survey | Families with a transaction account | Median balance | Mean balance |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1989 | 85.5% | $5,300 | $36,300 |
| 2001 | 91.4% | $7,200 | $43,400 |
| 2007 | 92.1% | $6,300 | $41,300 |
| 2013 | 93.2% | $5,700 | $50,600 |
| 2016 | 98.0% | $6,100 | $54,300 |
| 2019 | 98.2% | $6,700 | $52,800 |
| 2022 | 98.6% | $8,800 | $68,400 |
| 2025 | 98.7% | $8,200 | $62,900 |

Only 2022 had a higher median or a higher mean in the full series, which runs every three years from 1989. The jump in ownership between 2013 and 2016 coincided with the first survey to collect prepaid debit cards.

Transaction accounts also shrank as a share of families' financial assets. In the Fed's Table 5, they were 9.8% of all families' financial assets in 2025, down from 12.2% in 2022. Retirement accounts rose from 35.9% to 39.3%, and CDs from 1.3% to 1.9%.

## Bank balances by age

The tables group families by the age of a "reference person," a label the Fed uses "as a means of organizing the data consistently." For a couple, the reference person is "the male in a mixed-sex couple or the older person in a same-sex couple," so a couple's age group follows one partner's age.

| Age of reference person | Families with a transaction account | Median, 2025 | Median, 2022 (2025 dollars) | Mean, 2025 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Under 35 | 98.1% | $5,000 | $5,900 | $20,500 |
| 35 to 44 | 98.4% | $6,800 | $8,200 | $40,800 |
| 45 to 54 | 98.7% | $9,400 | $9,600 | $62,800 |
| 55 to 64 | 98.4% | $10,000 | $8,700 | $90,600 |
| 65 to 74 | 99.6% | $10,000 | $14,800 | $79,100 |
| 75 or older | 99.5% | $14,000 | $10,900 | $97,200 |

Medians rise with age, but far less steeply than means. The median for families 75 and older, $14,000, was 2.8 times the youngest group's $5,000. The mean for the oldest group was about 4.7 times the youngest group's.

Four of the six age groups had lower medians than in 2022. The largest move was for families 65 to 74, whose median fell from $14,800 to $10,000, while the median for families 75 and older rose from $10,900 to $14,000. Changes in a single age group should be read with caution. Each group is a slice of 4,367 interviews, and the report says that "although the statistical significance of the results generally is not addressed," it highlights "findings that are significant or are interesting in a broader context." Its discussion of transaction accounts doesn't break them out by age at all. The age figures come from the tables.

## Bank balances by income

The income groups are percentile ranges. The first four each contain 20% of families, and the last two each contain 10%. The second column, from the Fed's Table 1, is the median income within each group, which helps in placing a household.

| Income group | Median income in the group | Families with a transaction account | Median balance | Mean balance |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Bottom 20% | $22,400 | 95.3% | $800 | $7,600 |
| 20th to 40th percentile | $49,300 | 98.5% | $3,000 | $16,000 |
| 40th to 60th | $82,200 | 99.8% | $7,500 | $31,900 |
| 60th to 80th | $130,500 | 100% | $16,100 | $48,400 |
| 80th to 90th | $204,500 | 100% | $40,000 | $86,900 |
| Top 10% | $401,300 | 100% | $105,000 | $327,200 |

The spread is much wider by income than by age. The middle fifth's median balance, $7,500, was more than nine times the bottom fifth's $800, and the top tenth's median, $105,000, was 14 times the middle fifth's. Families without any transaction account were most common in the bottom fifth, where 95.3% had one and 4.7% didn't. The top tenth's median fell from $121,600 in 2022, the largest dollar drop of any income group, while the others moved by $3,100 or less.

At the top, balances run into deposit insurance limits. A mean of $327,200 in transaction accounts doesn't mean a family keeps that much at one bank, but large balances raise the question. FDIC coverage is "$250,000 per depositor, per FDIC-insured bank, for each account ownership category," and the FDIC's own examples give a single account and a joint account at the same bank separate coverage. Whether a large balance is fully insured depends on how it is split across banks, owners and ownership categories, and on how much of it sits in money market funds or brokerage cash that aren't bank deposits. Two of our guides go through the categories: one on [how FDIC ownership categories stack coverage at one bank](/2026/08/21/how-fdic-ownership-categories-stack-coverage-at-one-bank/) and one on [insuring more than $250,000](/2026/09/02/insuring-more-than-250000-without-opening-ten-accounts/).

## Who saved in the past year

Table 1 of the historic tables answers a different question: not what families hold, but whether they spent less than their income over the past year. In 2025, 53.4% of families saved, down from 56.5% in 2022 and 58.6% in 2019. The Fed's definitions file counts a family as saving if it "spent less than income" over the past year, "adjusting for durables purchases/investments." The table's note says "for questions on saving, respondents were asked to base their answers on the 12 months preceding the interview."

The share fell in five of the six income groups and held steady in the bottom fifth. The biggest drop came in the second fifth, from 48.7% to 41.2%. By age, families under 35 were the only group in which the share rose; the steepest decline was among families 45 to 54.

## What families would do with a month's income

The 2025 survey also tried a hypothetical, analyzed in one of the FEDS Notes. Respondents were asked: "Imagine you unexpectedly receive a sum of money equal to one month of your family's normal annual income. Thinking over the next 12 months, please indicate the percent of this money that you would save, the percent that you would spend, and the percent that you would use to pay down debts."

On average, the note says, households "would spend 22 percent of a one-month windfall of income within the next year, save 47 percent into assets, and use 30 percent to pay down debt." Roughly 47% of households would spend none of it, and around 4% would spend all of it. Based on households' normal income, the median implied windfall was $7,000 and the mean $11,800.

Saving was the largest of the three choices at every income level. The saved share was "relatively flat over the bottom two-thirds of the distribution—hovering between 41 and 48 percent of the windfall," then rose to 67% for households between the 95th and 99th percentiles of income and 78% for the top 1%. The answers are hypothetical. The authors note that the question "elicits responses to a hypothetical windfall rather than an observed transfer, though there is evidence that hypothetical responses align fairly well with real-world behavior."

The report's new spending questions give a sense of scale. Over the previous 12 months, families' total nonhousing spending averaged $48,700, and the median was $33,700.

The 2025 median, $35,000, was the highest in the series. Applied to the number of families each survey represents, 131.3 million in 2022 and 135.0 million in 2025, the ownership shares work out to roughly 8.5 million families with CDs in 2022 and 14.4 million in 2025.

## Where deposit rates stand now

The survey's balances date from 2025 and early 2026. For more recent figures, the FDIC's national rates table dated October 19, 2026 showed these national averages. The FDIC says "all published rates and yields are based on information available on the last business day of the prior month end," so they reflect the end of September.

| Product | National rate, Oct. 19, 2026 table |
|---|---|
| Savings | 0.38% |
| Interest checking | 0.07% |
| Money market | 0.63% |
| 12-month CD | 1.76% |
| 60-month CD | 1.41% |

The FDIC defines the national rate as "the average of rates paid by all insured depository institutions and credit unions for which data is available, with rates weighted by each institution's share of domestic deposits." Institutions with the most deposits count the most, so the average says little about any single account.

On a balance the size of the 2025 median, $8,200, the savings average works out to about $31 of interest in a year and the 12-month CD average to about $144, before tax. The two aren't interchangeable. A CD runs for a set term, and IRS Publication 550 notes that if you withdraw funds before maturity, "you may be charged a penalty." The survey's $8,200 also covers checking and every other transaction account, not only savings. ## Savings bonds: fewer holders, smaller balances

Savings bonds have been shrinking in the survey for decades. In 1989, 23.9% of families held them. In 2025, 5.4% did, the lowest share in the series. Among holders, the median fell from $2,200 in 2022 to $1,000 in 2025, and the mean from $39,000 to $18,700. The report gives no explanation. Savings bonds made up 0.2% of families' financial assets.

By age, the 2025 medians were $2,000 or less in every group except families 75 and older, whose median was $10,500. Of that group, 5.5% held savings bonds.

Two rules from the Treasury and the IRS apply to anyone holding older bonds. First, bonds stop earning at final maturity. TreasuryDirect says an EE bond earns interest "for 30 years (or until you cash them if you do that before 30 years)," and its I bond page gives the same 30 years. IRS Publication 550 (2025) says "all Series E bonds have matured and are no longer earning interest" and "all HH bonds reached final maturity and stopped earning interest in August 2024." It says the same of Series H bonds. A bond past final maturity earns nothing more.

Second, the tax timing follows maturity. Interest on EE and I bonds is subject to federal income tax but not state or local income tax, according to TreasuryDirect, which adds that "most people put off reporting the interest until they actually get it." Publication 550 calls that method 1: "Postpone reporting the interest until the earlier of the year you cash or dispose of the bonds or the year in which they mature." Under that method, a bond that reaches its 30-year final maturity puts its deferred interest on that year's return, whether or not it has been cashed. TreasuryDirect says the Form 1099-INT comes "for the year in which you get the interest," and for a paper bond that happens "when you cash the bond." For a paper bond left uncashed past final maturity, the year the interest must be reported under method 1 and the year the form arrives can differ. Our guide to [the EE bond guarantee](/2026/09/28/the-ee-bond-guarantee-how-a-low-stated-rate-still-doubles/) covers how EE bonds earn interest before then.

## Signs of strain in the same survey

The same survey found more families having trouble keeping up with payments. Families with debt at the time of the interview were asked whether they had been behind on any loan payments in the preceding year. In 2025, 19.6% of families reported being late, up from 12.2% in 2022, and 8.2% reported being 60 days late or more. The share of families whose debt payments exceeded 40% of income rose from 6.5% to 8.6%. The share of families carrying a credit card balance was about unchanged at 45%, but the median balance rose $150 to $3,100 and the mean rose $1,300 to $8,000. The share of families who didn't apply for credit for fear of being turned down rose from 12.9% to 13.5%.

One of the FEDS Notes looked past the balance sheet to help from friends and relatives. The survey asked whether respondents "could obtain at least $3,000 from friends or relatives during an emergency," a question earlier surveys also asked, and whether others might rely on them. The note found that 79.7% of families expected they could either be relied on for or obtain monetary support in an emergency. It also identified "the 5.5 percent of families who both have low financial wealth and lack an informal F/CG network," its shorthand for informal financial and caregiving networks among friends and relatives.

## How families use their banks

A few items in the report and tables are about banking itself. The report notes that 88% of families used online banking in 2025, up from 85% in 2022, while the share that visited a local bank branch "remained steady at 66 percent."

Asked for the most important reason they chose the institution for their main checking account, 40.0% of respondents cited the location of its offices and 12.2% the lowest fees or minimum balance requirement. The share that said it "offered safety and absence of risk" was 6.4%, up from 4.5% in 2019. Among families without a checking account, the most common reasons were not writing enough checks to make one worthwhile (29.6%) and not liking to deal with banks (25.6%). High service charges were cited by 9.7%, and high minimum balance requirements by 2.5%.

## Comparing your own accounts with the survey

It is easy to misread the survey's numbers when comparing them with one household's accounts. These steps keep the comparison like for like.

1. **Add up the same accounts.** To set your cash beside the $8,200 median, total checking, savings, money market deposit accounts, money market funds, brokerage cash, prepaid card and app balances, and any savings held in an HSA or 529 account. Keep CDs and savings bonds separate, since the survey does.
2. **Compare the same unit.** The survey's "family" is the economically dominant single person or couple "and all other persons in the household who are financially interdependent with that economically dominant person or couple." A couple's accounts count once, together.
3. **Use the median in your row.** For a typical figure, the median in your age, income or family-type row is closer than the mean, which the largest balances pull up.
4. **Keep the dates in mind.** The balances are from interviews held mostly from April to December 2025, with about one-seventh from January to May 2026, and they are stated in 2025 dollars.
5. **Watch for asterisks.** In the Fed's tables an asterisk marks "Ten or fewer observations," and small groups can swing between surveys.
6. **Check insurance separately.** The survey's categories don't follow insurance rules. The [FDIC's coverage page](https://www.fdic.gov/resources/deposit-insurance/understanding-deposit-insurance) points depositors to its Electronic Deposit Insurance Estimator (EDIE) "to calculate how much of your funds are covered by deposit insurance." For credit unions, the limits are on the NCUA's page.

The [Fed's survey page](https://www.federalreserve.gov/econres/scfindex.htm) has the report, an interactive chartbook and the Excel tables, including standard errors for the report's first two tables.

## Common questions

**What is the median savings account balance in the U.S.?**

The Fed's survey doesn't publish one. It reports transaction accounts, which combine checking, savings, money market accounts and funds, brokerage cash and prepaid cards. Its savings category also includes health savings accounts and Coverdell or 529 education accounts. The median across all of those was $8,200 in 2025 among the 98.7% of families that had at least one.

**Does the $8,200 include CDs or retirement accounts?**

No. CDs are a separate category, held by 10.7% of families with a median of $35,000. Retirement accounts are another, held by 54.9% of families with a median of $106,000. Because medians can't be added, those figures can't be combined into a typical family's total.

**Why did the median fall when interest rates were higher?**

The report doesn't give a reason for the change in transaction accounts. Part of the decline is the inflation adjustment: in each survey's own dollars, the median rose from $8,000 to $8,200, but after inflation it fell from $8,800 to $8,200. For CDs, the report says the rise in ownership likely reflects higher returns in 2025 than in 2022.

**Are Venmo or PayPal balances counted?**

Yes, if the app includes an account feature that lets families receive and store money. The survey groups those with prepaid debit cards. Whether a balance is insured is a separate question, and the FDIC says its insurance doesn't cover the failure of a company that isn't an FDIC-insured bank.

**How often does the Fed run this survey?**

The press release says the current version has been undertaken every three years since 1989. The 2025 survey's results came out on Oct. 9, 2026.

The survey can show where a balance sits among families of a similar age, income or household type. It can't say what that balance should be, and it doesn't show what the money earns, which depends on the rate each account pays.

Run your own numbers instead: your balance, the APY on offer, and the period you plan to hold.
