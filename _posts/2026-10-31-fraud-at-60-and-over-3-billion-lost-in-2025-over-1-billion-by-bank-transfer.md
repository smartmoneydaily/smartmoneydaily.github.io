![Fraud at 60 and Over: $3 Billion Lost in 2025, Over $1 Billion by Bank Transfer](/assets/pin-images/2026-10-31-fraud-at-60-and-over-3-billion-lost-in-2025-over-1-billion-by-bank-transfer.png)

On Oct. 19, 2026, the Federal Trade Commission sent Congress its yearly report on fraud against older adults, *Protecting Older Consumers 2025–2026*. Drawing on reports filed with the FTC's Consumer Sentinel Network in 2025, it says people 60 and over reported losing more than $3 billion to fraud that year, up from about $2.4 billion in 2024 and about $600 million in 2020. Among the payment methods older adults named, bank transfers and payments carried the largest losses, $1,033 million, and cryptocurrency came second at $603 million. Credit and debit cards together accounted for $133 million.

Scammers posing as businesses ranked third among fraud types by dollars lost to people 60 and over, and the costliest of them pretended to be banks. The report describes how those scams work: "These scams often work by convincing people that a security issue has put their accounts at risk. People report that they were directed to transfer money, mail cash, or deposit cash into Bitcoin ATMs to keep it 'safe.'"

This article is based on the FTC's [report](https://www.ftc.gov/system/files/ftc_gov/pdf/2026-older-adults-report-10192026.pdf) and its [Oct. 19 news release](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2026/10/ftc-issues-annual-report-congress-agencys-actions-protect-older-adults). Dollar amounts by year, age, fraud type, first contact and payment method come from the report's Figures 1, 2, 5, 6 and 7; the averages per report are this article's arithmetic from those charts. Reporting steps come from the FTC's consumer page [What To Do if You Were Scammed](https://consumer.ftc.gov/articles/what-do-if-you-were-scammed) and the Justice Department's [National Elder Fraud Hotline](https://ovc.ojp.gov/program/stop-elder-fraud/providing-help-restoring-hope) page. The figures come from reports people chose to file, not from a survey. This article describes them; it isn't legal or financial advice.

## The 2025 count for people 60 and over

Sentinel took in more than 6.2 million consumer reports in 2025. Over 3 million were about fraud, and consumers of all ages reported losing about $16 billion to it. About 46% of fraud reports included the person's age. Of those, 469,597, about 33%, came from people 60 and over, and about 123,000 of them included a loss of money.

| Measure, people 60 and over | 2025 |
|---|---|
| Fraud reports (among reports that gave an age) | 469,597 |
| Reports with a monetary loss | about 123,000 |
| Total reported losses | over $3 billion |
| Median loss, ages 60 and over | about $900 |
| Median loss, ages 80 and over | $2,000, up 21% from 2024 |
| Reports with no monetary loss | about 74% |

Most older adults who reported fraud had spotted it without paying. Adjusted for population, people 60 and over filed these no-loss reports at a 34% higher rate than people 18 to 59 and reported losing money at a 14% lower rate. Leaving out no-loss reports that gave no contact method or one classified as "other," the largest shares were texts (32%) and phone calls (29%), and the phone reports were often about scammers impersonating "Medicare, banks, Amazon, and the FTC."

When older adults did lose money, they typically lost more. Figure 2 of the report puts the 2025 median loss at $640 for ages 60 to 69, $1,000 for 70 to 79 and $2,000 for 80 and over, against $500 for 50 to 59 and $305 for 20 to 29. Among reports filed directly with the FTC, about 17% of those for people 80 and over were submitted by someone else, such as an adult child, a spouse or a caretaker, and those third-party reports had higher medians: $6,500 for people 80 and over, $5,994 for 70 to 79 and $3,000 for 60 to 69.

## A few thousand reports, most of the money

Figure 1 splits each year's losses by the size of the individual loss. The total has risen every year since 2020.

| Year | Reports with a loss (rounded) | Total reported losses | From losses over $100,000 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2020 | 69,000 | $600 million | $306 million |
| 2021 | 89,000 | $1.0 billion | $584 million |
| 2022 | 92,000 | $1.6 billion | $1.0 billion |
| 2023 | 105,000 | $1.9 billion | $1.3 billion |
| 2024 | 110,000 | $2.4 billion | $1.6 billion |
| 2025 | 123,000 | $3.0 billion | $2.1 billion |

Over those five years, the number of loss reports grew 78%. Total losses grew about fivefold, and losses in six-figure reports grew nearly sevenfold. The report counts 6,769 reports of losses over $100,000 in 2025, up from 1,136 in 2020.

**Worked example: the median and the average.** A footnote in the report sorts the 2025 loss reports into three sizes, and Figure 1 gives the dollars for each.

| Size of loss | Reports | Combined losses | Average per report |
|---|---|---|---|
| Under $10,000 | 93,135 | $123 million | about $1,300 |
| $10,000 to $100,000 | 22,655 | $781 million | about $34,000 |
| Over $100,000 | 6,769 | $2.1 billion | roughly $310,000 |

Spread across about 123,000 loss reports, $3 billion averages out to about $24,000 per report. The median is about $900. The two figures are that far apart because about three in four loss reports were under $10,000, while the six-figure reports, about 6% of the total, carried about 70% of the dollars. Controlling for population, the FTC says older adults were more than twice as likely as younger adults to report a six-figure loss, and these reports "were often about investment scams, romance scams, and scammers impersonating the FTC, banks, Publishers Clearing House, and Microsoft."

## The scams that cost the most

Figure 5 ranks fraud types by the dollars older adults reported losing. The last column divides each total by the number of loss reports the FTC lists for that type.

| Fraud type | 2025 losses, 60 and over | Change from 2024 | Loss reports | Average per report |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Investment scams | $1,059 million | +42% | 10,578 | about $100,000 |
| Government imposters | $448 million | +20% | 12,183 | about $37,000 |
| Business imposters | $422 million | +12% | 24,820 | about $17,000 |
| Romance scams | $384 million | +17% | 6,832 | about $56,000 |
| Prizes, sweepstakes and lotteries | $143 million | -1% | 5,280 | about $27,000 |
| Tech support scams | $126 million | -21% | 5,334 | about $24,000 |
| Online shopping | $59 million | +22% | 31,116 | about $1,900 |

Online shopping drew more loss reports from older adults than any other type and had the smallest average per report in the chart. Investment scams had the highest average, about $100,000 per report. People who lost money to them "often described being lured to fake cryptocurrency-investment platforms after being targeted on social media."

Government imposters were second by dollars, and the largest share of those losses came from people impersonating the FTC, the FBI and the Social Security Administration. Counting all ages, reported losses to scams impersonating those agencies were $161 million for the FTC, $144 million for SSA and $28 million for the FBI. Among reports that included an age, people 60 and over accounted for 92%, 85% and 75% of those losses.

Business imposters came third, and among them, scams impersonating banks had the highest reported losses. The report also notes "an increasingly blurred line between government and business impersonation, with many scammers impersonating more than one entity in a single scam."

## How the money was paid

Figure 7 shows how older adults paid, measured by total dollars and by share of loss reports. The shares count only loss reports that named one of these methods; reports with no payment method, or with a method people wrote in, are left out.

| Payment method | 2025 losses, 60 and over | Change from 2024 | Share of loss reports |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bank transfer or payment | $1,033 million | +24% | 11% |
| Cryptocurrency | $603 million | +33% | 10% |
| Cash | $196 million | +26% | 4% |
| Wire transfer | $142 million | +34% | 3% |
| Gift card | $139 million | +12% | 12% |
| Check | $129 million | +6% | 3% |
| Payment app or service | $110 million | +38% | 15% |
| Credit cards | $94 million | +21% | 28% |
| Debit card | $39 million | +27% | 15% |
| Money order | $16 million | -10% | 1% |

Since 2020, the report says, older adults' reported losses have grown more than tenfold on scams paid by bank transfer and more than twenty-sevenfold on scams paid with cryptocurrency. "The largest share of aggregated reported losses by older adults using bank transfers or cryptocurrency was on investment scams and scams impersonating well-known businesses, particularly banks and Microsoft."

**Worked example: share of reports against dollars lost.** Credit cards were the payment method in 28% of the loss reports in the chart and carried $94 million. Bank transfers appeared in 11% and carried $1,033 million. Because both shares come from the same pool of reports, the average bank-transfer loss works out to roughly 28 times the average credit-card loss ($1,033 million ÷ 11 against $94 million ÷ 28). Rounding in the chart means the true multiple could be somewhat higher or lower. The same arithmetic puts the average cash loss at about four times the average gift-card loss. Bank transfers, cash, wires and checks together come to about $1.5 billion, about 60% of the roughly $2.5 billion across the ten methods shown. That total is below $3 billion because the chart leaves out reports that named no payment method and methods people wrote in.

Gift cards still matter by count. The report says older adults named gift cards more often than any other method on tech support scams, romance scams, family and friend impersonation, and prize, sweepstakes and lottery scams.

## Where the scams started

Figure 6 shows how the fraud first reached people 60 and over.

| First contact | 2025 losses | Loss reports (rounded) | Median loss |
|---|---|---|---|
| Social media | $750 million | 26,000 | $599 |
| Phone call | $578 million | 20,000 | $3,000 |
| Website or app | $393 million | 24,000 | $250 |
| Text | $296 million | 9,000 | $2,500 |
| E-mail | $170 million | 8,000 | $1,000 |
| Online ad or pop-up | $148 million | 11,000 | $170 |

Social media and phone calls average out to about the same loss per report, roughly $29,000, but their medians are $599 and $3,000. The report's breakdown by fraud type helps explain the gap. On social media, older adults most often lost money to online shopping scams (34% of those loss reports), while investment scams took 56% of the dollars. On phone calls, the most common loss reports were business impersonation (36%), government impersonation (26%) and prize, sweepstakes and lottery scams (15%), and government imposters took 41% of the dollars. People 80 and over reported much higher losses from phone calls than from any other contact method.

Texts grew fastest: losses rose 61% from 2024 and the median rose 60%. The largest share of older adults' text loss reports, 31%, involved texts impersonating well-known businesses, "particularly Amazon, Apple, and banks." In the report's words, "These texts often look like account security alerts about suspicious activity or unauthorized purchases."

## The "keep it safe" script

The report spells the script out in its section on people who impersonate the FTC. These scammers "tell people their money is at risk due to a security issue or identity theft, and the FTC can help them." People are often "directed to 'protect' their money by mailing cash, handing cash to couriers, buying gift cards and sharing the codes, or depositing funds into Bitcoin ATM machines." The report says losses from these scams can involve hundreds of thousands of dollars and "can completely drain their life savings."

It also describes how the scammers keep control. Older adults "describe harrowing experiences where scammers kept them on the phone for extended periods, directing them to their bank to withdraw cash, to retailers to buy gift cards, or to Bitcoin ATM machines to deposit cash. These scammers coach people on what to say to bank employees and tell people not to speak to friends or family members."

Two details in that description matter for anyone with money in a savings account. In the versions the report describes, the scammer gets the person to move the money: a cash withdrawal at the bank, a transfer, cash mailed or handed to a courier, or cash fed into a Bitcoin ATM. And the explanation given to a bank employee may be one the scammer supplied. Our earlier guide, [You Wired It Yourself: Why Regulation E Will Not Get That Money Back](/2026/09/27/you-wired-it-yourself-why-regulation-e-will-not-get-that-money-back/), looks at what federal rules cover when the account holder sent the money.

The FTC co-led a public-private campaign called Never Ever that turns these warning signs into bright-line rules. Its messaging says government agencies will never, ever tell you to "1) move your money to supposedly protect it; 2) keep a conversation a secret; or 3) pay for a government grant." The campaign materials are at [ftc.gov/imposters](https://www.ftc.gov/imposters).

## What fast reporting did in 2025

The report gives numbers on one recovery route. The FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center has a Recovery Asset Team that uses a process called the Financial Fraud Kill Chain to notify banks and try to freeze money sent to scammers. In 2025, the team started that process for 3,900 incidents and, with banks' help, froze $679 million of $1.16 billion in reported losses, a little under 60%.

The FTC feeds that process. When a report of a high-dollar loss comes in through ReportFraud.ftc.gov or the FTC's call center, meets certain criteria and the person agrees, the FTC can refer it to the FBI. In 2025 it referred 680 reports. Of those, 451 went straight to the Recovery Asset Team, which could act on 200 that fit its criteria. Those 200 reports had more than $18.7 million in reported losses, and over $10.8 million was frozen. The report doesn't list the criteria.

**A case from the FBI: a $52,000 wire.** The report includes a case the FBI's team shared. In a report to the FTC in August 2025, an older adult said they had opened a spoofed text that appeared to come from Apple Support and claimed $500 had been charged to their credit card. A caller claiming to be from Apple Support then said the person was being investigated by the FBI and told them to send a $52,000 wire, along with smaller transfers through bitcoin ATMs, "in order to verify their identity." The wire went to a U.S. bank that held a custodial account for Coinbase. The Recovery Asset Team started the kill chain with its banking partners, with further instruction to Coinbase, and more than $51,000 was frozen.

## Where to report, by payment method

The FTC's consumer page on what to do after paying a scammer names the first contact, depending on how the money was sent:

- **Zelle or a bank transfer:** the bank or credit union, immediately, with a request to reverse the payment and refund the money.
- **A wire transfer company such as Western Union or MoneyGram:** the company, immediately, with a request for a refund.
- **Cryptocurrency, including a crypto ATM:** the exchange or the ATM operator, immediately, with word that the transaction was fraudulent. The page notes that cryptocurrency payments don't have the same legal protections as credit and debit cards.
- **Cash sent through the U.S. mail:** the U.S. Postal Inspection Service, at 1-877-876-2455, immediately; for a fee, the service may be able to intercept the package.
- **Credit or debit card:** the card issuer, or for a debit card the bank or credit union, through the number on the back of the card or the online account.

Each step is a request, not a guarantee that the money comes back. Reports to agencies are a separate step. [ReportFraud.ftc.gov](https://reportfraud.ftc.gov/) is the FTC's reporting site; its reports go into Sentinel and, for qualifying high-dollar losses with the person's consent, to the FBI as described above. Since April 2026, older adults who call the FTC to report significant fraud and need more support are connected directly to the Justice Department's National Elder Fraud Hotline, and online filers with substantial or very recent losses are given its number. The hotline, 833-FRAUD-11 (833-372-8311), takes reports of fraud against anyone 60 or older. It is open Monday through Friday, 10 a.m. to 6 p.m. Eastern time, in English, Spanish and other languages, and it assigns each caller a case manager. Its page carries a warning of its own: scammers have used the hotline's name and number to ask for personal information or money.

A report doesn't have to come from the person who lost the money. Among 2025 reports filed directly with the FTC, about 17% of those for people 80 and over were submitted by someone on the person's behalf.

## What the numbers leave out

The FTC is explicit about what Sentinel can show. Its data "is self-reported and not a survey." The figures for people 60 and over come only from reports that included an age, about 46% of fraud reports, and they exclude complaints from the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center. Because of a lapse in funding, the FTC took no consumer reports from Oct. 1 to Nov. 13, 2025, so the 2025 totals have a gap of about six weeks.

Most fraud is never reported. A study the FTC cites found that just 4.8% of victims of mass-market consumer fraud complained to a Better Business Bureau or a government entity. Using different assumptions about underreporting, the FTC estimates the overall cost of fraud to older adults in 2025 at between $13.3 billion and $119.5 billion, and it says those figures "reflect a considerable degree of uncertainty."

## Common questions

**Does the $3 billion count all fraud against people 60 and over in 2025?**

No. It counts losses in Sentinel reports that included an age, leaves out FBI Internet Crime Complaint Center data and misses about six weeks in October and November 2025. The FTC's own estimate of the full cost to older adults ranges from $13.3 billion to $119.5 billion.

**Which payment method had the largest losses?**

Bank transfers and payments, at $1,033 million for people 60 and over, followed by cryptocurrency at $603 million. Credit cards were named most often, in 28% of the loss reports that listed one of the chart's payment methods, but carried $94 million.

**Would a government agency ever tell someone to move savings to protect them?**

The FTC's Never Ever messaging says government agencies will never, ever tell you to move your money to supposedly protect it, keep a conversation a secret or pay for a government grant.

**Can someone else report a scam that hit a parent?**

Yes. Some Sentinel reports are filed on behalf of consumers by adult children, spouses, caretakers and others. Among 2025 reports filed directly with the FTC, about 17% of those for people 80 and over came from another person.

**How quickly does the FTC say to report a bank transfer to a scammer?**

Immediately, to the bank or credit union, according to its consumer page. The FBI's kill chain works by notifying banks in an attempt to freeze the money; in 2025 it froze $679 million of the $1.16 billion in reported losses it handled.

Of the losses older adults tied to a payment method in 2025, about four in five went by bank transfer, cryptocurrency, cash or wire. The FBI's 2025 figures show that some of the money sent to scammers could still be frozen through banks after it was reported.
