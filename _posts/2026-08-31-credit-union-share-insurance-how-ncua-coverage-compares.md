Rate comparison sites mix banks and credit unions in the same list, and the credit union is often at the top. The account is not [FDIC](https://www.fdic.gov/resources/deposit-insurance) insured, which prompts a reasonable question about whether the money is as safe.

The short answer is that federally insured credit unions carry equivalent protection through a different agency, at the same dollar limit. The longer answer involves some vocabulary differences and a few rules that do not line up exactly.

## Two systems, one limit

Bank deposits are insured by the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation through the Deposit Insurance Fund. Credit union accounts are insured by the [National Credit Union Administration](https://ncua.gov/consumers/share-insurance-coverage) through the National Credit Union Share Insurance Fund.

Both are backed by the full faith and credit of the United States. Both insure to $250,000 per owner, per institution, per ownership category. Both have operated without a depositor losing insured funds.

The vocabulary differs because of how credit unions are structured. You are a member rather than a customer, and you hold shares rather than deposits. A share draft account is a checking account. A share certificate is a CD. A regular share account is savings. The names describe ownership in a cooperative; the function is what you would expect.

## What that means for coverage math

The ownership category structure is close to the bank version. Single accounts, joint accounts, revocable trust accounts, and certain retirement accounts each carry their own limit at the same institution.

A joint account with two members is insured to $500,000, $250,000 per owner, the same as at a bank. Adding a payable-on-death beneficiary to a single account extends coverage per beneficiary, again mirroring the bank treatment.

The practical result is that a household can hold well over $250,000 at one credit union while staying fully insured, using the same techniques that work at a bank. The coverage estimator on the NCUA site does this arithmetic the way the FDIC's does.

## Where the two diverge

A few differences are worth knowing.

**Membership is a gate.** Credit unions serve a defined field of membership, based on employer, geography, association, or family relationship. Many have broad eligibility through a small donation to a partner organization, but the account cannot be opened without qualifying. Banks have no equivalent requirement.

**Not every credit union is federally insured.** A small number carry private share insurance instead, most often through a state-chartered private insurer. Private coverage is not backed by the federal government. Federally insured institutions display the NCUA insurance sign and can be verified through the NCUA's research tool, which is the check worth running before funding an account at an unfamiliar name.

**Resolution can look different.** When a credit union fails, the NCUA may arrange a merger into a healthy credit union, in which case accounts continue with minimal disruption, or it may liquidate and pay out insured shares. Payouts are generally prompt. The mechanics differ from a bank failure mainly in that the acquiring institution is another credit union, which occasionally means a change in the products offered.

**Dividends, not interest.** What a credit union pays on shares is technically a dividend declared from earnings rather than interest owed on a debt. In practice the rate is disclosed as an APY and it behaves like interest, including its treatment as ordinary income on a 1099-INT.

## Why the rates are often better

Credit unions are member-owned and not-for-profit. Earnings that would go to shareholders at a bank return to members as better rates, lower fees, or both. That structural difference is why credit unions cluster near the top of savings and CD rate tables and near the bottom of loan rate tables.

It is not a free lunch. Smaller institutions often have thinner technology, fewer branches, and slower transfer speeds, and a credit union's field of membership can limit where you can bank if you move. Those are service tradeoffs rather than safety tradeoffs.

## The checks worth running

Before moving a meaningful balance to a credit union you have not used:

1. Confirm federal insurance through the NCUA's credit union locator rather than the institution's own marketing.
2. Confirm your eligibility for membership before you plan around the rate.
3. Run the coverage arithmetic if the balance exceeds $250,000, using ownership categories the same way you would at a bank.
4. Check transfer limits and timing, which vary more between credit unions than between large banks.

Safety is not the reason to hesitate. A federally insured credit union and an FDIC insured bank are protected on the same terms by different agencies.

## How joining actually works

Field of membership sounds restrictive and usually is not, in practice.

Common paths include employment with a listed employer, residence or work in a defined geography, membership in an association the credit union serves, or a family relationship with an existing member. Many credit unions maintain an association partnership specifically to open eligibility, where a small one-time donation to a nonprofit qualifies you.

The mechanics: you open a share account with a minimum deposit, often small, and that deposit is your ownership stake. It stays there as long as you are a member. Once you have joined, eligibility is permanent at most institutions, even if the qualifying condition ends. Leaving the employer that qualified you does not usually end the membership.

Family eligibility is broader than people assume, frequently extending to spouses, parents, siblings, children, grandparents, grandchildren, and household members. One member in a family can effectively open the door for the rest.

## Rates, fees, and the honest comparison

Credit unions consistently rank well on deposit rates and loan rates. The picture is more mixed on service infrastructure.

Shared branching is the notable offset. Many credit unions participate in a cooperative network that lets members transact at other participating credit unions' branches, which turns a single-branch institution into a network with thousands of locations. ATM surcharge-free networks work similarly.

Where smaller institutions still lag is in transfer speed, mobile app maturity, and the breadth of account types. A credit union may not offer the same range of business accounts, trust services, or investment platforms as a large bank. For a household holding savings and a CD, none of that matters. For someone consolidating everything into one relationship, it can.

## If a credit union merges

Mergers are far more common than failures, and they usually improve the surviving institution's scale.

Accounts convert to the acquirer's product set on a stated date, share certificates generally hold their rate to maturity the way bank CDs do, and membership carries over. The six-month separate insurance window that applies to bank mergers has an equivalent in NCUA rules, which matters if you were a member of both institutions.

Loans transfer with their terms intact. What changes most often is the rate on variable-rate savings, the fee schedule, and the online banking platform.
