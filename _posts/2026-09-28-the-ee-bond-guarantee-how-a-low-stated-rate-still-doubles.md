![The EE Bond Guarantee: How a Low Stated Rate Still Doubles](/assets/pin-images/2026-09-28-the-ee-bond-guarantee-how-a-low-stated-rate-still-doubles.png)

The [TreasuryDirect page for Series EE savings bonds](https://www.treasurydirect.gov/savings-bonds/ee-bonds/) shows one number: 2.40%, "For EE bonds issued May 1, 2026 to October 31, 2026." That holds its own against the [FDIC](https://www.fdic.gov/national-rates-and-rate-caps) national average of 1.71% for a 12-month CD as of August 17, 2026. Next to what a competitive online bank pays on a one-year certificate, it reads as a rate nobody would lock money away for two decades to get.

The stated rate is not the offer. Directly above that rate box on the same Treasury page sits the sentence carrying the actual terms: "For EE bonds you buy now, we guarantee that the bond will double in value in 20 years, even if we have to add money at 20 years to make that happen." The rule behind it is in the offering itself, at [31 CFR 351.35(f)(2)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-31/part-351): "At original maturity, the redemption value of a book-entry bond shall not be less than double the purchase price of the bond." Original maturity, under [31 CFR 351.34(a)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-31/part-351), is "20 years after the issue date."

Doubling over twenty years is a compound rate whether or not anyone prints it. Two to the power of one-twentieth is 1.035265, so the guarantee is worth about 3.53% a year compounded annually. At the stated 2.40%, compounded semiannually as the regulation requires, $10,000 grows to $16,114.64 in twenty years. The guarantee has to supply the other $3,885.36, and it supplies it in a single entry on a single day.

Three things follow. The return is not a curve but a step: a holder earns 2.40% at every point from month twelve through month 239, then 3.53% at month 240, with nothing in between. Leaving early costs little in dollars and a lot in opportunity, the reverse of how a certificate of deposit behaves. And a CD ladder run over the same twenty years is not a contest between 3.53% and whatever a five-year CD pays today, because one side is fixed for the whole period, the other resets four times, and the tax code treats the two in opposite ways.

## Two rates on the same bond

The stated rate is set twice a year and then frozen. [31 CFR 351.35(c)(1)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-31/part-351) puts the schedule in writing: "The Secretary or the Secretary's designee will furnish a fixed rate of interest in announcements published each May 1 and November 1." Paragraph (b)(1) fixes what that announcement buys: a rate "established for the life of the bond, including the extended maturity period, unless, prior to the beginning of such maturity period, the Secretary either announces a different fixed rate applicable to the extended maturity period, or we expressly amend the terms and conditions applicable to the extended maturity period."

Accrual is monthly, compounding semiannual. Paragraph (d): "Interest accrues on the first day of each month; that is, we add the interest earned on a bond during any given month to its value at the beginning of the following month. The accrued interest compounds semiannually." Semiannual compounding at a 2.40% nominal rate produces an annual percentage yield of 2.414%, which is the rate at which the bond accrues in every month short of the twentieth anniversary, before the early-redemption penalty set out below.

What the guarantee is worth, if the bond survives to month 240, comes out of the two numbers in the regulation, double and twenty years, and does not move when the stated rate moves:

| Fixed rate at issue | $10,000 grown at that rate for 20 years | What the guarantee must add | Share of the final $20,000 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.10% | $10,201.96 | $9,798.04 | 48.99% |
| 2.10% | $15,186.33 | $4,813.67 | 24.07% |
| 2.40% | $16,114.64 | $3,885.36 | 19.43% |
| 2.70% | $17,098.19 | $2,901.81 | 14.51% |

The first row is not hypothetical. The rate table on Treasury's page for [EE bonds issued May 2005 and later](https://www.treasurydirect.gov/savings-bonds/ee-bonds/may-2005-and-later/) shows 0.10% for every announcement from November 2015 through May 2022, and nothing above 0.60% in any announcement going back to May 2012. Bonds bought in that stretch carry a stated rate that rounds to nothing and a guarantee that will eventually deliver close to half their redemption value in one payment. The bond issued in May 2022 reaches its twentieth anniversary in May 2042; nothing before then reflects the promise.

A bond needs a shade under 3.50% nominal, compounded semiannually, to reach twice its purchase price without help. Three announcements since May 2005 have cleared that line: 3.50% in May 2005, 3.70% in May 2006, and 3.60% in November 2006. Every announcement from May 2007 onward has come in below it, so for nineteen years the doubling clause has been the operative term rather than a backstop that occasionally matters.

## Month 240, and the month before it

No bank product behaves the way this step function does. Put $10,000 into an EE bond issued this month and the redemption value moves like this:

| Held for | Redemption value at 2.40% | Annualized |
|---|---|---|
| 1 year | $10,241.44 | 2.414% |
| 5 years | $11,266.92 | 2.414% |
| 10 years | $12,694.34 | 2.414% |
| 15 years | $14,302.61 | 2.414% |
| 19 years | $15,734.74 | 2.414% |
| 19 years, 11 months | $16,082.63 | 2.414% |
| 20 years | $20,000.00 | 3.5265% |

Look at the second-to-last row. Redeem one month early and the payment is $16,082.63; wait, and it is $20,000. That one month carries $3,917.37 on a $10,000 purchase — close to two-thirds of the $6,082.63 the bond accumulated across the preceding 239 months put together.

The adjustment is described on Treasury's own page as conditional and retrospective: "If you keep the bond for 20 years, we will make a one-time adjustment, if necessary, to fulfill this guarantee." The same page sends holders to their TreasuryDirect account or the Savings Bond Calculator for a bond's current value, and the only caveat it attaches to those figures is the one for bonds under five years old. Nothing in the wording makes the adjustment part of what accrues month by month.

Timing inside a month is a smaller matter but runs the same way. Because interest accrues on the first day of each month under 351.35(d), the redemption value is flat from the second of the month to the last day of it. Redeeming on the 3rd and redeeming on the 28th produce the same figure; waiting until the 1st adds a month. Bonds under five years old carry a further display quirk, flagged on the Treasury page: "For bonds less than 5 years old, values shown in TreasuryDirect and the Calculator don't include the last three months of interest."

Redemption at twenty years is also a decision someone has to make. Electronic bonds are redeemed automatically at final maturity, which [31 CFR 351.34(b)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-31/part-351) places at "30 years after the issue date," not at twenty. A bond left alone past the twentieth anniversary keeps running under whatever terms apply to its last decade.

## Twelve months locked, three months forfeited

The exit rules are short. [31 CFR 351.6(b)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-31/part-351): "Bonds with issue dates on or after February 1, 2003. You may redeem your Series EE savings bond at any time beginning twelve months after its issue date." There is no fee schedule that shortens this and no partial exit, because the bond is not redeemable at all inside the first year.

After twelve months, paragraph (e) of 351.35 governs: "If you redeem a bond with an issue date of May 1, 2005, or thereafter, less than five years following the issue date, we reduce the overall earning period from the issue date by three months. However, the redemption value of a bond subject to the 3-month interest penalty shall not be reduced below the issue price. This penalty does not apply to bonds redeemed 5 years or more after the issue date."

Three months of interest at 2.40% is not much money:

| Redeemed at | Value without penalty | Value after penalty | Forfeited |
|---|---|---|---|
| 12 months | $10,241.44 | $10,180.54 | $60.90 |
| 24 months | $10,488.71 | $10,426.34 | $62.37 |
| 36 months | $10,741.95 | $10,678.07 | $63.88 |
| 59 months | $11,244.54 | $11,177.67 | $66.87 |

Sixty-one dollars on $10,000 buys the whole first-year exit. The second sentence of paragraph (e) marks the structural difference from a bank certificate: the penalty cannot push the redemption value below what was paid. A CD penalty has no such floor. Under [12 CFR 1030.4(b)(6)(ii)](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1030/4/) a bank states "how it is calculated," and when that calculation is a year of interest on a CD three months old, the shortfall comes out of the deposit, which is worked through in [CD Early Withdrawal Penalties: The Disclosure and the Deduction](/2026/09/22/cd-early-withdrawal-penalties-the-disclosure-and-the-deduction/).

One more asymmetry sits in [31 CFR 351.7](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-31/part-351), a single sentence: "The Secretary of the Treasury may not call Series EE bonds for redemption prior to final maturity." The twenty-year clock cannot be cut short from the other side. A long CD carrying a call provision can be, and the call arrives precisely when the rate has become valuable, the mechanism described in [Callable CDs: The Risk That Only Appears When Rates Fall](/2026/08/29/callable-cds-the-risk-that-only-appears-when-rates-fall/).

Series I bonds run on the same twelve-month lock and the same three-month forfeiture, with a rate that moves instead of a doubling clause; the comparison is in [I Bonds vs a High-Yield Savings Account: The 12-Month Lock](/2026/09/24/i-bonds-vs-a-high-yield-savings-account-the-12-month-lock/).

## A CD ladder run over the same twenty years

Twenty years in five-year rungs means four maturities and four reinvestments at rates nobody knows yet. That is the comparison, and pretending the first rung's rate holds for the whole period is where the arithmetic usually goes wrong.

Today's averages give a sense of the floor. [FDIC](https://www.fdic.gov/national-rates-and-rate-caps) national rates as of August 17, 2026 were 1.36% at 60 months, 1.34% at 36 months and 1.71% at 12 months, with savings accounts at 0.38%. Compounded annually for twenty years, 1.36% turns $10,000 into $13,101.83 and never comes near doubling. Nobody builds a ladder at the national average, but it shows what the curve looks like when the long end is not being paid for; a ladder locking twenty years of five-year rungs at those levels would lose to the guarantee. The mechanics of building one are in [Building a CD Ladder When 12-Month CDs Pay the Most](/2026/08/19/building-a-cd-ladder-when-12-month-cds-pay-the-most/).

Take a more generous assumption instead: a ladder that averages 4% across all twenty years, reinvestment risk waved away. Pre-tax, $10,000 at 4% compounds to $21,911.23, and passes $20,000 after about 17.7 years. On rate alone the ladder wins, and it wins early.

Tax moves the line, because the two instruments are taxed on opposite schedules. CD interest is taxable in the year it is credited even when it cannot be withdrawn, which is the subject of [Why You Owe Tax on a 5-Year CD You Cannot Touch Yet](/2026/09/25/why-you-owe-tax-on-a-5-year-cd-you-cannot-touch-yet/). EE bond interest can be deferred to redemption, and is exempt from state and local income tax in every year. Paying the CD's tax out of the CD's own interest lowers its compounding rate for two decades; the EE pays once, at the end, on the full gain:

| Marginal rate assumed | Ladder at 4%, taxed each year | EE bond, $20,000 taxed once | CD rate needed to match |
|---|---|---|---|
| 12% federal, no state tax | $19,974.93 | $18,800.00 | 3.64% |
| 22% federal, no state tax | $18,486.64 | $17,800.00 | 3.75% |
| 22% federal + 5% state | $17,782.61 | $17,800.00 | 4.01% |
| 24% federal + 5% state | $17,508.19 | $17,600.00 | 4.04% |

Read the last column. It gives the average gross rate a twenty-year ladder has to hit, every rung, to end up where the guarantee ends up. In a state with no income tax and a low federal bracket the hurdle is 3.64%. Add a state income tax and a middle bracket and it crosses 4%, which is above the 3.53% the guarantee implies, because deferral and the state exemption are each worth something on their own.

Two cautions on that table. The $10,000 of EE interest arrives in one tax year and can push a return into a higher bracket than the one assumed, a risk the ladder spreads out by construction. And the whole comparison assumes the ladder actually averages 4%; four resets over twenty years is four chances for it not to.

## The tax treatment, in both directions

Treasury's summary is three lines: "Federal income tax: Yes. State and local income tax: No. Federal estate, gift, and excise taxes; state estate or inheritance taxes: Yes." The state exemption is the same feature that distinguishes Treasury bills from certificates, and it scales with the state rate rather than with the size of the deposit, as set out in [Treasury Bills vs CDs: The Tax Break That Changes the Comparison](/2026/09/23/treasury-bills-vs-cds-the-tax-break-that-changes-the-comparison/). At a 5% state rate, 3.5265% free of state tax is equivalent to about 3.71% that is not.

Federal timing is a choice, made once and applied to everything. Treasury states it as two options: "put off (defer) reporting the interest until you file a federal income tax return for the year in which you actually get the interest, or report the interest each year even though you don't actually get the interest then." Switching from deferral to annual reporting requires no permission but is not selective: "you must do this for all the savings bonds for the Social Security Number whose tax return this is," including all interest earned in earlier years. Switching the other way requires Form 3115 or the procedure in IRS Publication 550.

Treasury flags one situation for annual reporting: a bond in a child's name, where "The child may be paying taxes at a lower rate than will be true years later when the bond matures." Who owes the tax is settled separately, by the registration rather than by who paid. Treasury's own table is blunt about it: if "You buy the bond but someone else is named as the only owner (for example, your child)," the tax falls on "The person who is named as the owner (not you)." What a child's own rate turns out to be has its own rules, covered in [Savings Accounts for Kids: Custodial Rules and the Kiddie Tax](/2026/09/17/savings-accounts-for-kids-custodial-rules-and-the-kiddie-tax/).

The education exclusion is a separate track with its own form. [31 CFR 351.81](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-31/part-351) points to it, and the mechanics are on [Form 8815](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/f8815.pdf), "Exclusion of Interest From Series EE and I U.S. Savings Bonds Issued After 1989 (For Filers With Qualified Higher Education Expenses)." The 2025 form asks for "the total proceeds (principal and interest) from all series EE and I U.S. savings bonds issued after 1989 that you cashed during 2025" on line 5, then rations the exclusion by income. The note above line 10 is where most filers stop: "If line 9 is $114,500 or more if single, head of household, or qualifying surviving spouse; or $179,250 or more if married filing jointly, stop. You cannot take the exclusion." The phase-out begins at $99,500 and $149,250 respectively, across a $15,000 band for single filers and a $30,000 band for joint filers. The surviving amount goes on Schedule B (Form 1040), line 3.

The timing is where this turns awkward. Line 9 of Form 8815 asks for "your modified adjusted gross income" in the year the bonds were cashed, not in the year they were bought, and a bond bought to reach its doubling date is cashed two decades after anyone could have known what that income would be. How taxed interest compounds against inflation over a period that long is a separate calculation, laid out in [The Tax Bite on Savings Interest: What Your Real Yield Is](/2026/08/26/the-tax-bite-on-savings-interest-what-your-real-yield-is/).

## The ceiling the strategy runs into

The guarantee is attractive per dollar and capped in dollars. [31 CFR 363.52(a)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-31/part-363): "The principal amount of book-entry savings bonds that you may acquire in any calendar year is limited to $10,000 for Series EE savings bonds and $10,000 for Series I savings bonds." Treasury's own page repeats it in plainer terms: "The limit applies to the Social Security Number of the first person named on the bond." One carve-out sits on the limits page: "If you have an individual account and an entity account in TreasuryDirect that use the same Social Security Number, you can purchase up to the limits in each of the 2 accounts."

Gifts do not route around it. Paragraph (b) of the same section counts a gift "for the account of the recipient for the year in which the bonds are delivered to the recipient," and Treasury adds that a bond sitting undelivered is "in a special 'gift box'" belonging to nobody's limit yet. Co-ownership does not multiply it either: "only the bonds for which you are the first named owner count toward your limit."

The floors and increments are small. [31 CFR 363.53](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-31/part-363) sets a $25 minimum purchase "with additional one-cent increments above that amount." [31 CFR 363.57](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-31/part-363) applies the same $25 minimum to redemptions and adds the important clause: "Redemptions will be comprised of principal and proportionate interest." Partial redemption of an electronic bond is allowed, subject to [31 CFR 363.54](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-31/part-363), which requires "a redemption value of at least $25" to remain. Treasury's plain-language version adds what the proportionality means in practice: "If you cash only part of what a bond is worth, you only get the interest on the part you cash." A paper bond has no such option and "must be cashed for its entire value."

One restriction has no bank equivalent. [31 CFR 363.58](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-31/part-363): "Bonds may not be pledged or used as collateral for the performance of an obligation." A certificate of deposit can secure a loan, which is often the reason a saver tolerates a penalty clause. An EE bond cannot, so the twenty-year commitment is not softened by borrowing against it.

## What the twentieth anniversary opens

The guarantee resolves at twenty years and a second set of terms begins. Treasury states the reservation: "For bonds bought since May 2005, we may change the interest rate or the way the EE bond earns interest for the last 10 years of the bond's 30-year life. If we do that, we must do it before the bond is 20 years old."

The same authority is written into 351.35(b)(1), which fixes the rate for the life of the bond "unless, prior to the beginning of such maturity period, the Secretary either announces a different fixed rate applicable to the extended maturity period, or we expressly amend the terms and conditions applicable to the extended maturity period." The notice comes before the extension, not during it, so the terms for the final decade are knowable by the anniversary.

Treasury spells out the consequence: "If you do not want that new interest rate or the new way the bond will earn interest in its last 10 years, you can cash it when it reaches 20 years and has at least doubled what you paid for it." The decision is a live one, and the guarantee has already paid by the time it arrives. Whatever rate applies to years twenty-one through thirty applies to a balance twice the size of the original purchase.

Interest stops at the end. Under 351.34(b) bonds "cease to earn interest at final maturity," thirty years from issue. A bond held past that date earns nothing at all while remaining fully redeemable, which is the quiet cost of forgetting one exists. Treasury used to run a lookup for exactly that situation; its [Treasury Hunt page](https://www.treasurydirect.gov/savings-bonds/treasury-hunt/) now reads "As of September 30, 2025, the Treasury Hunt® tool is no longer available" and points anyone who suspects they hold an unredeemed lost, stolen, or destroyed bond to the savings bond forms page to submit a claim. That is a different problem from a bank balance going dormant and escheating to a state, described in [How a Savings Account Goes Dormant and Ends Up With the State](/2026/09/04/how-a-savings-account-goes-dormant-and-ends-up-with-the-state/).

## What the guarantee is measured against

A twenty-year commitment invites a twenty-year comparison, and Treasury publishes one on itself. The [daily par yield curve](https://home.treasury.gov/resource-center/data-chart-center/interest-rates/TextView?type=daily_treasury_yield_curve) put the 20-year Treasury at 5.38% on September 18, 2026, with the 5-year at 4.86%. Both are exempt from state and local income tax on the same footing as a savings bond. Against those numbers the EE bond's implied 3.53% is not a high long rate, and the guarantee is not a claim to be one.

The guarantee is a floor on a nominal dollar figure, known in advance, with no price risk attached. A marketable twenty-year Treasury can be sold before maturity at whatever the market pays that day, which may be well under face value. An EE bond has one price, published in the account, and that price never falls. The trade is liquidity and market upside for certainty about a single number twenty years out.

That certainty is nominal. Doubling over twenty years preserves purchasing power only if prices rise less than about 3.5% a year over the same period, and the EE structure contains nothing that responds to inflation. A Series I bond runs the other way: its rate adjusts, and no doubling guarantee exists.

What settles whether the structure fits is not a rate question. It is the issue month, since the anniversary is a date and not a range; whether the money can sit untouched until that date, because an exit before it leaves the stated rate as the entire return and forfeits the clause; and the $10,000 cap per Social Security Number per year, which decides whether the guarantee covers a meaningful share of a portfolio or a corner of one.

## Common questions

**Is the doubling a rate or a lump sum?** Mechanically it is a lump sum. The bond earns its stated rate month by month, and at original maturity Treasury makes what its own page calls "a one-time adjustment, if necessary" so that the redemption value is at least twice the purchase price. Expressed as a compound rate over the full twenty years it comes to about 3.53%, but no part of it accrues before the anniversary.

**What happens if the bond is redeemed at nineteen years?** It pays the stated rate and nothing else. On a $10,000 bond issued at 2.40%, nineteen years produces $15,734.74; the twentieth year produces $20,000. The guarantee has no partial version and does not scale with time held.

**Can an EE bond be worth less than what was paid for it?** No. [31 CFR 351.35(e)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-31/part-351) states that "the redemption value of a bond subject to the 3-month interest penalty shall not be reduced below the issue price," and the penalty is the only deduction the bond carries. A CD penalty has no equivalent floor.

**Does the $10,000 limit apply to the purchase price or the redemption value?** To the purchase. 31 CFR 363.52(a) caps "the principal amount of book-entry savings bonds that you may acquire in any calendar year." What the bonds grow to is not counted against any later year's limit.

**Can the interest be reported annually instead of at redemption?** Yes, without IRS permission, but the election covers every savings bond held under that Social Security Number and requires reporting all prior years' accrued interest in the year of the change. Reversing it takes Form 3115 or the procedure in Publication 550. Which method suits a particular return is a question for a tax professional.

An EE bond bought today turns on a calendar date, not a rate. The 2.40% on the purchase screen is what a holder earns for being early; the number that matters is printed in a regulation, applies once, and applies only to whoever still holds the bond on the twentieth anniversary of its issue month.
