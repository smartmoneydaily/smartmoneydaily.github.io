Breaking a CD early is usually described as a mistake. It is actually a calculation, and it has a definite answer: compare what the penalty costs against what the extra interest earns over the time the money would otherwise stay locked. If the second number is larger, breaking is correct. If not, it is not.

Here is the calculation, worked out with real figures, plus the situations where the answer comes out differently than people expect.

## The three inputs

**The penalty.** Stated in your CD's disclosure as a number of days or months of interest. Compute it in dollars:

Daily interest = Principal × Current APY ÷ 365
Penalty = Daily interest × Penalty days

**The interest you would give up and gain.** Over the remaining term, compare the interest at the current rate against the interest at the new rate on the same principal.

**The remaining term.** In days, from today to the maturity date. This is the number that decides everything — the shorter it is, the less room there is for a better rate to make up the penalty.

## The break-even, worked out

Say you hold a $30,000 CD at 2% APY with 14 months remaining and a 180-day early-withdrawal penalty. A competitive 14-month CD is available at 4%.

**Penalty:**
Daily interest = $30,000 × 0.02 ÷ 365 = **$1.6438 per day**
Penalty = $1.6438 × 180 = **$295.89**

**Interest over the remaining 14 months at the current rate:**
$30,000 × 0.02 × (14 ÷ 12) = **$700.00**

**Interest over 14 months at the new rate:**
$30,000 × 0.04 × (14 ÷ 12) = **$1,400.00**

**Gain from switching:** $1,400.00 − $700.00 = **$700.00**
**Net after penalty:** $700.00 − $295.89 = **+$404.11**

Breaking is worth about $404 here. The rate gap is wide (2 points) and the remaining term is long enough for that gap to compound into more than the penalty.

## The same math with a short remaining term

Change one input. Same CD, same rates, but only 4 months remain.

**Penalty:** unchanged at **$295.89** — this is the key asymmetry. The penalty is fixed by contract; it does not shrink because less time is left.

**Interest over 4 months at 2%:** $30,000 × 0.02 × (4 ÷ 12) = **$200.00**
**Interest over 4 months at 4%:** $30,000 × 0.04 × (4 ÷ 12) = **$400.00**
**Gain:** **$200.00**
**Net after penalty:** $200.00 − $295.89 = **−$95.89**

Now breaking costs about $96. Nothing changed except the time remaining, and the answer reversed.

This is the general rule the arithmetic produces: **the closer you are to maturity, the worse breaking becomes.** Waiting four months and then moving at maturity captures the entire rate improvement with no penalty at all.

## The break-even rate gap

You can skip the full calculation with a shortcut. Breaking is worth it when:

Rate gap (in percentage points) > Penalty days ÷ Remaining days × Current APY

For the 14-month case: 180 ÷ 426 × 2% is roughly nine-tenths of a point. The actual gap was 2 points, comfortably above it — break.

For the 4-month case: 180 ÷ 122 × 2% is close to 3 points. The actual gap of 2 points falls short — hold.

The formula ignores compounding and the fact that the penalty comes out up front, so treat it as a screen rather than a final answer. When the result is close, run the full arithmetic.

## Where current rates sit

Whether a wide enough gap exists depends on the market. [FDIC](https://www.fdic.gov/national-rates-and-rate-caps) national average CD rates as of August 17, 2026:

| Term | National average APY |
|---|---|
| 3 months | 1.14% |
| 6 months | 1.41% |
| 12 months | 1.71% |
| 24 months | 1.57% |
| 36 months | 1.34% |
| 60 months | 1.36% |

Two observations relevant to a break-even decision.

The curve peaks at 12 months and declines after it. If you are holding a long CD and considering breaking into another long one, the averages suggest the replacement will not pay more for the extra commitment.

These are averages across all insured institutions, most of which pay well below competitive rates. The gap that makes breaking worthwhile usually comes from moving to a competitive institution rather than from a change in the rate environment.

## Three cases where the arithmetic is not the whole answer

**You need the money.** Then the question is not whether breaking beats holding — it is whether the penalty beats the alternatives. Compare it against the cost of borrowing. A $296 penalty is cheaper than several months of interest on a credit card balance, and this is the situation the penalty exists for.

**The penalty can reach principal.** Some disclosures allow the penalty to be taken out of principal when the interest earned so far is smaller. Break early enough in a term and you withdraw less than you deposited. Check for language about the penalty reducing principal before assuming the worst case is losing interest.

**You are inside a grace period.** After maturity, most CDs give 7 to 10 days to withdraw or change terms with no penalty at all. If you are near maturity, there is nothing to calculate — wait for the window and move for free. This is why the maturity date belongs in your calendar the day you open the account.

## The tax detail

Early-withdrawal penalties are generally deductible on a federal return as an adjustment to income, reported on the Form 1099-INT the bank issues. That reduces the effective cost of a penalty somewhat, which can flip a decision that lands close to break-even. Tax treatment depends on your own situation, so confirm it with a tax professional rather than adjusting the arithmetic on assumption.

## The checklist

1. Find the penalty terms in your CD disclosure — days of interest, and whether principal can be reduced
2. Compute the penalty in dollars: principal × APY ÷ 365 × penalty days
3. Compute interest over the remaining term at both the current and the replacement rate
4. Subtract the penalty from the gain
5. If you are within a month or two of maturity, stop — wait for the grace period instead
6. Confirm the replacement institution is insured and that your balance there stays within the coverage limit

The whole exercise takes ten minutes and produces a number rather than an opinion, which is the reason to do it before acting on either instinct.

## A second scenario: partial withdrawal

Some CDs permit withdrawing part of the balance, with the penalty applied only to the amount withdrawn. Where that is allowed, the arithmetic scales down cleanly.

On the same $30,000 CD at 2% with a 180-day penalty, withdrawing $10,000:

Daily interest on the withdrawn portion = $10,000 × 0.02 ÷ 365 = **$0.5479**
Penalty = $0.5479 × 180 = **$98.63**

The remaining $20,000 continues at 2% under the original terms. This is worth knowing when you need some of the money rather than all of it — the default assumption that a CD is all-or-nothing costs unnecessary penalty on the portion you could have left alone.

Partial withdrawal is not universally offered, and where it is, some institutions require the remaining balance to stay above a minimum. Both details are in the disclosure.

Rates cited are FDIC national averages as of August 17, 2026 and change over time. Penalty structures and grace periods vary by institution — your account disclosure governs.
