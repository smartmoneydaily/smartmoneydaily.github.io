Take a hypothetical married couple with $240,000 of wages and $30,000 of interest from high-yield savings and CDs. Under the 2026 rules, their return would show $760 of net investment income tax, because their income is above a threshold that has stayed at the same dollar amount since 2013.

Interest from savings accounts, CDs, money market accounts and Treasury bills counts as net investment income for the federal net investment income tax (NIIT). The tax is 3.8% of whichever is smaller: your net investment income, or the amount by which your modified adjusted gross income (MAGI) is above $250,000 for married couples filing jointly, $200,000 for single filers and heads of household, or $125,000 for married filing separately. Those thresholds are not indexed for inflation. Below the line, savings interest owes no NIIT at all. A little above it, only the excess is taxed. Once the excess is at least as large as your net investment income, all of that income, interest included, pays 3.8% on top of regular income tax.

The principal sources are [Topic No. 559, Net investment income tax](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc559), the IRS [Questions and answers on the net investment income tax](https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/questions-and-answers-on-the-net-investment-income-tax), the [Instructions for Form 8960](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i8960) and, for the 2026 tax brackets used in the examples, [Revenue Procedure 2025-32](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-drop/rp-25-32.pdf), with [Publication 550](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p550) and [Topic 424](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc424) for two narrower points. This is general information about how the tax works, not tax advice for any household. Your own numbers depend on your full return.

## What the 3.8% tax is

The NIIT comes from section 1411 of the Internal Revenue Code. The IRS Q&A describes it this way: "The NIIT applies at a rate of 3.8% to certain net investment income of individuals, estates and trusts that have income above the statutory threshold amounts." It "went into effect on Jan. 1, 2013."

For individuals, Topic 559 gives the formula: "the NIIT is 3.8 percent on the lesser of: the net investment income, or the excess of modified adjusted gross income (MAGI) over the following threshold amounts":

| Filing status | MAGI threshold |
|---|---|
| Married filing jointly | $250,000 |
| Qualifying surviving spouse | $250,000 |
| Married filing separately | $125,000 |
| Single | $200,000 |
| Head of household | $200,000 |

The IRS Q&A adds one sentence that matters more each year: "Taxpayers should be aware that these threshold amounts are not indexed for inflation." The tax brackets and the standard deduction are adjusted for inflation; Revenue Procedure 2025-32 did that for 2026. The NIIT lines aren't. A household whose income has merely kept pace with inflation since 2013 can cross a threshold it used to be under.

The NIIT is separate from the 0.9% Additional Medicare Tax on wages. The IRS Q&A says "You may be subject to both taxes, but not on the same type of income." The 0.9% tax applies to wages and self-employment income over its own thresholds, "but it does not apply to income items included in Net Investment Income."

## Savings interest is net investment income

The IRS Q&A lists what goes in: "In general, investment income includes, but is not limited to: interest, dividends, capital gains, rental and royalty income, non-qualified annuities," and income from passive businesses and trading.

On Form 8960, interest is the very first line. The instructions for Line 1, Taxable Interest, say to "Enter the amount of taxable interest received," and for most people that means the amount from "Form 1040 or 1040-SR, line 2b." Whatever taxable interest you report on your return, including interest from:

- high-yield savings accounts and regular savings accounts
- CDs, including the yearly interest on multi-year CDs you can't touch yet
- money market deposit accounts
- Treasury bills, notes and bonds

all of it flows to line 1. Treasury interest is exempt from state and local income tax, which is why it often wins a [T-bill vs CD comparison](/2026/09/23/treasury-bills-vs-cds-the-tax-break-that-changes-the-comparison/), but it is federally taxable interest, so it counts for the NIIT the same as bank interest.

What doesn't count, from Topic 559 and the IRS Q&A:

- **Tax-exempt interest.** Topic 559 says the NIIT doesn't apply to income excluded for regular income tax purposes "such as tax-exempt state or municipal bond interest."
- **Wages, Social Security and retirement-plan distributions.** The Q&A lists "Wages, unemployment compensation; operating income from a nonpassive business, Social Security Benefits, alimony, tax-exempt interest, self-employment income," and "distributions from certain Qualified Plans (those described in sections 401(a), 403(a), 403(b), 408, 408A or 457(b))." Publication 550 notes that its interest rules "do not apply to investments held in individual retirement arrangements (IRAs), section 401(k) plans, and other qualified retirement plans," and the distribution from those plans isn't net investment income when it comes out.

The second point comes with a qualification, shown in the examples below. Items that aren't net investment income can still raise your MAGI, and MAGI decides whether the tax applies at all.

## MAGI: the number that decides whether you owe it

For most people, MAGI for the NIIT is simply adjusted gross income. Topic 559 says: "For individual taxpayers who haven't excluded any foreign earned income, their MAGI is generally the same as their regular AGI." The Form 8960 instructions say the same: if you don't own interests in certain foreign corporations or funds "and don't exclude any foreign earned income on Form 2555," you "enter your AGI from Form 1040 or 1040-SR on line 13 of Form 8960."

People who use the foreign earned income exclusion add the excluded amount back, with an adjustment for related deductions. That is relevant to Americans abroad who keep savings in foreign banks, a group we wrote about in [Foreign Savings Accounts and the Oct. 15 FBAR Deadline: The $10,000 Test](/2026/10/14/foreign-savings-accounts-and-the-oct-15-fbar-deadline-the-10000-test/).

Because MAGI is mostly AGI, everything in AGI counts toward the threshold: wages, the taxable part of Social Security benefits, the taxable part of IRA and 401(k) withdrawals, capital gains and the interest itself. Taxable interest enters the calculation twice: once as net investment income and once as part of MAGI.

The form requirement follows the same line. The Form 8960 instructions say: "Attach Form 8960 to your return if your modified adjusted gross income (MAGI) is greater than the applicable threshold amount."

## Example 1: just over the line

These examples use hypothetical households and the 2026 thresholds above. Each assumes no other income, no adjustments to AGI, no foreign income exclusion or other NIIT-specific MAGI adjustments, no investment expenses and no other investment income, so MAGI equals AGI and net investment income equals the interest. Where income tax is shown, the only deduction assumed is the basic 2026 standard deduction.

A married couple filing jointly has $225,000 of wages and $30,000 of interest from savings accounts and CDs.

- MAGI: $225,000 + $30,000 = $255,000
- Excess over the $250,000 threshold: $5,000
- Net investment income: $30,000
- The lesser of $5,000 and $30,000: $5,000
- NIIT: $5,000 × 3.8% = **$190**

The tax isn't 3.8% of the $30,000 of interest, which would be $1,140. It's 3.8% of the $5,000 by which their income crossed the line. People sometimes assume crossing $250,000 by one dollar puts all their interest into the tax. The "lesser of" formula prevents that.

## Example 2: further over the line

Same couple, but wages are $240,000.

- MAGI: $240,000 + $30,000 = $270,000
- Excess over $250,000: $20,000
- Net investment income: $30,000
- The lesser: $20,000
- NIIT: $20,000 × 3.8% = **$760**

Suppose the couple had an extra $1,000 of interest. MAGI becomes $271,000, the excess becomes $21,000 and net investment income becomes $31,000. The lesser is $21,000, and the NIIT is $21,000 × 3.8% = $798, or $38 more. Once MAGI is over the threshold, a dollar of added interest raises both numbers in the formula, so it adds 3.8 cents of NIIT whichever one is smaller. A dollar of added wages works differently: it raises the excess but not net investment income, so it adds NIIT only while the excess is the smaller number.

The regular income tax applies at the same time. Using the 2026 standard deduction for married couples filing jointly, $32,200 under Revenue Procedure 2025-32, the couple's taxable income is $270,000 − $32,200 = $237,800. The 2026 joint table puts taxable income "Over $211,400 but not over $403,550" in the 24% bracket. So the extra $1,000 of interest costs:

| Item | Extra tax on $1,000 more interest |
|---|---|
| Regular income tax at 24% | $240 |
| NIIT at 3.8% | $38 |
| Total federal | $278 |

That is a combined federal marginal rate of 27.8% on the last dollars of interest, before any state income tax. For interest taxed at that marginal rate, a hypothetical 4% APY keeps 72.2% after federal tax, or about 2.9% (4% × 0.722 = 2.888%). Not all of the couple's $30,000 is taxed that high: part of it falls in the 22% bracket, and the NIIT applies only to the $20,000 excess, so the average rate on all of their interest is lower than the marginal rate.

## Example 3: far over the line, where every dollar is taxed

A single filer earns $400,000 in wages and has $10,000 of interest.

- MAGI: $410,000
- Excess over the $200,000 single threshold: $210,000
- Net investment income: $10,000
- The lesser: $10,000
- NIIT: $10,000 × 3.8% = **$380**

Here the interest itself is the smaller number, so all of it is taxed at 3.8%. With the 2026 single standard deduction of $16,100, taxable income is $393,900, which falls in the 2026 single bracket "Over $256,225 but not over $640,600," taxed at 35%. Each extra dollar of interest carries 35% regular tax plus 3.8% NIIT, or 38.8% federal. At the top 37% bracket, the combined federal rate on interest would be 40.8%.

## Example 4: retirement withdrawals push savings interest into the tax

This case involves retirement income. A single filer takes $170,000 from a traditional IRA during the year, all of it taxable, and earns $45,000 of interest on CDs and savings.

- MAGI: $170,000 + $45,000 = $215,000
- Excess over $200,000: $15,000
- Net investment income: $45,000 (the IRA distribution isn't net investment income)
- The lesser: $15,000
- NIIT: $15,000 × 3.8% = **$570**

The IRA withdrawal isn't taxed by the NIIT, but it's in AGI, so it pushes MAGI over the threshold and exposes the interest. Had the same person taken $150,000 from the IRA instead, MAGI would have been $195,000 and the NIIT would have been zero. The IRS Q&A has a parallel example with wages: a single filer with "$180,000 and $15,000 of dividends and capital gains" has MAGI of $195,000, "which is less than the $200,000 statutory threshold," and "is not subject to the Net Investment Income Tax."

The same logic runs the other way for anything that lowers AGI. IRS Topic 424 says pre-tax 401(k) elective deferrals generally aren't reported as wages on Form 1040, so they don't count in AGI or MAGI. Whether any particular change affects your NIIT depends on where your MAGI and net investment income land, so the answer depends on the full return.

## Special cases for savers

**Joint accounts and who reports the interest.** The NIIT is figured return by return, so when joint owners file separate returns, how the interest on a joint account is divided between them matters. We cover how the 1099-INT is divided in [Joint Savings Account, One 1099-INT: Who Reports the Interest and How](/2026/09/30/joint-savings-account-one-1099-int-who-reports-the-interest-and-how/).

**Married filing separately.** The threshold for married filing separately is $125,000, half of the joint figure. Which spouse reports the interest isn't decided by the account title alone. Publication 550 says that for property held jointly, "each person's share of any interest or dividends from the property is determined by local law," and community property states have their own rules for splitting income between spouses.

**A child's interest reported on your return.** For parents who elect to report a child's interest on their own return with Form 8814, the IRS Q&A says "The amounts of Net Investment Income that are included on your Form 1040 by reason of Form 8814 are included in calculating your Net Investment Income." Amounts excluded from your return under Form 8814's own thresholds don't count. Our article on [Savings Accounts for Kids: Custodial Rules and the Kiddie Tax](/2026/09/17/savings-accounts-for-kids-custodial-rules-and-the-kiddie-tax/) covers Form 8814 itself.

**Foreign tax credits don't reduce it.** For savers with accounts abroad, the IRS Q&A says foreign income tax credits "are allowed as credits only against the tax imposed by chapter 1 of the Code, and therefore may not be used to reduce your NIIT liability."

**Investment expenses.** Net investment income is gross investment income minus deductions "properly allocable" to it. The older IRS Q&A lists examples that include investment advisory and brokerage fees and tax preparation fees, but the current Form 8960 instructions say "Miscellaneous investment expenses are generally no longer deductible," and that P.L. 119-21 made that disallowance permanent. Deductions that can still apply include investment interest expense and, for taxpayers who itemize, the share of state, local and foreign income taxes attributable to net investment income, subject to the limits in the instructions. (The instructions add that you "may not take a deduction for any foreign income taxes paid for the tax year if you took a credit for any portion of them.") One adjustment matters directly to savers and doesn't depend on itemizing: "Early withdrawal penalty (Schedule 1 (Form 1040), line 18) is reported on Form 8960, line 10," which reduces net investment income. Beyond that, a saver whose investment income is all bank interest may have little to subtract.

**Nonresidents.** The IRS Q&A says "Nonresident Aliens (NRAs) are not subject to the Net Investment Income Tax," with special rules for an NRA married to a U.S. citizen or resident who elects to file jointly.

## How the 3.8% changes yield comparisons

Savers compare accounts on after-tax yield, and the NIIT changes that math only in specific cases.

Between two kinds of taxable interest, it changes nothing. A CD, a high-yield savings account and a Treasury bill all produce federally taxable interest, and all of it goes on Form 8960 line 1, so the NIIT treats them the same way. The Treasury bill's advantage over a bank product is still the state and local exemption, not anything about the NIIT.

Between taxable interest and tax-exempt interest, it widens the gap. Because Topic 559 excludes "tax-exempt state or municipal bond interest," a household paying the NIIT gives up 3.8 more cents of each taxable interest dollar that it wouldn't give up on tax-exempt interest. A common way to compare the two is the taxable-equivalent yield: the tax-exempt yield divided by (1 minus the combined marginal rate).

Using a hypothetical 3% tax-exempt yield and a hypothetical 4% taxable yield, ignoring state taxes:

| Federal marginal rate on interest | Taxable yield equal to 3% tax-exempt | Kept from $40 of taxable interest ($1,000 at 4%) |
|---|---|---|
| 24% (under the NIIT threshold) | about 3.9% | $30.40 |
| 27.8% (24% + 3.8% NIIT) | about 4.2% | $28.88 |
| 38.8% (35% + 3.8% NIIT) | about 4.9% | $24.48 |
| 40.8% (37% + 3.8% NIIT) | about 5.1% | $23.68 |

The math: 3 ÷ (1 − 0.278) = 4.16, and $40 × (1 − 0.278) = $28.88. These are illustrations, not quotes. Tax-exempt bonds and funds carry price risk and other features a bank deposit doesn't, and an [FDIC](https://www.fdic.gov/resources/deposit-insurance)-insured account isn't the same kind of holding as a bond fund, so after-tax yield is only one part of that comparison.

## Savings held in a trust or estate

The NIIT also applies to estates and trusts, and the threshold there is far lower. Topic 559 says that for an estate or trust, the tax is 3.8% on the lesser of "the undistributed net investment income, or the excess (if any) of: the adjusted gross income over the dollar amount at which the highest tax bracket begins for an estate or trust for the tax year." It gives the 2025 threshold: "$15,650."

For 2026, Revenue Procedure 2025-32's table for estates and trusts starts the top 37% bracket at taxable income "Over $16,000," so that is the 2026 figure under the same rule. A trust that holds CDs and keeps the interest instead of distributing it can cross $16,000 of AGI quickly. It then owes 3.8% on the excess, or on its undistributed net investment income if that is smaller, at an income level where an individual would owe nothing.

This matters for trusts that file their own returns and keep their income. The Form 8960 instructions list trusts that aren't subject to the NIIT at the trust level, including "Trusts that are classified as 'grantor trusts' under sections 671–679." Whether a particular trust is a grantor trust, so that its income is reported by the grantor under the individual thresholds, is a question for the trust's tax preparer. The IRS Q&A also lists trusts that aren't subject to the tax, including "Trusts that are exempt from income taxes imposed by Subtitle A of the Internal Revenue Code (e.g., charitable trusts and qualified retirement plan trusts exempt from tax under section 501, and Charitable Remainder Trusts exempt from tax under section 664)."

## Paying it: withholding and estimated tax

The IRS Q&A, answering whether the tax has to be withheld from wages, says: "No, but you may request that additional income tax be withheld from your wages."

The tax is part of the estimated tax rules. The Q&A says: "The Net Investment Income Tax is subject to the estimated tax provisions." Topic 559 adds that taxpayers "may need to increase their income tax withholding or estimated taxes because of any additional tax liability from the NIIT to avoid certain penalties," and points to Publication 505.

In practice, the NIIT is one more piece of the total tax that the estimated tax rules measure. January 15, 2027 is the last regular estimated payment date for 2026. Whether earlier installments were also needed depends on withholding, the safe harbors and when during the year the income came in. We walked through the $1,000 underpayment test in [Savings Interest and the Jan. 15, 2027 Estimated Tax: The $1,000 Test](/2026/10/07/savings-interest-and-the-jan-15-2027-estimated-tax-the-1000-test/).

On the return, the tax is figured on Form 8960 and carried over. The Form 8960 instructions for Line 17 say Form 1040 filers "Include this amount on Schedule 2 (Form 1040), line 12."

## A fourth-quarter check

Mid-October leaves about two and a half months of 2026 interest still to come. A rough projection uses three figures:

1. Projected 2026 AGI, counting each taxable item once: wages, interest credited so far plus an estimate for the rest of the year, dividends and capital gains, the taxable part of retirement withdrawals and Social Security, minus any adjustments to income.
2. Projected net investment income: interest, dividends, gains and other investment income, minus the deductions the Form 8960 instructions allow.
3. The threshold for the filing status: $250,000, $200,000 or $125,000.

For most people without excluded foreign income, the first figure is the NIIT MAGI. If it's under the threshold, the NIIT doesn't apply for the year. If it's over, the excess and net investment income are compared, and the smaller number times 3.8% is the rough NIIT. Whether withholding and estimated payments already cover it is a separate question for the estimated tax rules.

Here is how the tax scales for a married couple filing jointly with $30,000 of interest, at different wage levels:

| Wages | MAGI | Excess over $250,000 | NIIT (3.8% of the lesser) |
|---|---|---|---|
| $215,000 | $245,000 | $0 | $0 |
| $225,000 | $255,000 | $5,000 | $190 |
| $240,000 | $270,000 | $20,000 | $760 |
| $250,000 | $280,000 | $30,000 | $1,140 |
| $300,000 | $330,000 | $80,000 | $1,140 |

Once the excess reaches the full $30,000 of interest, the NIIT stops growing with wages. At that point it equals 3.8% of the interest, $1,140, and only more investment income raises it.

## Common questions

**Does the NIIT apply to interest on my emergency fund?**

If your MAGI is over the threshold, yes. The tax doesn't look at why you hold the money. Taxable interest from a savings account is on Form 8960 line 1 the same as any other taxable interest.

**Is interest on Treasury bills exempt?**

From state and local income tax, yes. From the NIIT, no. It's federally taxable interest reported on Form 1040 line 2b, and that is the figure Form 8960 starts from.

**Do municipal bond funds avoid it?**

Topic 559 says the NIIT doesn't apply to "tax-exempt state or municipal bond interest." Whether a particular fund's income is fully tax-exempt is a question for the fund's tax reporting.

**Will the thresholds go up for 2027?**

Not under current law. The IRS says "these threshold amounts are not indexed for inflation." The yearly inflation revenue procedure adjusts many other figures but not these.

**Does Social Security count?**

Social Security benefits aren't net investment income. The taxable part of benefits is in AGI, so it does count toward MAGI. Our article [Does Savings Interest Make Your Social Security Taxable?](/2026/10/11/does-savings-interest-make-your-social-security-taxable/) explains how interest affects that taxable part.

**I'm under the threshold. Do I need Form 8960?**

The instructions say to attach it "if your modified adjusted gross income (MAGI) is greater than the applicable threshold amount." Below the threshold, the tax doesn't apply.

The formula itself is short: 3.8% of the smaller of net investment income or MAGI above the threshold. Estimating it takes a projected MAGI, total net investment income and the filing-status threshold; interest alone is enough only when, as in the examples, it's the household's only investment income. Tax software or a tax professional can confirm the exact figure on Form 8960.
