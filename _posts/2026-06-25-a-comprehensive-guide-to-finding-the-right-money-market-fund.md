A money market fund and a money market account share three words and almost nothing else. One is a mutual fund you buy through a brokerage. The other is a deposit account at a bank or credit union, covered by federal deposit insurance up to the standard limit. The names are nearly identical and the protections behind them are not, which makes one assumption costly: that cash held at a brokerage carries the same federal guarantee a bank deposit does.

This post covers what a money market fund owns, how its yield is quoted and why that quote is not an APY, which risks the prospectus spells out, what SIPC does and does not cover, and a worked comparison against an insured deposit. Every rate stated here as current fact is an FDIC national average as of August 17, 2026. The fund figures in the example are labeled hypothetical, because fund yields move daily and only the fund's own page reports today's number.

Nothing below is a recommendation to buy or avoid either product. The aim is narrower and more useful: that you can read a fund's fact sheet next to a bank's disclosure and know precisely what each one is promising.

## Fund versus deposit account

The distinction is legal, not cosmetic. A money market fund is a registered investment company — a mutual fund — and you own shares of it. A money market deposit account is money you have lent to a bank, and the bank owes it back.

| | Money market fund | Money market deposit account |
|---|---|---|
| What you hold | Shares of a mutual fund | A deposit claim on the bank |
| Federal deposit insurance | None | FDIC or NCUA, $250,000 per depositor, per insured institution, per ownership category |
| If the institution fails | SIPC may cover missing assets at a failed brokerage; investment losses are not covered | Insured deposits are paid up to the limit |
| Yield quote | 7-day yield, net of fund expenses, restated daily | APY set by the institution, which can change |
| Value of principal | Not guaranteed; the prospectus states the objective | Does not fluctuate |

Deposits at a bank that fails are insured. Investments are not. That single line is the whole distinction, and it holds even when the two yields look similar.

## The holdings inside the fund

Money market funds hold short-term debt. A government fund concentrates on Treasury bills, government agency issues, and repurchase agreements collateralized by government securities; its prospectus states the minimum share of assets it must keep in those categories. A prime fund can also hold short-term corporate obligations such as commercial paper and negotiable bank CDs. A tax-exempt fund holds short-term municipal debt.

These funds are registered with the SEC and run under Rule 2a-7, which imposes requirements on maturity, credit quality, diversification, and liquidity. The rule sets the boundaries; the prospectus tells you where inside those boundaries a specific fund chose to sit. If you read nothing else, read the fund's stated investment objective and its list of principal risks.

## The yield quote and the expense ratio

A fund reports a seven-day yield: the income earned over the past seven days, annualized. The seven-day effective yield restates the same figure assuming that income compounds. Both are backward-looking and both change daily.

An APY on a deposit is a different animal. It reflects a rate the institution has set, together with its compounding, and it applies going forward until the institution changes it. Comparing a fund's seven-day yield to a bank's APY is reasonable as a rough sizing exercise, as long as you remember that only one of the two numbers is a promise about tomorrow.

One practical detail prevents double-counting: the quoted seven-day yield is already net of the fund's expenses. If a fund's expense ratio is 0.30% and its posted yield is 3.00%, the 0.30% has been taken out before that 3.00% was printed. Do not subtract it again. Some funds also waive part of their fee; the prospectus states whether a waiver is contractual and when it is scheduled to end.

## Risk points in a money market fund

None of these are exotic. All of them appear in the prospectus, usually within a page or two of each other.

- **Share price.** A fund that seeks a stable $1.00 share price is pursuing an objective, not delivering a guarantee. Falling below that price has a name in the industry — breaking the buck — which tells you it is a thing that can happen.
- **Liquidity provisions.** SEC rules allow certain funds to impose a liquidity fee under stated conditions. Whether and how that applies to a given fund is described in its prospectus, not inferred from the fund's name.
- **Rate sensitivity.** The portfolio is short-dated, so the yield resets quickly as the debt it holds matures and is replaced. Today's posted yield is not locked in for you the way a CD rate is.
- **Credit quality.** Prime and municipal funds hold obligations of non-government issuers. The prospectus states the credit standards the fund applies.
- **Settlement timing.** Redeeming shares follows the fund's settlement schedule, which is not the same as pulling money out of a deposit account. Check the stated timing before you rely on the money being available on a particular day.

## SIPC coverage and its limits

SIPC exists for one narrow situation: a member brokerage firm fails and customer assets are missing. In that case SIPC's published limit is $500,000 per customer, which includes a $250,000 limit on claims for cash. Confirm the current terms with SIPC directly rather than with a brokerage's marketing page.

What SIPC does not do matters more. It does not insure against a fund losing value, and it is not a substitute for FDIC or NCUA coverage on deposits. If a fund's share price drops, that is an investment outcome, and no federal program makes you whole.

### Cash sweep destinations

If your brokerage moves uninvested cash automatically, find out where it goes. Some sweep programs place cash on deposit at partner banks, in which case FDIC coverage applies at each of those banks subject to the standard limit. Others sweep into a money market fund, in which case it does not. The brokerage's cash sweep disclosure names the destination and, for bank programs, lists the participating banks. That document answers whether the cash sitting in your account is insured or invested, which is worth knowing before you need the answer.

## A worked comparison

Round numbers, chosen so you can follow the arithmetic and then substitute the quotes you actually find. Assume $25,000 held for one year, a constant balance, simple annual proration, and no compounding.

**Insured deposit.** At the FDIC national average money market rate of 0.63% APY as of August 17, 2026: $25,000 × 0.0063 = **$157.50**.

**Hypothetical fund.** Suppose it earns 3.30% gross and charges a 0.30% expense ratio, leaving a posted seven-day yield of 3.00%.

- Gross: $25,000 × 0.0330 = $825.00
- Expenses: $25,000 × 0.0030 = $75.00
- Net: $825.00 − $75.00 = **$750.00**

The gap is $750.00 − $157.50 = **$592.50**, and $157.50 + $592.50 = $750.00 confirms it.

Two cautions about that gap. The 0.63% is a national average across reporting institutions, not the best available rate, and the 3.00% is invented for the illustration. What the arithmetic genuinely shows is smaller and more durable: 0.30% of expenses removed exactly $75.00, and that deduction was already inside the yield the fund advertised.

### Tax-exempt yields

Comparing a tax-exempt fund to a taxable one requires converting. Divide the tax-free yield by (1 − your marginal federal rate). A 2.00% tax-free yield at a 24% marginal rate: 0.0200 ÷ 0.76 = 0.0263158, or **2.63%** rounded to two decimals. State and local taxes may still apply, so treat that as a federal-only comparison.

## The place for a fund in a cash plan

Money you cannot afford to watch fluctuate — this month's rent, the deductible, the emergency fund — has an obvious home in insured deposits, and the $250,000 limit covers more than a single figure suggests, because single, joint, certain retirement, and revocable trust accounts are each insured separately. FDIC trust-account rules were revised effective April 1, 2024, so if trusts are part of your plan, run the numbers through the FDIC's EDIE estimator instead of any formula you remember.

For everything else, a money market fund is a legitimate tool whose terms are written down. Read the fact sheet for the yield and the expense ratio, read the prospectus for the risks and the settlement timing, and keep the two buckets labeled correctly in your own head. The names will keep being confusing. Your file of saved documents does not have to be.
