"Three to six months of expenses" is the answer you hear everywhere. It is a fine starting point and a weak answer, because it never asks what your expenses actually are or how long your income might be interrupted. A two-income renting household and a single earner with a child and commission-based pay get handed the same range, which cannot be right for both.

What follows is a worksheet. You count essential monthly expenses, adjust the month count for five conditions that either lengthen an income gap or raise the cost of a single emergency, multiply, and get a dollar figure. Then you turn that figure into a funding schedule with a date on it. Every number in the example is worked out so you can drop your own in.

Settle one thing up front: the size of the fund matters far more than the yield on it. The arithmetic for that is in its own section below, and it is not close.

## Essential expenses, not total spending

Your emergency fund covers the expenses that keep running after your income stops. That is a shorter list than your monthly spending.

Count these:

- Housing — rent or mortgage payment, plus property tax and homeowners or renters insurance if they are not already inside the payment
- Utilities and phone — electricity, gas, water, internet, mobile
- Groceries — food you cook, not restaurants
- Transportation — car payment, insurance, fuel, transit passes; enough to get to interviews and appointments
- Health — the premium you would pay yourself, plus recurring prescriptions
- Minimum debt payments — the contractual minimum, not your accelerated payoff amount
- Dependent care you cannot pause

Leave out subscriptions, dining out, travel, gifts, retirement contributions, and extra debt payments. Those are the first things to cut in a real emergency, which is exactly why they do not belong in the target.

One caution on health coverage. If your insurance runs through an employer, your premium after a job loss is not the payroll-deducted amount you see on your pay stub. Price out continuation coverage or a marketplace plan for your household and use that figure. It is frequently the largest single gap between your current budget and your emergency budget.

## Five risk adjustments

Start at three months. Add months for each condition that applies. This is a judgment framework, not a measured result — its whole job is to make you answer specific questions instead of picking a round number.

| Condition | Add |
|---|---|
| One income supports the household | +1 month |
| Income is variable — commission, tips, freelance, seasonal | +1.5 months |
| Specialized role, or your field hires slowly where you live | +1 month |
| Someone depends on your income | +1 month |
| You carry a large uninsured exposure — a deductible above one month of essentials, or repairs that fall on you | +0.5 month |

If the total lands above twelve months, stop at twelve and put the surplus toward whatever is generating the risk instead: paying off the debt, or building a second income stream.

If you are carrying revolving debt right now, you face two costs at once — the interest on the balance, and the risk of adding to that balance the next time something breaks. A workable order is one month of essentials first, then the debt, then the full target. Run it against your own card's APR before you commit.

## A worked example

One earner, salaried, one child, renting, employer health plan with a $4,000 deductible.

| Essential expense | Monthly |
|---|---|
| Rent | $1,650 |
| Utilities and phone | $240 |
| Groceries | $520 |
| Transportation | $610 |
| Health premium and prescriptions | $310 |
| Minimum debt payments | $180 |
| **Total** | **$3,510** |

Adding those six lines: 1,650 + 240 + 520 + 610 + 310 + 180 = **$3,510 per month**.

Now the adjustments. Base of 3 months. One income, so +1. One dependent, so +1. The $4,000 deductible is larger than one month of essentials ($3,510), so +0.5. Variable income does not apply, and the specialized-role condition does not apply.

3 + 1 + 1 + 0.5 = **5.5 months**

Target = $3,510 × 5.5 = **$19,305.00**. (Check it: $3,510 × 5 = $17,550, and $3,510 × 0.5 = $1,755. $17,550 + $1,755 = $19,305.)

Note how far that sits from the reflexive answer. Three months would have been $10,530 and six months $21,060. The worksheet lands between them, and it lands there for reasons you can point at.

## Yield vs. size

Where the money sits changes the outcome by less than most people expect. Here is the comparison.

The FDIC's national average rates as of August 17, 2026 were 0.38% APY for savings accounts, 0.63% APY for money market accounts, and 0.07% APY for interest checking. Those are averages across insured institutions — not offers, not the best terms available, and individual accounts sit on both sides of them.

Suppose this household funds the target over two years, so the balance climbs from $4,200 to $19,305 and averages roughly ($4,200 + $19,305) ÷ 2 = $11,752.50. Using simple interest on that average balance as a rough approximation:

- At 0.38%: $11,752.50 × 0.0038 = $44.66 per year, or **$89.32 over two years**
- At 0.63%: $11,752.50 × 0.0063 = $74.04 per year, or **$148.08 over two years**

The difference is $148.08 − $89.32 = **$58.76** across two full years. One extra month of essential expenses in this household is $3,510. The rate you chase is worth about 1.7% of a single month of coverage.

That does not make yield pointless — it is free money and you should take it. It makes yield the last decision, not the first one.

## Where the cash sits

The binding requirement is that you can reach the money in a day or two without paying to do it. That rules out anything with a withdrawal penalty attached for the core of the fund.

Savings accounts and money market accounts both clear that bar. Regulation D's six-transfer-per-month limit was suspended in 2020, but many banks still impose limits of their own, so read the deposit agreement rather than assuming the cap disappeared. CDs pay more — the FDIC national average for a 12-month CD was 1.71% APY on August 17, 2026, against 0.38% for savings — and you buy that gap by accepting an early-withdrawal penalty stated in the disclosure. That trade is fine for money you have already decided not to touch. It is wrong for the first tier of an emergency fund.

FDIC insurance covers $250,000 per depositor, per insured bank, per ownership category; credit unions carry the same standard limit through the NCUA. Ownership categories — single, joint, certain retirement accounts, revocable trusts — are insured separately from one another, so a couple's joint account is covered apart from either person's individual accounts. Most emergency funds sit nowhere near that ceiling. The number starts to matter if you are also parking a home down payment or a business reserve at the same bank, since it is the combined balance within each category that counts.

One practical detail worth more than the rate difference: hold the fund at an institution separate from your everyday checking. Transfers still clear in a day or two, and the balance stops sitting next to your spending money every time you open the app.

## A funding schedule

A target without a date is a wish. Take the example household with $4,200 already saved:

$19,305.00 − $4,200.00 = **$15,105.00 to go**

At $650 a month: 23 payments of $650 come to $14,950, leaving $155 in month 24. So the honest answer is two years, not "someday." Interest adds roughly $89 over that stretch by the calculation above, which shortens it by a few days.

Two years is a long time to look at one number, so set checkpoints you actually reach:

1. **$1,000** — covers most single repairs without a credit card
2. **One month, $3,510** — covers a paycheck that arrives late
3. **Three months, $10,530** — covers a short gap between jobs
4. **$19,305** — the full target

Automate the transfer for the day after payday and route irregular money — a tax refund, a bonus, a rebate — into the same account. Raising the monthly amount to $800 pulls the finish line in: $15,105 ÷ $800 = 18.88, so 18 payments of $800 come to $14,400, leaving $705 in month 19.

## The short version

Count essentials, not spending. Start at three months and adjust for the five conditions that apply to you. Multiply, write the number down, and attach a monthly transfer and a completion date to it. Keep the whole thing somewhere insured and reachable, take whatever yield you can get without giving up access, and stop optimizing after that — the balance is what protects you, and the rate is a rounding error against it.
