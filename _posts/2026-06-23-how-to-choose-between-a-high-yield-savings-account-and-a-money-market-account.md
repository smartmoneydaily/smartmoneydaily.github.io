A high-yield savings account and a money market account occupy the same slot on a bank's menu: an insured deposit account that pays interest and is not built for daily spending. The labels differ more than the mechanics do. Compare the two on advertised rate alone and you can end up moving thousands of dollars for a difference of a few dollars a month.

The question that actually separates them is how money gets out. One account may come with a checkbook and a card. Another may only move funds by transfer to a linked account. Same insurance, same deposit status, very different behavior on the day you need $8,000 for a contractor.

What follows: the insurance rule that covers both, the access features that distinguish them, the tier arithmetic that turns an advertised APY into the rate you earn, where the FDIC's published national averages sat on August 17, 2026, and three saver profiles to hold your own cash against.

## Two labels, one insurance rule

Both are deposit accounts. At an FDIC-insured bank, standard coverage is $250,000 per depositor, per insured bank, per ownership category. Credit unions carry the same standard limit through the NCUA. Ownership categories — single, joint, certain retirement accounts, revocable trusts — are insured separately, so a couple's joint account is covered apart from either person's individual account at the same institution.

One naming trap is worth knowing. A money market *deposit account* opened at a bank is a deposit and is insured. A money market *fund* bought through a brokerage is an investment. Deposits at a failed bank are insured; investments are not. The two products share most of a name and none of that protection.

If a trust holds the money, the FDIC revised its trust-account rules effective April 1, 2024. Rather than working from an older per-beneficiary formula you may have read somewhere, run the account through the FDIC's EDIE estimator, which applies the current rules to your specific setup.

## The access difference

The product name does not determine how you withdraw. The account disclosure does. Read the feature list and note which of these the account supports:

- Check writing
- A debit or ATM card
- ACH transfer to an external account, plus any daily or monthly cap
- Outgoing wire, and its fee
- Branch or mail withdrawal

None of those features is implied by the product name, in either direction. Verify each one on the disclosure for the specific account in front of you, and treat anything absent from that list as unavailable.

Speed belongs in the same list. A transfer to an outside institution moves on a business-day schedule, and the account's funds-availability terms state when the money is usable at the other end. If a payment has a hard deadline, that timing matters as much as the withdrawal method — which is what an outgoing wire, fee included, exists for.

Regulation D once capped certain transfers out of savings and money market accounts at six per month. That limit was suspended in 2020, and many banks still set their own limits. Where a limit exists, the charge for exceeding it appears in the fee schedule. If you plan to pay anything directly from the account, that number is the first thing to look up.

## Tier arithmetic

An advertised APY may apply to every dollar, or only to dollars inside a band. Two accounts can publish identical tier tables and pay different amounts, because the tiers can be applied two different ways.

Take a hypothetical account, used here only to show the math: it pays 1.00% APY on the first $15,000 and 0.40% APY on the portion above that. You keep $40,000 in it for a full year at an unchanged rate.

- First $15,000 at 1.00% = $150.00
- Next $25,000 at 0.40% = $100.00
- Total interest = $150.00 + $100.00 = $250.00
- Blended APY = $250.00 ÷ $40,000 = 0.625%

Now apply the same table the other way, where your balance selects one rate for the entire amount. At $40,000 you fall in the 0.40% band, so the whole balance earns it: $40,000 × 0.40% = $160.00. Identical headline numbers, $90.00 less interest, since $250.00 − $160.00 = $90.00.

Neither method is hidden. The rate sheet states which one applies, often in a footnote separating "on balances of" from "on the portion of the balance." Where the wording is ambiguous, ask the bank to confirm in writing which calculation the account uses, and on which balance measure — the daily balance or the average across the statement period.

### Reading the rate you were quoted

APY includes the effect of compounding; a stated interest rate does not. Comparing an APY on one account against an interest rate on another understates the second one. The figures above assume a constant rate for twelve months with no deposits or withdrawals, which is simple annual proration — a real year with a moving balance will differ.

## National averages as a benchmark

The FDIC publishes national average deposit rates. As of August 17, 2026:

| Account type | FDIC national average APY |
|---|---|
| Savings | 0.38% |
| Money market | 0.63% |
| Interest checking | 0.07% |
| 12-month CD | 1.71% |

These are averages across insured institutions, not rates you can walk in and open. Their use is as a floor check: an account paying well under the average for its category is worth a question. That the money market average sits above the savings average, 0.63% against 0.38% as of August 17, 2026, is a fact about the two categories, not a promise about any particular account.

## Variable rates and repricing

Both account types generally carry a variable rate. The deposit agreement states whether the bank may change that rate at its discretion and whether it will notify you first. That clause explains why an account opened at an appealing number may not be one now, and it is worth reading before you treat a quoted rate as fixed.

Two mechanisms move deposit pricing. The Federal Reserve sets policy rates, which affect what institutions earn on funds and what they must offer to attract them. Separately, institutions compete with one another for deposits; rates inside a single category are not uniform, which is exactly what the existence of a national average implies. Neither mechanism tells you where any particular rate goes next.

What you can do is measure. Take the interest posted on your statement and work backward. If a steady $12,000 balance earned $18.90 over a quarter, annualize it: $18.90 × 4 = $75.60, and $75.60 ÷ $12,000 = 0.63%. Set that against the category average for the same period and you have a number that does not depend on what a marketing page currently says.

## Fees against the rate gap

Put $25,000 in each category for a year at those averages, using simple annual proration:

- At 0.38%: $25,000 × 0.0038 = $95.00
- At 0.63%: $25,000 × 0.0063 = $157.50
- Difference: $157.50 − $95.00 = $62.50

A $5 monthly maintenance fee costs $60.00 over the same year. Charged against the higher-yielding account, it leaves $157.50 − $60.00 = $97.50, which is $2.50 more than the fee-free account at the lower rate. The entire category advantage came down to $2.50 because of one line in the fee schedule. Read the fee schedule and the balance conditions that waive the fee before you compare rates at all.

## Three saver profiles

- **An emergency fund you hope not to touch.** Withdrawal features barely matter; net yield after fees and insurance coverage do. Either product qualifies. Decide on the net number, not the label.
- **Money with a payment already scheduled out of it.** A property tax bill, a tuition installment, a contractor draw. Here the checkbook, card, wire fee, and transfer limit decide it. Count the number of withdrawals you expect in a month and compare that against the account's stated limit.
- **A balance near the coverage limit.** Above $250,000 at one bank in one ownership category, the rate is a secondary question. Split across ownership categories or institutions, and confirm the result with EDIE rather than assuming.

## The short version

Both products are insured deposits, so insurance is not the tiebreaker. Rate is a weak tiebreaker too, because a tier table and a monthly fee can erase the gap, as the $25,000 example shows. The tiebreaker is the withdrawal method you will actually use, priced against the fee schedule. Pull up the disclosure for each account, find the tier structure, the fee waiver conditions, and the transfer limit, and the choice usually makes itself.
