You moved money out of savings for the seventh time this month and a $5 fee landed on the account. You searched for the rule, found article after article saying the six-withdrawal limit was scrapped in 2020, and the fee is still sitting there on the statement.

Both things are true. The federal requirement went away. The sentence in your deposit agreement is a different document, and nobody deleted that one on your behalf.

## What the Federal Reserve actually did

The limit existed for a reason that stopped applying in March 2020. Regulation D divided deposits into reservable transaction accounts and non-reservable savings deposits, and the cap on convenient transfers was the boundary between them. Then [the Board reduced reserve requirement ratios to zero percent](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy/reservereq.htm), effective March 26, 2020, for every depository institution. With nothing to reserve against, the boundary had no job left.

That April the Board issued an interim final rule deleting the six-per-month limit from the definition of a savings deposit. The current text of 12 CFR 204.2(d) says a depositor may make transfers and withdrawals "regardless of the number of such transfers and withdrawals or the manner in which such transfers and withdrawals are made."

The Board's own FAQ is blunt about the limits of that. The interim final rule "permits depository institutions to suspend enforcement of the six-transfer limit, but it does not require depository institutions to do so." Asked whether the change was a temporary pandemic measure, the [Federal Reserve answered](https://www.federalreserve.gov/supervisionreg/savings-deposits-frequently-asked-questions.htm) that the monetary policy framework behind it "is not a short-term choice," and that the Board "does not have plans to re-impose transfer limits but may make adjustments to the definition of savings accounts in response to comments received on the Board's interim final rule and, in the future, if conditions warrant." Read the whole sentence and you get a durable position with a door left open, which is still not a repeal of anything your bank had you sign.

Supervision moved the same direction. On March 29, 2021, the Board joined the other FFIEC agencies in [suspending its consumer compliance examiners' use of the Regulation D Examination Procedures](https://www.federalreserve.gov/supervisionreg/caletters/caltr2106.htm). The same letter is precise about what did not change: institutions still owe Truth in Savings disclosures, and "any subsequent changes to these terms that adversely affect consumers must also be disclosed at least 30 days prior to the effective date of the change." Examiners put the transfer count away in 2021 and left the disclosure obligation exactly where it was.

## Why the limit outlived the rule

Deleting a line from the Code of Federal Regulations does not delete it from a deposit agreement. Rewriting one of those means legal review, new disclosures, and core-system changes to stop counting and stop charging, all on a term that also produces fee income. Some banks did the work within weeks. Some never started.

The more consequential detail is that a bank writing its own limit is no longer working inside the old boundaries. The federal count applied to a defined list, mainly preauthorized, automatic, and telephone transfers plus transfers by check, debit card, or similar order payable to a third party, while withdrawals made in person, by mail, or at an ATM were exempt. That exemption is [why savings felt restricted through the app and unrestricted at the teller window](/2026/08/12/what-you-should-know-about-accessing-funds-from-a-money-market-account/).

Nothing preserves the split now, and two large banks show how far apart the results sit.

[Truist](https://www.truist.com/savings/truist-one-savings/disclosures-and-fees) One Savings: "$5 will be assessed per withdrawal over six (6) with a maximum of six (6) Withdrawal Limit Fees per statement cycle," and the fee "applies, regardless of the balance, to all withdrawals and transfers made from a Truist personal savings and/or money market account including those made at a branch, ATM, by mail, or through any electronic means."

[Ally](https://www.ally.com/bank/online-savings-account/) Online Savings, which counts ten limited withdrawals and transfers per statement cycle: "There's no fee for going over the limit, but we'll close your account if you exceed it on more than an occasional basis."

| | Truist One Savings | Ally Online Savings |
|---|---|---|
| Transactions before the limit applies | 6 per statement cycle | 10 per statement cycle |
| Price of one excess item | $5 | none |
| Methods that count | branch, ATM, mail, electronic | limited withdrawals and transfers |
| Worst case in one cycle | $30, the six-fee ceiling | account closed |

An in-person withdrawal that the federal rule exempted for decades is billable at the first bank and free at the second. Same deregulated environment, opposite policies, and the only way to know which shape yours took is to open your own document.

## Finding it in your own disclosure

What federal law still guarantees is that the answer is written down somewhere you can reach. Truth in Savings, Regulation DD, requires an account disclosure to state [any limitations on the number or dollar amount of withdrawals or deposits](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1030/4/) along with the amount of any fee and the conditions under which it may be imposed.

1. **Get the right document.** It is the Truth in Savings disclosure, the personal deposit account agreement, or the schedule of fees, usually reachable from the account page online without calling anyone.
2. **Search the text, not the index.** Useful terms are "withdrawal limit," "excess," "excessive," "restricted transfer," "transaction limit," and "per statement cycle." Searching for "Regulation D" often returns nothing, because plenty of banks removed the phrase while keeping the mechanic.
3. **Answer four questions.** How many transactions, which methods count toward that number, what the fee is and whether it is capped, and what happens if it keeps happening.
4. **Check the period.** A statement cycle is not a calendar month. Two transfers on the 30th and the 2nd may fall in the same cycle or in different ones depending on where your cycle closes.
5. **Look for a notice if the terms moved.** Under [Regulation DD](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1030/5/), a change to a disclosed term that adversely affects you has to be mailed or delivered "at least 30 calendar days before the effective date of the change." That is the same 30 days the Board reminded institutions about in 2021. A new limit or a new fee should have reached you in writing before it reached the statement.

## What exceeding it actually costs

The first response is a per-transaction fee, and the cap matters more than the headline number. Truist prints its ceiling, so a bad month there tops out at $30 and stops. The cap is the number to hunt for in your own schedule, because it, not the $5, sets your worst case. If the schedule names a per-item price and no ceiling, you have not found your worst case yet.

Then measure that ceiling against what the balance actually earns, at your rate rather than a headline one. Take a $3,000 savings balance. At a high-yield rate near 4%, the year pays about $120, so one capped month of fees takes a quarter of it. At the [FDIC](https://www.fdic.gov/national-rates-and-rate-caps) national average for savings, 0.38% APY as of August 17, 2026, the same $3,000 pays about $11.40 for the year, and a single $30 month costs close to three years of interest.

The second response is structural. Under the old rule, an institution had to either block excess transfers or monitor them after the fact and contact customers who went over, and for persistent cases close the account or strip its transfer capability. That obligation is gone. What replaced it is discretion, and some banks kept the harsher end of it, as Ally's wording shows. Conversion to a checking account is another outcome banks use, and it usually drops the whole balance to a lower rate.

An excess transaction fee is not an overdraft and does not reach a credit report. A closure can. Banks and credit unions report account information to account screening companies such as ChexSystems and Early Warning Services, and the [Consumer Financial Protection Bureau](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/) says institutions use [those reports](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-is-a-bank-or-credit-union-account-screening-report-en-1113/) "to help determine whether to offer you a checking account and the type of checking account to offer you." The entry that does the damage is the one the CFPB describes, an account "closed by the bank or credit union" carrying "an unpaid negative balance ... that you have not repaid," so if a closure is coming, empty the account and settle what is owed before it lands. Most negative information can stay on file for up to seven years, and the CFPB notes that some of these companies disregard anything older than five.

## Ways around it

- **Batch the moves.** The count is per transaction, not per dollar. One transfer of $2,000 uses one slot; four transfers of $500 use four.
- **Check whether the teller and the ATM still escape.** If your agreement uses the old definition, an in-person or ATM withdrawal does not add to the count. If it reads like Truist's, that route is closed.
- **Keep the spending buffer where transactions are free** and treat savings as where money sits rather than where it passes through.
- **Ask.** Many banks will reverse a first fee if you ask, and the same bank often has another savings or money market product with a different limit.
- **Compare limits when you compare rates.** A quarter point of extra yield is worth less than $30 a year on a balance under $12,000.

The [Federal Reserve](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy.htm)'s current rule says withdrawals may be made regardless of number or manner. That sentence governs the Federal Reserve. It does not govern the bank holding your money.
