A brokerage account will show you certificates of deposit paying more than the bank down the street, and both will carry [FDIC](https://www.fdic.gov/resources/deposit-insurance) insurance. The rate comparison is real. The product comparison is not, because a brokered CD and a bank CD are different instruments wearing the same name.

The distinction matters most at the moment you want out early, which is exactly the moment nobody plans for.

## Who is holding the money

A bank CD is a contract between you and the bank. You opened it, your name is on it, and the bank knows you as the depositor.

A brokered CD is issued by a bank and sold through a brokerage. The brokerage holds it for you in a custodial arrangement, and the deposit is recorded at the issuing bank in the broker's name for the benefit of its customers. Insurance still runs to you as the underlying owner, but it depends on the broker's records being accurate and properly titled. That is normally routine. It is also one more link in the chain than a direct account has.

One practical consequence: if you already hold deposits at the issuing bank directly, a brokered CD from that same bank stacks against the same $250,000 limit. Brokerage platforms list dozens of issuers, and it is easy to buy two CDs without noticing they came from the same institution.

## Getting out early

This is the difference that surprises people.

A bank CD has an early withdrawal penalty written into the disclosure, some number of months of interest, known in advance. Break it and you know the cost before you act.

A brokered CD has no early withdrawal option at all. To exit before maturity, you sell it on the secondary market at whatever price a buyer will pay. If rates have risen since you bought, your CD pays below market and sells at a discount. There is no ceiling on that loss the way a penalty caps the cost of breaking a bank CD.

The direction of rates decides which structure treats you better:

| Situation | Bank CD | Brokered CD |
|---|---|---|
| Rates rose since purchase | Lose a set number of months of interest | Sell below face value; loss depends on term left |
| Rates fell since purchase | Same penalty applies | May sell above face value |
| Need only part of the money | Usually all or nothing | Can sell part of the position |

The bank CD gives you a known, capped cost. The brokered CD gives you a market price, which can be better or considerably worse.

## Interest that does not compound

Most brokered CDs pay simple interest, distributed to the brokerage account as cash. Nothing reinvests unless you do it.

Bank CDs commonly compound internally, so the interest earns interest for the remaining term. On a one-year CD the gap is small. On a five-year CD it is not, and comparing a brokered CD's stated rate against a bank CD's APY without adjusting for this compares two numbers that are not measuring the same thing.

Cash sitting in a brokerage sweep while you decide what to do with it is usually earning less than the CD paid. That drag belongs in the comparison too.

## Call features

Many brokered CDs are callable, meaning the issuing bank can redeem them early after a stated protection period. Banks call when rates have fallen, which is when your above-market CD was worth the most to you. You get your principal back and reinvest at the lower rates that caused the call.

Bank CDs sold to retail depositors are rarely callable. Brokered CDs frequently are, and the higher headline rate on a callable issue is compensation for handing that option to the bank. The listing will say callable and give the first call date; that line deserves a look before the rate does.

## Reading a brokered CD listing

A few fields carry most of the meaning:

- **Issuing bank** — check it against deposits you already hold elsewhere.
- **Call protection** — non-callable, or callable after a date.
- **Coupon frequency** — monthly, semiannual, or at maturity.
- **Simple or compounding** — usually simple.
- **Survivor option** — sometimes called an estate feature, it lets an estate redeem at par on death of the holder. Not universal.
- **Settlement date** — interest starts then, not on the day you place the order.

## When each one fits

A bank CD suits money that might be needed early, because the exit cost is knowable in advance. It also suits anyone who would rather not think about secondary market pricing.

A brokered CD suits money that is genuinely staying put, held alongside other investments, where the ability to spread deposits across many issuers from one account is worth something. Laddering across ten banks is an afternoon of paperwork at branches and a few minutes on a brokerage platform.

Neither is the better product in the abstract. The one that fits depends on whether the money is more likely to stay put or come back.

## How each one shows up at tax time

A bank CD produces a single Form 1099-INT from the bank, with interest in Box 1 and any early withdrawal penalty in Box 2.

A brokered CD produces interest reported by the brokerage on its consolidated statement, also as interest income. Selling one before maturity adds a second layer: the gain or loss on the sale is a capital transaction reported on Form 1099-B, not an interest adjustment. That means a loss on an early exit is a capital loss, subject to capital loss rules, rather than the above-the-line deduction that a bank CD's early withdrawal penalty produces.

For most people the difference is small. For someone with no other capital gains, a capital loss is deductible against ordinary income only up to a limited amount each year, with the rest carried forward. The bank CD's penalty gets used immediately.

Brokered CDs bought at a discount on the secondary market add market discount rules to the picture, which is a complication worth knowing about before buying seasoned issues rather than new ones.

## If the issuing bank fails

Insurance works in both cases, through different paperwork.

A direct depositor is on the bank's records and the FDIC pays from those records, usually within days.

A brokered CD holder is behind an omnibus account titled for the benefit of customers. The FDIC pays based on the broker's records of who owns what, which requires the broker to supply those records. This is a normal process and it works, but it is slower than a direct claim, and it depends entirely on the broker's recordkeeping being in order.

The FDIC has published guidance repeatedly on pass-through insurance for these arrangements, and the recurring theme is that the underlying records determine the outcome. A statement showing the issuing bank for each CD position is the document to keep.

## A short checklist before buying

1. Look up the issuing bank and compare it against every deposit you hold elsewhere.
2. Check callable or non-callable before comparing yields, since the two are not comparable.
3. Confirm whether interest is paid out or compounds, and where paid interest lands.
4. Note the settlement date, which is when interest starts.
5. Decide in advance whether this money can tolerate a secondary market sale, because that is the only exit.
