![Hurricane Isaias Tax Relief: The Feb. 2027 Deadlines and the $22,000 Withdrawal Catch](/assets/pin-images/2026-10-23-hurricane-isaias-tax-relief-the-feb-2027-deadlines-and-the-22000-withdrawal-catch.png)

On Oct. 14, the IRS announced tax relief for people and businesses hit by Hurricane Isaias in Florida and Alabama. In 27 Florida counties, many federal tax deadlines that fall on or after Oct. 9, 2026, and before Feb. 8, 2027, now run to Feb. 8. In 40 Alabama counties the window opens Oct. 7 and closes Feb. 4, 2027. The Florida release says affected taxpayers "now have until Feb. 8, 2027, to file various federal individual and business tax returns and make tax payments." The Alabama release uses the same words with its own date.

For households that keep their money in savings accounts, CDs and retirement accounts, the relief reaches past the tax return. The last 2026 estimated tax payment, normally due Jan. 15, moves into February. So do several retirement-account deadlines that run on fixed clocks, including the 60 days allowed to finish a rollover and the year-end deadline for required minimum distributions.

What the relief doesn't do by itself is make available a separate disaster benefit that both releases mention: a withdrawal of up to $22,000 from a 401(k), 403(b) or IRA that is exempt from the 10% early-withdrawal tax and can be repaid within three years. That rule applies only where the President has declared a major disaster. As of Oct. 19, the federal declarations for Isaias in Florida and Alabama were emergency declarations. The Alabama relief rests on the state's own declaration, which reaches 37 counties that FEMA's emergency declaration doesn't, under a power the IRS has had only since a 2025 law.

This article is based on the IRS releases for [Florida](https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/irs-announces-tax-relief-for-taxpayers-impacted-by-hurricane-isaias-in-florida-various-deadlines-postponed-to-feb-8-2027) (FL-2026-07) and [Alabama](https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/irs-announces-tax-relief-for-taxpayers-impacted-by-hurricane-isaias-in-alabama-various-deadlines-postponed-to-feb-4-2027) (AL-2026-01); [26 U.S.C. 7508A](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/7508A) and its regulation, [26 CFR 301.7508A-1](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/26/301.7508A-1); [Rev. Proc. 2018-58](https://www.irs.gov/irb/2018-50_IRB#RP-2018-58), the IRS list of acts a postponement can cover; the [Filing Relief for Natural Disasters Act](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/PLAW-119publ29/html/PLAW-119publ29.htm) and the [Disaster Related Extension of Deadlines Act](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/PLAW-119publ64/html/PLAW-119publ64.htm); the disaster-distribution rules in [26 U.S.C. 72(t)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/72) and the IRS [disaster relief FAQs for retirement plans and IRAs](https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/disaster-relief-frequent-asked-questions-retirement-plans-and-iras-under-the-secure-20-act-of-2022); the 401(k) hardship rules in [26 CFR 1.401(k)-1](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/26/1.401%28k%29-1); FEMA's [disaster declarations](https://www.fema.gov/disaster/declarations), checked through its OpenFEMA data on Oct. 19; and TreasuryDirect's page for [savings bond owners affected by a disaster](https://www.treasurydirect.gov/savings-bonds/cashing-a-bond/affected-by-a-disaster/).

## Who the relief covers

The Florida relief covers individuals and households that live or have a business in Baker, Bay, Calhoun, Citrus, Columbia, Dixie, Escambia, Franklin, Gadsden, Gilchrist, Gulf, Hamilton, Holmes, Jackson, Jefferson, Lafayette, Leon, Levy, Liberty, Madison, Okaloosa, Santa Rosa, Suwannee, Taylor, Wakulla, Walton and Washington counties.

The Alabama list is longer: Autauga, Baldwin, Barbour, Bibb, Bullock, Butler, Chambers, Chilton, Choctaw, Clarke, Coffee, Conecuh, Coosa, Covington, Crenshaw, Dale, Dallas, Elmore, Escambia, Geneva, Greene, Hale, Henry, Houston, Lee, Lowndes, Macon, Marengo, Mobile, Monroe, Montgomery, Perry, Pickens, Pike, Russell, Sumter, Tallapoosa, Tuscaloosa, Washington and Wilcox counties.

Both releases define "affected taxpayers" the same way: individuals who live in the covered area, businesses whose principal place of business is there, taxpayers whose records needed to meet a deadline are there, relief workers affiliated with a recognized government or philanthropic organization who are assisting in the area, and visitors killed or injured as a result of the storm.

Most people don't have to apply. "The IRS automatically identifies taxpayers located in the covered disaster area and applies filing and payment relief," both releases say. Affected taxpayers who live or have a business outside the covered area are told to call the IRS Special Services toll-free number, 866-562-5227. Anyone covered who receives a late-filing or late-payment penalty notice for a due date inside the postponement period "should call the telephone number on the notice to have the IRS abate the penalty."

## One storm, three kinds of declarations

How Isaias is treated for tax purposes depends on which government declared what. Section 7508A lets the IRS postpone deadlines for taxpayers affected by "a federally declared disaster (as defined by section 165(i)(5)(A))." That definition covers "any disaster subsequently determined by the President of the United States to warrant assistance by the Federal Government under the Robert T. Stafford Disaster Relief and Emergency Assistance Act." An emergency declaration meets it, and the Florida relief cites one: FEMA declaration 3655-EM.

Other parts of the tax code are written more narrowly. The penalty-free retirement withdrawal requires a major disaster declared under section 401 of the Stafford Act. An automatic 120-day postponement in Section 7508A(e), which applies without any IRS announcement, covers only a "disaster area," defined as "an area in which a major disaster for which the President provides financial assistance under section 408 of the Robert T. Stafford Disaster Relief and Emergency Assistance Act (42 U.S.C. 5174) occurs." Section 408 is the Stafford Act's program of assistance to individuals and households.

FEMA's records, checked on Oct. 19, showed the following:

| Area | What the IRS relief rests on | FEMA declaration | IRS deadline |
|---|---|---|---|
| 27 Florida counties | FEMA declaration 3655-EM | Emergency (EM-3655-FL), declared Oct. 9; public assistance only, no Individuals and Households Program | Feb. 8, 2027 |
| 40 Alabama counties | The State of Alabama's declaration, numbered AL-0017-DR in the IRS release | Emergency (EM-3656-AL), declared Oct. 9, for Baldwin, Escambia and Mobile counties and the Poarch Band of Creek Indians | Feb. 4, 2027 |
| 76 Georgia counties | No IRS announcement | Emergency (EM-3657-GA), declared Oct. 9 | None announced |
| Guam, Super Typhoon Bavi (for comparison) | FEMA declaration 4946-DR | Major disaster (DR-4946-GU), declared Oct. 7, with the Individuals and Households Program | Feb. 4, 2027 |

Where the IRS has announced relief, the postponement works the same way. The declaration type matters for other rules: whether the $22,000 withdrawal is available, which 401(k) hardship reasons apply, and whether a release includes a casualty-loss paragraph.

## What a postponement does and doesn't do

A Section 7508A postponement doesn't extend every deadline. It sets a window, and acts that fall due inside it can be done as late as its last day. The regulation puts it this way: "When an affected taxpayer is required to perform a tax-related act by a due date that falls within the postponement period, the affected taxpayer is eligible for postponement of time to perform the act until the last day of the period. The affected taxpayer is eligible for relief from interest, penalties, additional amounts, or additions to tax during the postponement period."

Three limits in the releases matter for households.

**Payments that were due before the window stay late.** The releases say the February deadline "applies to individuals who had a valid extension to file their 2025 individual income tax return," then add: "Because tax payments related to these 2025 returns were due on April 15, 2026, those payments are not eligible for this relief." A household on extension can file its 2025 return as late as Feb. 8 in Florida or Feb. 4 in Alabama, but tax it still owes for 2025 has been accruing interest and penalties since April 15, and the relief doesn't stop that.

**Existing payment plans keep running.** "For taxpayers in an installment agreement or payment plan, reminder notices will continue to be issued, and direct debits will also continue. Missed payments during the postponement period will not cause an installment agreement to default." The releases add: "However, the usual interest and late-payment penalty charges will continue to accrue during this period." Anyone who wants a debit from a checking or savings account stopped is told to "contact the IRS or their bank for assistance."

**Information returns get no extra time.** "Unless an act is specifically listed in Rev. Proc. 2018-58, the postponement of time to file and pay does not apply to information returns in the W-2, 1094, 1095, 1097, 1098 or 1099 series; to Forms 1042-S, 3921, 3922 or 8027; or to employment and excise tax deposits." Banks and credit unions in the covered counties get no extra time under this relief for the Forms 1099-INT that report 2026 interest to depositors.

## Estimated tax on savings interest moves to February

Interest from savings accounts, CDs and Treasury bills usually arrives with no tax withheld, so savers with a lot of it often make quarterly estimated payments. The tests that decide who owes them are in our guide to [savings interest and the Jan. 15, 2027 estimated tax](/2026/10/07/savings-interest-and-the-jan-15-2027-estimated-tax-the-1000-test/).

The Florida release changes the last payment of the year: "Estimated income tax payments originally due on or after Oct. 9, 2026, are postponed through Feb. 8, 2027, and affected taxpayers will not be subject to penalties for failure to pay estimated tax installments as long as such payments are paid on or before Feb. 8, 2027." Alabama's says the same with Oct. 7 and Feb. 4.

The fourth installment for 2026, due Jan. 15, 2027, falls inside both windows. The third, due Sept. 15, 2026, came before either window opened. Shortfalls in the April, June and September payments are outside this relief.

**A worked example.** A retired couple in Bay County, Florida, pays estimated tax on interest from CDs and a high-yield savings account. Their plan was to send $2,000 on Jan. 15. Under the release, a $2,000 payment made by Feb. 8 counts as on time for the fourth installment. Suppose their Sept. 15 payment had been $500 short. That shortfall is handled under the normal rules, because Sept. 15 came before Oct. 9. Paying on Jan. 15 is still allowed; the postponement only adds time.

The April 15, 2027 deadline to file and pay 2026 income tax falls after Feb. 8, outside the window, so it doesn't move. Both releases add that the IRS "may provide additional disaster relief in the future."

## Retirement deadlines that move

Affected taxpayers also get until the February date "to perform other time-sensitive actions described in Treas. Reg. § 301.7508A-1(c)(1) and Rev. Proc. 2018-58" when those acts come due inside the window. That revenue procedure is the IRS's master list. It "does not, by itself, provide any postponements," but it says that unless the guidance for a particular disaster limits the relief, "the guidance will generally postpone all of the acts listed in the regulations and this revenue procedure." Apart from the information-return and deposit exclusions above, neither release limits it.

**60-day rollovers.** Rev. Proc. 2018-58 lists the rule that "An eligible rollover distribution may be rolled over to an eligible retirement plan, including an IRA, no later than the 60th day following the day the distributee received the distributed property." The regulation names IRA and plan rollovers too. If the 60th day lands inside the window, the deadline becomes Feb. 8 in Florida or Feb. 4 in Alabama.

Take a Leon County, Florida, resident who withdrew $15,000 from an IRA on Sept. 1, 2026, planning to deposit it in another IRA. The 60th day after Sept. 1 is Oct. 31, inside Florida's window, so the rollover is on time if it is completed by Feb. 8, 2027. Had the money come out on Aug. 1, the 60th day would have been Sept. 30, before the window opened, and this relief wouldn't help. The postponement moves the deadline and nothing else; [Publication 590-A](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p590a)'s limit of "one rollover within a 1-year period" between traditional IRAs still applies.

**HSA, ABLE, 529 and Coverdell rollovers.** The same list includes the 60-day rollover deadlines for health savings accounts, ABLE accounts, 529 plans and Coverdell education savings accounts. For the ABLE rules themselves, see [ABLE accounts in 2026](/2026/10/17/able-accounts-in-2026-age-46-a-20000-limit-and-ssis-100000-rule/).

**Excess IRA contributions and recharacterizations.** Publication 590-A says that "if the excess contributions for a year aren't withdrawn by the date your return for the year is due (including extensions), you are subject to a 6% tax." Someone who "timely filed your 2025 tax return without withdrawing a contribution" can still have it returned "within 6 months of the due date of your 2025 tax return, excluding extensions." For a calendar-year taxpayer who had a valid filing extension, or who filed on time and could use the six-month period, the usual deadline to withdraw an excess 2025 contribution was Oct. 15, 2026, before any disaster postponement. The deadline to recharacterize a 2025 contribution from a Roth to a traditional IRA, or the reverse, is tied to the same extended due date. Oct. 15 falls inside both windows, and the regulation lists "making distributions under section 408(d)(4)" and "recharacterizing contributions under section 408A(d)(6)" among the acts it covers.

**Required minimum distributions.** [Publication 590-B](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p590b) says: "The required minimum distribution for any year after the year you reach age 73 must be made by December 31 of that later year." Rev. Proc. 2018-58 includes that deadline among postponable acts, describing the rule that "subsequent required minimum distributions must be made by the end of each distribution calendar year," and neither release carves it out. Dec. 31, 2026 falls inside both windows. (A first RMD has a later deadline: "by April 1 of the year following the year you reach the age 73.") Anyone relying on the postponement should first ask the IRA custodian how it will process and report a payment received in early 2027 to satisfy the postponed 2026 RMD.

**Plan loan payments.** The list also includes the repayment schedule for 401(k) and similar plan loans. How a plan handles payroll-deducted payments during the window is a question for the plan administrator.

IRA contributions don't move. Publication 590-A says contributions for a year are due "by the due date for filing your return for that year, not including extensions." For 2026 contributions, that is April 15, 2027, after both windows close.

## The $22,000 withdrawal requires a major disaster

The Florida, Alabama and [Guam](https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/irs-announces-tax-relief-for-taxpayers-impacted-by-super-typhoon-bavi-in-guam-various-deadlines-postponed-to-feb-4-2027) releases contain the same paragraph on retirement accounts. It says, in part, that "a taxpayer may be eligible to take a special disaster distribution that would not be subject to the additional 10% early distribution tax and that the taxpayer may take into income over three years."

That special distribution is the qualified disaster recovery distribution, created by the SECURE 2.0 Act, which was enacted Dec. 29, 2022. The statute's definition of a qualifying disaster is specific: "the term 'qualified disaster' means any disaster with respect to which a major disaster has been declared by the President under section 401 of the Robert T. Stafford Disaster Relief and Emergency Assistance Act after December 27, 2020."

The IRS's disaster FAQs say the same. They tell readers to use FEMA's search tool, "filtering for declaration type: Major Disaster Declaration." They warn that "a disaster may initially be declared to be an 'emergency' but may subsequently be declared to be a 'major disaster.'" And they conclude: "The relief provided under SECURE 2.0 only applies in the case of declared major disasters." The IRS's table of exceptions to the 10% tax is looser, describing losses "by reason of a federally declared disaster where they live." The statute and the FAQs set the narrower test.

As of Oct. 19, the only federal declarations for Isaias in Florida and Alabama were emergency declarations, and a state declaration isn't a presidential major disaster declaration either. For now, a retirement withdrawal by someone in those counties gets the ordinary treatment. The [IRS states the general rule](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/plan-participant-employee/retirement-topics-exceptions-to-tax-on-early-distributions) for amounts withdrawn before age 59½: "Individuals must pay an additional 10% early withdrawal tax unless an exception applies."

If a major disaster is declared later, these rules would apply:

- **Amount.** Up to $22,000 per disaster, counting all of a person's plans and IRAs together.
- **Who.** A person whose principal residence was in the declared area at any time during the incident period and who "has sustained an economic loss by reason of" the disaster. The FAQs' examples include property damage, displacement from the home and "Loss of livelihood due to temporary or permanent layoffs."
- **When.** On or after the first day of the incident period and before the date that is 180 days after the latest of Dec. 29, 2022, the first day of the incident period, or the date of the declaration.
- **Tax.** No 10% additional tax. The income is spread evenly over three years unless the taxpayer elects on Form 8915-F to report it all in the year of the distribution.
- **Repayment.** The money can be put back in an eligible plan or IRA within three years, beginning the day after it was received, and is then treated as a direct trustee-to-trustee transfer.
- **Plan loans.** An employer may raise the loan limit to the full vested balance, "but not more than $100,000 (minus outstanding plan loans of the individual)," and may allow up to an extra year to repay certain loans.

Employers aren't required to offer these distributions or loan changes. The FAQs add: "Even if an employer does not treat a distribution as a qualified disaster recovery distribution, an individual may still treat a distribution as such on their federal income tax return if they are a qualified individual and the distribution meets the requirements to be a qualified disaster recovery distribution."

Because the window runs from the first day of the incident period, a distribution taken before a major disaster declaration could later qualify, if the declaration's incident period covers it and the person meets the other conditions. A major disaster declaration would set its own incident period, though, and nothing in FEMA's current records says whether one will be issued.

## Hardship withdrawals and the $1,000 emergency withdrawal

The releases add that "Taxpayers may also be eligible to make a hardship withdrawal." That depends on the plan: "Although not required, a retirement plan may allow participants to receive hardship distributions," the [IRS's hardship page](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/plan-participant-employee/retirement-topics-hardship-distributions) says.

The 401(k) regulation lists seven expenses that count as an immediate and heavy financial need. Two concern disasters, and the declaration type decides which one fits. Item 7 covers "Expenses and losses (including loss of income) incurred by the employee on account of a disaster declared by the Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA) under the Robert T. Stafford Disaster Relief and Emergency Assistance Act," but only "provided that the employee's principal residence or principal place of employment at the time of the disaster was located in an area designated by FEMA for individual assistance with respect to the disaster." FEMA's records for the Isaias emergency declarations show no individual assistance.

Item 6 doesn't depend on any declaration: "Expenses for the repair of damage to the employee's principal residence that would qualify for the casualty deduction under section 165 (determined without regard to section 165(h)(5) and whether the loss exceeds 10% of adjusted gross income)." Section 165(h)(5) is the provision that limits personal casualty deductions to losses from declared disasters, so item 6 can cover storm repairs to a home with or without a declaration, in a plan that uses the list.

The tax rules for hardship withdrawals are stricter. The IRS says: "Hardship distributions are subject to income taxes (unless they consist of Roth contributions). They may also be subject to a 10% additional tax on early distributions." They can't be repaid to the plan or rolled over.

A smaller option doesn't depend on any declaration. The emergency personal expense distribution, described in [IRS Notice 2024-55](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-drop/n-24-55.pdf), allows one distribution a calendar year without the 10% additional tax, capped at the lesser of $1,000 or the vested balance above $1,000, for "unforeseeable or immediate financial needs relating to necessary personal or family emergency expenses." The notice's list of factors includes "accident or loss of property due to casualty." Plans don't have to offer it, and it is still taxable. Repayment and the limits on taking a second one are explained in our post on [the $1,000 emergency 401(k) withdrawal](/2026/10/10/the-1000-emergency-401k-withdrawal-vs-your-savings-account-the-rules/).

## Savings bonds, CDs and bank accounts

Savings bonds have their own disaster rule, from the Treasury rather than the IRS. TreasuryDirect's page says: "If you live in an area that has had an official disaster declaration, we are sorry that you have been affected. You can find out which areas have been declared official disaster areas by visiting fema.gov." Owners can then cash bonds "Even if they are lost, illegible, or contaminated" and "Even if they are less than a year old," the page says, adding "(Normally, bonds can't be cashed in their first year.)"

Whether an emergency declaration is enough, or only a major disaster, isn't addressed. Owners of electronic bonds less than a year old are told to "Call us at 844-284-2676 and briefly explain the circumstances" or to send a certified FS Form 5512 marked "DISASTER." Lost or damaged paper bonds go through FS Form 1048, marked the same way, and for owners who want cash, the page says, "we will waive the requirement to hold the bonds for a year." The page is silent on a separate I bond cost: cashing before five years gives up the last three months of interest, as our comparison of [I bonds and high-yield savings](/2026/09/24/i-bonds-vs-a-high-yield-savings-account-the-12-month-lock/) shows.

A CD's early withdrawal penalty comes from the account agreement and its required disclosure, as our post on [CD early withdrawal penalties](/2026/09/22/cd-early-withdrawal-penalties-the-disclosure-and-the-deduction/) describes. None of the IRS, FEMA or Treasury material discussed here addresses CD penalties or requires a bank to waive one.

After Super Typhoon Bavi in the Northern Mariana Islands, the [FDIC's Aug. 28 letter](https://www.fdic.gov/news/financial-institution-letters/2026/supervisory-relief-help-financial-institutions-and-12), FIL-54-2026, encouraged institutions "to work constructively with customers in affected areas to meet their financial services needs." [The FDIC's list of financial institution letters](https://www.fdic.gov/news/financial-institution-letters/2026) showed none for Isaias as of Oct. 19. A penalty waiver after the storm is the bank's call.

## Alabama's relief rests on the state's declaration

FEMA's emergency declaration for Alabama names three counties and the Poarch Band of Creek Indians, while the IRS relief covers 40 counties. The difference comes from the Filing Relief for Natural Disasters Act, Public Law 119-29, approved July 24, 2025. It added Section 7508A(c), under which the Secretary, after consulting FEMA, "may, upon the written request of the Governor of a State ... apply the rules of subsections (a) and (b) to a qualified State declared disaster." The law applies to declarations made after July 24, 2025.

The IRS's [FAQs for disaster victims](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/faqs-for-disaster-victims) describe the effect: "Affected taxpayers may be eligible for the same filing and payment extensions provided in federally declared disasters." Subsections (a) and (b) are the postponement rules, including the one for retirement plans. They don't turn a state declaration into a federal major disaster, so the $22,000 distribution, item 7 of the hardship list and the automatic 120-day postponement still depend on federal declarations.

Two other details separate the Alabama release from Florida's.

**No casualty-loss paragraph.** The Florida and Guam releases say affected taxpayers "have the option of claiming disaster-related casualty losses on their federal income tax return for either the year in which the event occurred, or the prior year," and give the FEMA number to write on the return. The Alabama release has no such paragraph. The prior-year option in Section 165(i) is written for federally declared disasters, and the release doesn't address how losses in Alabama's state-declared counties will be treated.

**A date that doesn't match.** One sentence in the Alabama release reads: "The Feb. 7, 2027, deadline applies to individuals who had a valid extension to file their 2025 individual income tax return." The headline and every other date in the release say Feb. 4, 2027. Filing an extended 2025 return by Feb. 4 satisfies both readings.

## Where no relief has been announced

FEMA's emergency declaration for Georgia, EM-3657, covers 76 counties. As of Oct. 19, the IRS's list of disaster relief announcements had no Isaias entry for Georgia, so there is no postponement based on Georgia's declaration. A Georgia resident can still qualify under the Florida or Alabama relief by meeting the affected-taxpayer rules, for example if the records needed to meet a deadline are kept in a covered county.

The releases note that taxpayers "who do not qualify for disaster tax relief may qualify for reasonable cause penalty abatement." For a missed 60-day rollover, Publication 590-A says "The IRS may waive the 60-day requirement where the failure to do so would be against equity or good conscience, such as in the event of a casualty, a disaster, or another event beyond your reasonable control."

A ruling isn't the only route. Under [Rev. Proc. 2020-46](https://www.irs.gov/irb/2020-45_IRB#REV-PROC-2020-46), a taxpayer can certify in writing to the IRA trustee or plan administrator that the deadline was missed for a listed reason, one of which is that "the taxpayer's principal residence was severely damaged." The deposit must be made "as soon as practicable," which the procedure treats as met "if the contribution is made within 30 days after the reason or reasons no longer prevent the taxpayer from making the contribution." Publication 590-A adds: "A self-certification is not a waiver by the IRS of the 60-day rollover requirement."

The FBAR, the foreign account report filed with FinCEN rather than the IRS, had an extended due date of Oct. 15. The releases say "the government may further extend the due date for filing the Report of Foreign Bank and Financial Accounts (FBAR)." As of Oct. 19, the newest disaster notices on [FinCEN's FBAR page](https://www.fincen.gov/report-foreign-bank-and-financial-accounts) dated from 2024, and none mentioned Isaias. The filing rules are in our post on [foreign savings accounts and the Oct. 15 FBAR deadline](/2026/10/14/foreign-savings-accounts-and-the-oct-15-fbar-deadline-the-10000-test/).

## Refund claims and lost records

The Disaster Related Extension of Deadlines Act, Public Law 119-64, approved Dec. 26, 2025, added Section 7508A(f): "For purposes of section 6511(b)(2)(A), any period disregarded under this section with respect to the time prescribed for filing any return of tax shall be treated as an extension of time for filing such return." Section 6511(b)(2)(A) is the lookback rule that limits how much tax a refund claim can recover, and the change applies to claims filed after Dec. 26, 2025. In the IRS FAQs' words: "If you have additional time to file and pay due to a federally declared disaster, this additional time will also extend the time you have to claim a refund."

For records lost in the storm, "The IRS will waive the usual fees for requests for copies of previously filed tax returns for affected taxpayers." The releases say to write the declaration number in bold letters at the top of [Form 4506](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/f4506.pdf) or Form 4506-T: 3655-EM for Florida and AL-0017-DR for Alabama. The April 2025 revision of Form 4506 says "There is a $30 fee for each return requested," and the IRS FAQs say expedited transcripts are free through the disaster hotline, 866-562-5227.

## Common questions

**Can I take up to $22,000 from my 401(k) or IRA without the 10% tax because of Isaias?**

Not under the declarations in place on Oct. 19. The qualified disaster recovery distribution requires a major disaster declaration, and the IRS FAQs say the SECURE 2.0 relief "only applies in the case of declared major disasters." FEMA's Isaias declarations for Florida and Alabama were emergency declarations.

**My 60-day rollover deadline falls in November. Do I have more time?**

If you are an affected taxpayer in one of the covered counties, yes: through Feb. 8, 2027 in Florida or Feb. 4, 2027 in Alabama. Rev. Proc. 2018-58 lists 60-day rollovers among the acts the releases postpone, and the IRS regulation on postponements, 26 CFR 301.7508A-1, names them directly.

**I'm in Georgia. Do these dates apply to me?**

Not on the strength of Georgia's own declaration, as of Oct. 19: the IRS hadn't announced a postponement for the 76 counties in FEMA's emergency declaration. A Georgia resident who meets the Florida or Alabama affected-taxpayer rules, such as keeping the needed records in a covered county, is covered by that relief.

For people in the covered counties, most of this requires no paperwork: the IRS identifies taxpayers in the area on its own, and deadlines inside the window run to February without a request. The $22,000 exception doesn't come with the postponement. As of Oct. 19, Isaias had emergency declarations in Florida and Alabama rather than a major disaster declaration, and the IRS FAQs point to FEMA's declaration search as the place to check for a change.
