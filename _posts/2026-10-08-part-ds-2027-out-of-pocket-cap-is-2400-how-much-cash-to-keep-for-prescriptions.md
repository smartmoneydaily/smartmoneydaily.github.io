![Part D's 2027 Out-of-Pocket Cap Is $2,400: How Much Cash to Keep for Prescriptions](/assets/pin-images/2026-10-08-part-ds-2027-out-of-pocket-cap-is-2400-how-much-cash-to-keep-for-prescriptions.png)

A retiree with a brand-name prescription, a monthly Social Security check and a few thousand dollars in savings is about to pick a drug plan for 2027 and wants to know one number: how much cash to keep on hand for drug costs next year. For covered Part D drugs, the ceiling is $2,400 per person in 2027, up from $2,100 in 2026. Once your out-of-pocket spending on covered Part D drugs reaches that amount, you pay nothing more for covered Part D drugs for the rest of the calendar year. So a reserve of $2,400 per enrolled person covers the worst case for covered drugs, and a married couple where both spouses could hit their own cap would need twice that. Premiums and drugs your plan doesn't cover sit outside that figure. The harder question is timing: a plan can have a deductible of up to $700 in 2027, and you pay it first, as you fill prescriptions, so for someone with costly prescriptions all of it can come due in January.

The figures come from two federal sources. The Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services published the 2027 Part D parameters in its [Announcement of Calendar Year (CY) 2027 Medicare Advantage (MA) Capitation Rates and Part C and Part D Payment Policies](https://www.cms.gov/files/document/2027-announcement.pdf), and Medicare.gov's [Costs for Medicare drug coverage](https://www.medicare.gov/drug-coverage-part-d/costs-for-medicare-drug-coverage) page already lists the 2027 deductible and out-of-pocket amounts next to the 2026 ones. The choices that set your 2027 costs are made during [Open Enrollment](https://www.medicare.gov/basics/get-started-with-medicare/get-more-coverage/joining-a-plan), which runs Oct. 15 to Dec. 7. This is general information about how the rules work, not advice about any particular plan or household.

## The 2027 Part D numbers CMS has already set

CMS updates the defined standard Part D benefit each year, and the 2027 figures are final. Table V-2 in Attachment V of the announcement lists both years side by side.

| Standard Part D benefit | 2026 | 2027 | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| Maximum deductible | $615 | $700 | +$85 |
| Out-of-pocket threshold for covered Part D drugs | $2,100 | $2,400 | +$300 |
| Your share in the initial coverage stage | 25% | 25% | No change |
| What you pay after reaching the threshold | $0 for covered Part D drugs | $0 for covered Part D drugs | No change |

Medicare.gov's wording on the deductible is precise: "No Medicare drug plan may have a deductible more than $700 in 2027 ($615 in 2026), and some plans have no deductible." The $700 is a maximum; plans can set a lower deductible or none. The out-of-pocket threshold is a single number: $2,400 in 2027.

The increases follow a fixed formula. The announcement says the 2027 deductible is updated "by multiplying the 2026 amount of $615 by the 2027 API and rounding to the nearest multiple of $5," and the out-of-pocket threshold is updated by multiplying the 2026 amount of $2,100 by the same factor "and rounding to the nearest multiple of $50." API stands for the annual percentage increase in average per capita aggregate expenditures for covered Part D drugs, as the announcement defines it, and Table V-1 puts the 2027 API at 13.65%.

You can check the result yourself:

- Deductible: $615 × 1.1365 = about $698.95, which rounds to the nearest $5 as $700.
- Out-of-pocket threshold: $2,100 × 1.1365 = about $2,386.65, which rounds to the nearest $50 as $2,400.

CMS notes that its percentages are carried to more decimal places than the tables show, so a hand calculation may be off by a few cents, but the rounded results match. For savings planning, a reserve sized to the 2026 cap is now $300 short per person.

## How the three drug coverage stages work in 2027

Medicare.gov says Medicare drug plans and Medicare Advantage Plans with drug coverage generally have three stages. The stages run on the calendar year, so knowing where the money goes in each one is what turns the $2,400 figure into a cash plan.

**Deductible stage.** "If your Medicare plan has a deductible, you pay all out-of-pocket costs until you reach the full deductible." In 2027 that can be as much as $700. CMS's Table V-6 shows cost sharing of 100% in this phase of the standard benefit. If your plan has no deductible, you skip this stage.

**Initial coverage stage.** After the deductible, "you'll pay 25% of the cost as coinsurance for your generic and brand-name drugs until your out-of-pocket spending on covered Part D drugs reaches $2,100 in 2026 ($2,400 in 2027)." Medicare's page adds that this spending includes "certain payments made on your behalf, like through the Extra Help program." Then, in the page's words, "you'll automatically get 'catastrophic coverage.'"

**Catastrophic coverage stage.** "You won't have to pay out-of-pocket for covered Part D drugs for the rest of the calendar year."

Two details shape the cash flow. The deductible is the first part of the $2,400, not an extra charge on top of it. And the 25% share is the defined standard benefit; Medicare.gov says that, depending on the plan, you could pay a copayment (a fixed amount) or coinsurance (a percentage), so your plan's charges may look different. The $2,400 ceiling applies either way.

How much in total drug costs does it take to reach the cap under the standard benefit? You pay the first $700 in full. The remaining $1,700 of out-of-pocket spending ($2,400 − $700) comes at 25%, which takes $1,700 ÷ 0.25 = $6,800 in further drug costs, for $7,500 in total. That is our calculation from the published parameters, not a CMS figure, and a plan with copays or a lower deductible reaches the cap on a different path.

Your plan keeps the running count. Medicare.gov says the Explanation of Benefits you get the month after the pharmacy bills your plan shows "your coverage stage, and what counts toward your out-of-pocket costs and your total drug costs."

## What the $2,400 cap covers and what it leaves out

The $2,400 limit is narrower than "everything I spend on prescriptions." Medicare.gov ties it to "out-of-pocket spending on covered Part D drugs."

| Cost | Counts toward the 2027 $2,400 cap? | What it means for the reserve |
|---|---|---|
| Deductible payments on covered Part D drugs | Yes | Part of the $2,400, and it comes first in the year |
| Your 25% coinsurance or copays on covered Part D drugs | Yes | Fills the rest of the $2,400 |
| Certain payments made on your behalf, such as through Extra Help | Yes, per Medicare.gov | The cap can be reached with less of your own cash |
| Monthly plan premium | No, it is a separate charge | Budget it in addition to the $2,400 |
| Drugs your plan doesn't cover | Not under the cap, which applies to covered Part D drugs | Planned and paid outside the $2,400 |

**Premiums.** Medicare.gov defines the premium as "a monthly amount you pay for coverage, whether you get covered drugs or not." It depends on the plan you choose, and "you may have to pay more, depending on your income." 2027 plan premiums and income-related amounts were not on the pages reviewed for this article, so budget with the premium your chosen plan shows, times 12.

**Drugs your plan doesn't cover.** The cap applies to covered Part D drugs, so checking that each of your drugs is covered matters for cash planning as much as for health care.

**Help from other programs.** Medicare's page says contributions from State Pharmaceutical Assistance Programs "may count toward your Medicare drug coverage out-of-pocket limit," depending on each program's rules. If you get such help, ask the program and your plan how it is counted.

One premium-side cost deserves a warning. Medicare.gov says the Part D late enrollment penalty is figured by multiplying 1% times the "national base beneficiary premium" ($38.99 in 2026) times the number of full, uncovered months you were eligible for drug coverage but went without it and without other creditable coverage. For a 14-month gap at the 2026 base, that is 0.14 × $38.99 = about $5.46, which Medicare rounds to the nearest 10 cents, or $5.50 a month added to the premium. Dropping coverage to save on premiums can cost more than it saves.

## Worked example: a brand-name prescription and the January hit

Take a single person enrolled in a 2027 plan that follows the standard benefit: a $700 deductible, then 25% coinsurance until out-of-pocket spending reaches $2,400. Assume one covered brand-name drug with a full cost of $1,000 per monthly fill. The $1,000 is a round number chosen for the arithmetic, not the price of any real drug or plan.

**January.** The first $700 is the deductible, paid in full. The other $300 of the fill is in the initial coverage stage at 25%: $300 × 0.25 = $75. January out of pocket: $700 + $75 = $775.

**February through July.** Each fill costs 25% of $1,000 = $250. Six months at $250 adds $1,500, bringing the running total to $775 + $1,500 = $2,275 by the end of July.

**August.** Only $2,400 − $2,275 = $125 of room is left under the cap. At 25%, that is used up by the first $500 of the August fill ($125 ÷ 0.25). The person pays $125.

**September through December.** Catastrophic coverage applies, so the person pays $0 for covered Part D drugs.

| Month | Paid at pharmacy | Running total toward $2,400 |
|---|---|---|
| January | $775 | $775 |
| February | $250 | $1,025 |
| March | $250 | $1,275 |
| April | $250 | $1,525 |
| May | $250 | $1,775 |
| June | $250 | $2,025 |
| July | $250 | $2,275 |
| August | $125 | $2,400 |
| September to December | $0 | $2,400 |

Total for the year: $775 + (6 × $250) + $125 = $2,400, exactly the cap.

The lesson is in the January row: $775 of the $2,400, nearly a third, lands in the first month. Someone who budgeted "$200 a month for prescriptions" would be $575 short in January, and the shortfall would keep growing to $875 by the end of July ($2,275 paid against $1,400 budgeted) before the monthly budget catches up in December. A reserve in savings absorbs that front-loading. For comparison, under the 2026 figures the same January would have cost $615 + (25% × $385) = $711.25.

## Worked example: a lower-cost prescription and a $975 year

The cap matters less for someone whose drug costs are modest, but the deductible still shapes the first months. Take the same standard-benefit plan and a covered drug with a full cost of $150 per monthly fill, again a round number for illustration.

- January through April: each fill is inside the $700 deductible, so the person pays the full $150. After four months: 4 × $150 = $600.
- May: $100 of deductible remains ($700 − $600). The person pays $100, plus 25% of the other $50 ($12.50), for $112.50.
- June through December: 25% of $150 = $37.50 a month for seven months, or 7 × $37.50 = $262.50.

Year total: $600 + $112.50 + $262.50 = $975. As a check, the full cost of the drug for the year is 12 × $150 = $1,800, and the standard benefit gives $700 + 25% × ($1,800 − $700) = $700 + $275 = $975.

This person never gets near $2,400, so a $2,400 reserve would overshoot. Still, $712.50 of the $975, about 73%, is paid by the end of May. In a plan with a deductible, the cash need is heaviest early in the year, and a plan with a lower deductible or none would change both the timing and the total.

## Sizing the reserve for one person or a couple

Three numbers set the outer bounds of a 2027 drug-cost reserve:

1. **The deductible**, up to $700, which is the most you could pay before the plan shares costs and the first amount you pay each year.
2. **The out-of-pocket cap**, $2,400, which is the most you could pay for covered Part D drugs in the year, deductible included.
3. **Twelve months of premiums**, which depend on the plan and are paid whether or not you fill a prescription.

| Household and drug profile | Covered-drug amount to plan for in 2027 | Plus |
|---|---|---|
| One person, no regular prescriptions, plan with a deductible | The deductible, up to $700, comes first; a costly new prescription can push the total toward $2,400 | 12 months of premiums |
| One person, steady modest prescriptions | Your own estimate from plan tools, at most $2,400 | 12 months of premiums |
| One person, high-cost prescription | $2,400 | 12 months of premiums |
| Married couple, both with high-cost prescriptions | $2,400 + $2,400 = $4,800 (our calculation) | 12 months of premiums for each plan |

The couple's figure needs care. Medicare.gov ties the $2,400 to your own out-of-pocket spending, and the pages reviewed for this article do not describe a household cap. The $4,800 is the per-person cap applied twice, not an official CMS number, and a couple where only one spouse takes expensive drugs would plan for one cap plus the other spouse's expected drug costs.

**The cap is an upper limit.** Holding $2,400 for the person in the second example ties up $1,425 more than their covered drugs cost ($2,400 − $975). Some households keep the full cap as a buffer against a new diagnosis; others hold their estimate plus a margin. Because the deductible and cap run on the calendar year, the need also returns each January for anyone on long-term medications.

**Premiums need their own line.** Medicare.gov lists four ways to pay: automatic deduction from checking or savings, a credit or debit card ("fees may apply"), a monthly bill, or withholding from Social Security. Withholding "may take up to 3 months to start," and if withholding starts a month or two after you join a plan, you get a bill for the months that weren't withheld, so leave room for catch-up payments when you join a new plan.

For people with limited income and savings, Extra Help and the Medicare Savings Programs change the arithmetic; the Medicare Savings Programs resource limit is covered in [Medicare Savings Programs: The $9,950 Resource Limit and What Counts](/2026/10/06/medicare-savings-programs-the-9950-resource-limit-and-what-counts/). On the drug side alone, CMS's Table V-2 shows that for 2027, Extra Help enrollees in the group CMS calls category code 1 (non-institutionalized full-benefit dual eligible beneficiaries with incomes between 100% and 150% of the federal poverty level, plus full-subsidy beneficiaries who are not full-benefit dual eligibles) have a $0 deductible, and until they reach the out-of-pocket threshold their maximum copayments are $5.80 for a generic or preferred multi-source drug and $14.40 for other drugs.

## How the Medicare Prescription Payment Plan spreads the bill

The [Medicare Prescription Payment Plan](https://www.medicare.gov/prescription-payment-plan) is the main alternative to paying out of a savings reserve at the pharmacy counter. It "works with your current drug coverage to help you manage your out-of-pocket costs for drugs covered by your plan by spreading them across the calendar year (January – December)." Medicare.gov says, "All plans offer this payment option, and participation is voluntary."

How it changes the cash flow:

- You don't pay the pharmacy for covered Part D prescriptions. Instead, "you'll get a bill from your health or drug plan to pay for your prescription drugs."
- You still pay your plan premium (if you have one) each month, and Medicare's [Using the Medicare Prescription Payment Plan](https://www.medicare.gov/prescription-payment-plan/using-payment-option) page says it comes as a separate bill.
- "There's no cost to participate," and if a payment is late, "you won't pay any interest or fees."
- It does not reduce what you owe. Medicare.gov is direct: "it doesn't save you money or lower your drug costs."

The billing formula is the same at every plan. According to Medicare's [Before you use the Medicare Prescription Payment Plan](https://www.medicare.gov/prescription-payment-plan/before-payment-option) page, "Your monthly bill is based on what you would have paid for any prescriptions you get, plus your previous month's balance, divided by the number of months left in the year. All plans use the same formula to calculate your monthly payments."

The first month works differently. Medicare's [Examples of this payment option](https://www.medicare.gov/prescription-payment-plan/examples) page explains that the plan first works out a "maximum possible payment" for that month: the annual out-of-pocket maximum, minus any out-of-pocket costs you had before joining, divided by the months left in the year. It then compares that figure with your actual costs for the month, and in Medicare's words, "Your plan will bill you the lesser of the two amounts." Whatever is not billed carries into the next month's balance, and from the second month on, the formula above applies.

The "Before you use" page also sets a ceiling. In a calendar year, you'll never pay more than what you would have paid the pharmacy without the program, or the out-of-pocket maximum for covered drugs. That page states the maximum as $2,100 for 2026. For 2027, Medicare.gov's costs page puts the out-of-pocket threshold for covered Part D drugs at $2,400.

**The full-cap January case.** Suppose a person enrolled from January has prescriptions that bring their out-of-pocket costs to the full $2,400 in January alone. The first month's maximum possible payment is $2,400 ÷ 12 = $200, which is less than the $2,400 in costs, so the January bill is $200. The remaining $2,200 is spread over the 11 months left, or $200 a month from February through December, with nothing added later. That is our calculation using Medicare's stated method and the 2027 cap, and it shows the program's main use: $200 a month instead of $2,400 in cash in January.

That is also the program's limit. Spreading the bill is only possible over the months that remain. Medicare's page says you can start "at any time in the year," but "starting earlier in the year (like before September), gives you more months to spread out your drug costs."

## Worked example: payment plan bills month by month

Go back to the $1,000-a-month drug: pharmacy costs of $775 in January, $250 a month from February to July and $125 in August. Suppose the person enrolls in the payment option effective January. Applying Medicare's method with the 2027 cap:

- January: the maximum possible payment is $2,400 ÷ 12 = $200, which is less than the $775 in costs, so the bill is $200. Balance carried: $775 − $200 = $575.
- February: ($575 + $250) ÷ 11 = $825 ÷ 11 = $75.00. Balance carried: $750.
- March: ($750 + $250) ÷ 10 = $1,000 ÷ 10 = $100.00.

Continuing the same way gives this schedule:

| Month | Paid at pharmacy without the program | Payment plan bill (Medicare's formula) |
|---|---|---|
| January | $775.00 | $200.00 |
| February | $250.00 | $75.00 |
| March | $250.00 | $100.00 |
| April | $250.00 | $127.78 |
| May | $250.00 | $159.03 |
| June | $250.00 | $194.74 |
| July | $250.00 | $236.41 |
| August | $125.00 | $261.41 |
| September to November | $0 each month | $261.41 each month |
| December | $0 | $261.40 |
| Year total | $2,400.00 | $2,400.00 |

Check: January through July adds to $1,092.96, and 4 × $261.41 + $261.40 = $1,307.04 for August through December, for $2,400.00. Figures are rounded to the cent, with December absorbing the rounding; your plan's bill is the number that counts.

What the comparison shows:

- **The year's total does not change.** Both columns add up to $2,400. The program moves payments later; it does not shrink them.
- **January cash need falls from $775 to $200.** For a household whose savings are thin at the start of the year, that is the main benefit.
- **Bills rise after the first month.** Medicare's page warns that "future payments might increase when you fill a new prescription (or refill an existing prescription)" because there are fewer months left to spread the remaining balance. Here the bill drops to $75 in February, then climbs to $261.41 by August and stays at about that level through December, after the pharmacy cost would have dropped to $0.

For the $150-a-month drug, the first-month comparison goes the other way: $150 in costs is less than the $200 maximum, so the January bill is the full $150. The bill then falls to $13.64 in February and climbs every month to about $156.59 in December, still $975 for the year. Those late-year bills are why Medicare.gov lists low yearly costs and costs that are the same each month among the situations where the option may not be the best choice.

## When paying from savings may beat spreading the bill

Medicare's "Before you use" page says, "You're most likely to benefit from participating in the Medicare Prescription Payment Plan if you have high drug costs earlier in the calendar year." The same page lists situations where the payment option "may not be the best choice," including:

- Your yearly drug costs are low.
- Your drug costs are the same each month.
- You're considering signing up late in the calendar year (after September).
- You don't want to change how you pay for your drugs.
- You get or are eligible for Extra Help, or for a Medicare Savings Program.
- You get help paying for drugs from other organizations, such as a State Pharmaceutical Assistance Program, a coupon program or other health coverage.

You can also combine the two. A household with enough cash can pay the pharmacy and keep the reserve as the backstop; one with less can use the payment option and keep a smaller reserve sized to the monthly bill plus the premium.

Missed payments carry consequences even without interest. Medicare's "Using" page says you'll get a reminder if you miss a payment, and "if you don't pay your bill by the date listed in that reminder, you'll be removed from the Medicare Prescription Payment Plan." You still owe the balance, though you stay enrolled in your drug plan. The page also sets a priority: "Always pay your health or drug plan monthly premium first (if you have one), so you don't lose your drug coverage."

Switching plans has a specific effect. "If you leave your current plan, or change to a new Medicare drug plan or Medicare health plan with drug coverage (like a Medicare Advantage Plan with drug coverage), your participation in the Medicare Prescription Payment Plan will end." Medicare's instruction is to "contact your new plan if you'd like to participate in the Medicare Prescription Payment Plan again." The pages reviewed here do not say whether participation carries into a new calendar year for someone who stays in the same plan, so ask your plan instead of assuming.

## Open Enrollment dates and where the reserve sits

The Open Enrollment Period runs from Oct. 15 to Dec. 7. Medicare's Joining a plan page says that during it you can join, drop or switch Medicare Advantage and Medicare drug plans. Coverage starts Jan. 1 of the next year, and "the plan must get your request to join by December 7." A plan choice the plan receives by Dec. 7, 2026, sets the deductible, the cost sharing and the premium you start paying on Jan. 1, 2027.

That calendar gives a reserve plan a natural order:

1. **By Dec. 7, 2026:** compare plans with your actual drug list. The figures that drive cash needs are the plan's deductible (from $0 up to the $700 maximum), how it charges for each of your drugs, whether each drug is covered at all, and the monthly premium. Those numbers come from each plan's materials.
2. **Before Jan. 1, 2027:** set the reserve to the plan you picked. For someone with a deductible plan and costly regular prescriptions, the whole deductible can come due with the first fills in January, so that amount, plus the first premium if it is not already set to be paid automatically, is worth having on hand by then. With lower-cost prescriptions, the deductible is paid over several months instead.
3. **In January:** if early costs will be high, consider signing up for the payment option with your plan; Medicare's guidance favors starting early.
4. **Each month:** read the Explanation of Benefits to see how much of the $2,400 is used.

Where the reserve is held matters less than whether you can reach it the day a prescription is filled. Medicare.gov lists "having your plan deduct it from your checking or savings account" as one way to pay premiums. A savings account you can move to checking without delay fits a January pharmacy bill or deductible. Money in investments that can fall in value, or in deposits with early-withdrawal penalties, is a weaker match for a bill likely to arrive in the first weeks of the year. How much to keep there, and where the rest of your money belongs, depends on your whole situation. Check with your plan for plan-specific costs, and with a financial professional for questions about the rest of your savings.

## Common questions

**Does the $2,400 cap in 2027 include my monthly Part D premium?**

No. Medicare.gov describes the cap as a limit on "out-of-pocket spending on covered Part D drugs," and it describes the premium separately as a monthly amount you pay for coverage "whether you get covered drugs or not." The premium depends on the plan you choose and may be higher depending on your income. Budget 12 months of your plan's premium in addition to any drug-cost reserve.

**Is the $700 deductible the same in every 2027 plan?**

No. The $700 is the most any Medicare drug plan may charge as a deductible in 2027, up from $615 in 2026. Medicare.gov notes that "some plans have no deductible," and others may set a lower one. Your plan's actual deductible is listed in its materials during Open Enrollment, and it is the figure that sets how much cash you may need in January.

**Does a married couple share one $2,400 limit?**

Medicare.gov describes the threshold in terms of your own out-of-pocket spending on covered Part D drugs, and the pages reviewed for this article do not describe a shared household cap. A couple where both spouses reach their own limit would pay up to $2,400 each, or $4,800 combined. That $4,800 is arithmetic, not an official figure. A couple where only one spouse has high drug costs would plan around one cap plus the other spouse's expected drug costs.

**Does the Medicare Prescription Payment Plan lower what I pay?**

No. Medicare.gov says it "might help you manage your monthly expenses, but it doesn't save you money or lower your drug costs." It replaces pharmacy payments with a monthly bill from your plan, at no cost and with no interest or fees even on late payments. The yearly total stays the same, but the timing changes, which can help when costs are high early in the year. If you miss a payment and don't pay by the date in the reminder, you are removed from the program and still owe the balance.

**If I switch plans during Open Enrollment, does my payment plan participation carry over?**

No. Medicare's "Using the Medicare Prescription Payment Plan" page says participation ends if you leave your current plan or change to a new Medicare drug plan or Medicare health plan with drug coverage. To use the option in 2027 with a new plan, contact that plan to participate again. If you are staying with the same plan, ask the plan how participation works for the new year, because the pages reviewed here do not address that case.
