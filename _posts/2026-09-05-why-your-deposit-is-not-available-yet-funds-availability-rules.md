You deposit a check on Monday and the balance updates immediately. Try to spend it and part of it is not there. The account shows two numbers, a balance and an available balance, and only one of them is money you can use.

The rules behind this are federal, they are older than online banking, and they set maximums rather than minimums. A bank can always release funds faster than required. It cannot hold them longer without a stated reason.

## The basic schedule

Regulation CC, which implements the Expedited Funds Availability Act, sets the outer limits.

Some deposits must be available the next business day: cash deposited in person, electronic payments including direct deposit and wire transfers, Treasury checks, cashier's checks and certified checks deposited in person, and the first portion of a check deposit. That first portion is $275 as of July 1, 2025, raised from $225 under the inflation adjustment the [Federal Reserve](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy.htm) applies on a five-year cycle.

Most remaining check funds must be available by the second business day. Business day means a banking day, so weekends and federal holidays extend the calendar meaningfully. A Friday afternoon deposit at a bank with a cutoff of 2 p.m. is treated as Monday's deposit, and second-business-day availability lands on Wednesday.

## When a bank may hold longer

Regulation CC allows exception holds in specific circumstances, and requires the bank to give you a notice saying which one applies and when the funds will be released.

- **New accounts**, generally in the first 30 days, where a longer schedule applies.
- **Large deposits**, meaning check deposits above a threshold on a single day, currently $6,725 after the same 2025 adjustment. The amount above the threshold may be held longer.
- **Redeposited checks** that were returned unpaid the first time.
- **Repeated overdrafts** in the recent past.
- **Reasonable cause to doubt collectibility**, which requires the bank to state the reason.
- **Emergency conditions**, such as a communications failure.

Exception holds are typically a few extra business days rather than an indefinite freeze. The notice must state the availability date, and if the hold is imposed after the deposit, the notice has to be sent promptly.

## Why mobile deposits often differ

Mobile check deposit is not fully covered by the same next-day rule that applies to a check handed to a teller. Banks generally treat mobile deposits under their own schedules, which are frequently slower and often tiered by account history and deposit size.

That is why the same check can clear faster at a branch than through the app, and why a new customer sees longer holds than a ten-year customer at the same bank. The bank is managing its own risk within the room the rules leave it.

## Available balance versus posted balance

The two numbers on an account do different jobs.

The posted or current balance includes deposits that have been credited, whether or not you can spend them. The available balance subtracts holds, pending debits from card authorizations, and anything else not yet settled.

Overdraft fees are assessed against the available balance. This is the mechanism behind a familiar complaint: the app showed enough money, the transaction went through, and a fee arrived anyway. The number to watch before spending is the available balance, and the number people usually look at is the other one.

## What actually helps

The holds are not negotiable in general, but several things shift the outcome:

1. **Use electronic payment where possible.** ACH and wire transfers are outside the check hold framework entirely.
2. **Deposit before the cutoff time**, which is posted and is often earlier than closing. After the cutoff, the deposit is the next banking day's.
3. **Ask before depositing a large check** whether a hold will apply. Banks will usually tell you, and knowing beforehand is better than discovering it after.
4. **Read the hold notice** rather than assuming the standard schedule. It names the release date.
5. **Keep the account healthy.** Deposit history and overdraft history feed directly into the discretion the rules allow.

For a savings account funded by transfer, none of this comes up. It matters most on checking accounts receiving paper checks, and on newly opened accounts of any type, where the longer new-account schedule catches people moving a balance to chase a rate.

The general principle is simple enough: the money is legally yours on deposit, and the availability rules govern when the bank must let you use it. They are a ceiling on delay, not a description of how fast things normally go.

## New accounts opened to chase a rate

The longer new-account schedule catches a specific group of people: those moving a balance to a bank offering a better rate.

For roughly the first thirty days, a bank may apply an extended availability schedule to a new account. The first portion of certain check deposits is still available quickly, and the rest can take substantially longer than the standard schedule allows.

The way around it is to fund the account by transfer rather than by check. An ACH pull initiated from the new bank, or a push from the old one, sits outside the check hold framework. Wire transfers arrive same-day and cost a fee, which is occasionally worth it on a large balance where the days of lost interest exceed the wire cost.

The other thing worth knowing is that the transferred money earns nothing while it is in transit. An ACH transfer typically leaves the sending account on day one and becomes available at the receiving bank a few business days later. On a large balance chasing a small rate advantage, those days can consume a meaningful share of the first year's gain.

## What a returned check does

A check that bounces resets the situation. The deposit is reversed, the funds come back out, and any spending that relied on them can trigger overdraft fees even though the original deposit appeared to be fine.

A redeposited check that previously bounced is one of the listed exception conditions, so the second attempt can be held longer than the first. Banks also track this at the account level, and a history of returned deposits feeds into the discretion the rules allow on future holds.

For deposits from an unfamiliar payer, the conservative habit is to wait until the funds are not just available but settled, which is a different and later moment. Availability means you may spend the money. It does not mean the check cannot come back.

## The check-scam version of this

Scams that ask a victim to deposit a check and forward part of the proceeds rely entirely on the gap between availability and settlement. The deposit shows up, the funds become available on schedule, the victim sends money, and the check is returned weeks later. The bank reverses the deposit and the victim is responsible for the shortfall.

The rule that protects against it is simple and worth stating plainly: funds becoming available is a legal deadline for the bank, not a verification that the check was good. Money forwarded on the strength of a deposited check from someone you do not know is money at risk regardless of what the balance says.
