The [FDIC](https://www.fdic.gov/national-rates-and-rate-caps)'s national deposit rate table has a shape right now that breaks the rule most people learned about CDs. The 12-month average sits at 1.71% APY. Go out to 24 months and the average drops to 1.57%. At 36 months it's 1.34%, at 48 months 1.27%, and at 60 months 1.36% — still below the one-year figure. Those are national averages as of August 17, 2026, and the direction of travel is the point: on average, banks are not paying you extra to commit money for longer.

That matters because the ladder structure most primers describe assumes the opposite. The classic version tells you to buy CDs at one, two, three, and four years, then roll each maturing rung into a new four-year CD, because the long end pays the most and staggering the maturities gives you regular access. Feed today's numbers into that structure and you're taking your lowest yields on the money you can touch least.

## The shape of the curve right now

Here is the full FDIC national average CD table, plus savings and money market for comparison, all as of August 17, 2026:

| Product | National average APY |
|---|---|
| Savings | 0.38% |
| Money market | 0.63% |
| 1 month CD | 0.22% |
| 3 month CD | 1.14% |
| 6 month CD | 1.41% |
| 12 month CD | 1.71% |
| 24 month CD | 1.57% |
| 36 month CD | 1.34% |
| 48 month CD | 1.27% |
| 60 month CD | 1.36% |

Read it top to bottom and the curve climbs steeply from one month to twelve, peaks, then slides back down through four years before ticking up slightly at five. A curve that rises and then falls like this gets called flat-to-inverted. The premium you'd normally collect for term risk has gone missing past the one-year mark.

Two cautions before you build anything on this table. First, an average has institutions on both sides of it, so some banks pay well above every figure here and some pay well below. Check your own institution's rate sheet rather than assuming these levels apply to you. Second, the product mix behind each tenor differs, so the small 60-month uptick is not necessarily a signal about five-year money. Use this table for the shape, not the levels.

## The break-even on locking long

Say you're choosing between a 48-month CD at the 1.27% average and a 12-month CD at 1.71%, with a plan to reinvest the 12-month CD three more times.

Over four years the 48-month CD compounds to about 5.18% total on your principal — that's 1.0127 to the fourth power. The rolling plan earns 1.71% in year one, then whatever three more one-year renewals bring. Solve for the renewal rate that makes the two tie and you get roughly **1.12% per year**. Do it with simple interest instead of compounding and the answer barely moves — about 1.12% either way.

So the question isn't whether rates fall. It's narrower: would 12-month CDs have to average below about 1.12% for the next three years? That's a drop of nearly 0.6 points from today's average, sustained for three years. It might happen. But notice the price of that insurance — 0.44 points a year of current yield, on money you can't reach — and notice that it only pays off in a fairly specific scenario. Locking long isn't wrong here. It's just no longer free, which is exactly what a flat curve means.

## A four-rung ladder for a front-loaded curve

Take $24,000 and split it into four equal $6,000 rungs. Compare two structures at the national averages.

**The classic long ladder** — 12, 24, 36, and 48 months:

- 12 month at 1.71% → $102.60 of first-year interest
- 24 month at 1.57% → $94.20
- 36 month at 1.34% → $80.40
- 48 month at 1.27% → $76.20
- First-year interest: **$353.40**, a blended 1.4725%

The 24-, 36-, and 48-month rungs hold those rates for their full terms. The 12-month rung does not — it matures after year one and gets reinvested at whatever the curve offers then, exactly like the shorter ladder's rungs.

**A front-loaded ladder** — 3, 6, 12, and 24 months — can't be totaled without stating an assumption, because two of its rungs mature inside the first year and have to be reinvested. Assume the plan laid out in the next section: each rung rolls into a 12-month CD as it comes due, and the 12-month average is still near 1.71% when it does.

- 3 month at 1.14% earns $17.10, then $6,017.10 earns 1.71% for nine months → $94.27 for the year
- 6 month at 1.41% earns $42.30, then $6,042.30 earns 1.71% for six months → $93.96
- 12 month at 1.71% → $102.60
- 24 month at 1.57% → $94.20 accrued in year one
- First-year interest: **$385.03**, a blended 1.6043%

On that assumption the front-loaded ladder beats the classic one by $31.63 in the first year, and it does it while handing you cash in month 3 instead of month 12.

The assumption is doing real work there, so test it against a worse one. Suppose the reinvestment rates available at months 3 and 6 were today's *short* averages instead — 1.14% and 1.41%. Then the 3-month rung earns $68.55 across the year, the 6-month rung earns $84.90, and the total falls to $350.24, a blended 1.4594%. The classic ladder wins that scenario by $3.16 over the year. That is 26 cents a month, in exchange for locking $12,000 away until 2029 and 2030.

So the honest range runs from beating the long ladder by $31.63 to losing to it by $3.16 — and the losing case still leaves you with a maturity every three to six months. The downside of staying short, on these averages, is small enough to measure in cents. A single 24-month rung stays in as a partial hedge: if one-year rates do slide, that rung holds 1.57% while everything shorter re-prices.

## Rolling each rung at maturity

The plan is to convert every rung into a 12-month CD as it comes due, so that after two years you own four rolling one-year CDs sitting at the tenor the curve pays best.

**Month 3.** The 3-month rung matures with about $17.10 of interest. Roll $6,017.10 into a 12-month CD, which now matures in month 15.

**Month 6.** The 6-month rung matures with about $42.30. Roll $6,042.30 into a 12-month CD maturing in month 18.

**Month 12.** The original 12-month rung matures with $102.60. Roll the full $6,102.60 forward again, maturing month 24.

**Month 24.** Two separate rungs come due this month. The original 24-month rung matures with about $189.88 of compounded interest on its $6,000. The rung you rolled at month 12 is a different amount: one year at 1.71% on $6,102.60 is about $104.35. Two maturities landing in the same month is a real scheduling flaw, and it's worth fixing rather than ignoring — when you roll at month 12, use a 9-month or 15-month term if your bank offers one, which re-spaces that rung to month 21 or 27 and restores the gaps.

From there the ladder self-maintains. Each maturity is a decision point where you look at the current curve, not a mechanical renewal. If the 24-month rate has climbed above the 12-month by then, extend. And find the length of your bank's automatic-renewal grace period in the disclosure — that window after maturity is when you can move the money without a penalty.

## The cost of breaking a rung early

Here's why term choice deserves the attention. A CD's early-withdrawal penalty is set in its disclosure and is often quoted as a number of months of interest.

Take the 48-month rung: $6,000 at 1.27% earns $76.20 a year, or $6.35 a month. Suppose you need the money after eight months and the penalty is six months of interest.

- Interest earned in eight months: $50.80
- Penalty: $38.10
- Net to you: **$12.70**

That's 0.21% over eight months, or about 0.32% annualized — less than the 0.38% savings average, and roughly half the money market average. Break it earlier still and the penalty can exceed what the CD has earned, at which point the shortfall comes out of principal. Penalty terms vary by bank and by term length, so the disclosure is the only place to check.

The lesson isn't that penalties are unfair. It's that a CD's stated APY is only what you earn if you hold it to maturity. Money that might be needed belongs in an emergency fund built from liquid accounts, not in a rung you'll break.

## FDIC coverage across a multi-bank ladder

Chasing the best rate at each tenor often means several banks, which is fine and sometimes changes your insurance math. FDIC coverage is $250,000 per depositor, per insured bank, per ownership category. Credit unions carry [NCUA](https://ncua.gov/consumers/share-insurance-coverage) coverage instead, on similar terms.

A $24,000 ladder isn't near any limit. But a $900,000 ladder might be, and the fix is straightforward: spreading it across four banks gives each account its own $250,000 of coverage, and different ownership categories at the same bank — single, joint, certain revocable trusts — are insured separately from each other. The easy thing to miss is accrued interest. Coverage applies to principal plus interest earned, so a rung funded right at $250,000 will be uninsured on its interest by maturity. Leave headroom.

## A checklist before the first rung

- Plot this same curve from your own bank's rate sheet. National averages tell you the shape; only your bank tells you your rate.
- Compare the 12-month CD to your existing savings rate. If savings is close, you're paying a lock-up for very little — and savings floats while a CD doesn't.
- Read the early-withdrawal penalty for each term before funding, not after.
- Check whether automatic renewal is on by default, and write every maturity date on a calendar. A ladder nobody tracks becomes a pile of auto-renewed CDs at whatever rate the bank picks.

Remember that APY already accounts for compounding while a quoted interest rate does not, so compare APY to APY. And treat the current curve as information about today, not a forecast. The reason a front-loaded ladder makes sense right now is not that anyone knows where rates are heading. It's that the long end isn't paying you enough to care.
