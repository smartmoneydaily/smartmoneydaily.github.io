The S&P 500 fell 3.7% on Friday, Nov. 20, to 6,910.39, its seventh losing session in a row. That close was about 11.6% below the record of 7,818.93 set on Oct. 6, the highest close in [FRED's S&P 500 series](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/series/SP500). A drop like that sends people to two questions at once: whether to sell, and where the money goes if they do. The first depends on things I can't see from here. The second comes with a tax rule that's worth getting right before December 31.

In the ordinary case, where you sell a company's stock at a loss in a taxable brokerage account, putting the cash in a savings account or a bank CD, or buying Treasury bills or a money market fund with it, doesn't by itself cause a wash sale. What the rule looks at is whether you, your spouse, a corporation you control or your IRA acquired the same stock, or something substantially identical, within 30 days before or after the sale. If none of that happened, the loss counts. It offsets your capital gains first, then up to $3,000 of other income, and anything left carries forward.

## What the wash-sale rule looks at

The rule is [26 U.S.C. 1091](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/1091). It disallows a loss on stock or securities when, "within a period beginning 30 days before the date of such sale or disposition and ending 30 days after such date," the taxpayer has acquired "substantially identical stock or securities," or a contract or option to acquire them. With the sale day in the middle, that's a 61-day window.

A bank deposit isn't stock, and a bank CD isn't either. A Treasury bill is a security, but it isn't substantially identical to a company's shares. [IRS Publication 550](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p550) says that "ordinarily, stocks or securities of one corporation are not considered substantially identical to stocks or securities of another corporation." It doesn't ordinarily treat a company's own bonds or preferred stock as identical to its common stock either, unless they're convertible and their prices and terms make them behave like the common shares. Where the cash waits isn't what the test is about. What you buy, and when, is.

For a sale on Wednesday, Nov. 25, the window runs from Monday, Oct. 26 through Friday, Dec. 25, and Dec. 25 itself is still inside it. The first day outside is Saturday, Dec. 26, so the first trading day you could buy the shares back without a wash sale is Monday, Dec. 28. For a sale on the last trading day of the year, Thursday, Dec. 31, the window runs from Dec. 1 through Saturday, Jan. 30, 2027, and the first trading day after it is Monday, Feb. 1.

## Purchases that count, including the ones before the sale

Pub 550 lists four ways to trip the rule within 30 days before or after the sale: buying substantially identical stock or securities, acquiring them in a fully taxable trade, acquiring a contract or option to buy them, or acquiring substantially identical stock "for your individual retirement arrangement (IRA) or Roth IRA." It adds that if "your spouse or a corporation you control buys substantially identical stock, you also have a wash sale."

The 30 days before the sale is the part that catches people in a falling market. Say you bought 100 shares in March at $100 each, and on Nov. 5 you bought 50 more at $70 because the price looked cheap. On Nov. 25 you sell the 100 March shares at $65, a $3,500 loss. Because 50 shares were bought inside the window, the loss on 50 of the shares you sold, $1,750, is disallowed. Assuming those are the only purchases between Oct. 26 and Dec. 25, the other $1,750 is an allowable capital loss. That's how Pub 550 matches shares in its own examples, pairing the shares bought with an equal number of shares sold, earliest purchases first.

The disallowed $1,750 is added to the cost of the Nov. 5 shares, which now have a basis of $5,250 instead of $3,500, and their holding period includes the time you held the March shares. That higher basis shrinks the gain, or enlarges the loss, when you eventually sell those shares, as long as that sale doesn't run into another wash sale.

An IRA purchase is different. Pub 550 leaves IRA and Roth IRA purchases out of the basis adjustment, and [Rev. Rul. 2008-5](https://www.irs.gov/irb/2008-03_IRB) says the loss is disallowed and "the individual's basis in the IRA or Roth IRA is not increased." There's no later sale where that loss comes back.

Automatic dividend reinvestment is a purchase, too. If dividends on the same stock reinvest, whether in the account you sold from or in another one, a reinvestment that lands anywhere in the 61 days can turn part of the loss into a wash sale.

Pub 550 doesn't offer a test for two different funds that hold similar things. It says to "consider all the facts and circumstances in your particular case." I'd read that as a reason for caution, not as a loophole.

## How much of the loss you can use for 2026

Under [26 U.S.C. 1211(b)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/1211), capital losses offset capital gains in full. Past that, you can deduct the lower of your net loss or $3,000 ($1,500 if you're married and file separately) against other income, like wages, IRA withdrawals or the interest your savings earned. Pub 550 says the unused part carries over to later years "until it is completely used up," and a carried loss stays short-term or long-term.

Take a simple case, with no wash sale, a filing status other than married filing separately, and enough taxable income to absorb the deduction. You bought shares for $20,000 in March and sell them for $15,000 on Nov. 25, a $5,000 short-term loss. With no other gains or losses in 2026, $3,000 comes off your other income and $2,000 carries into 2027. If that $3,000 would otherwise have been taxed at 22%, your 2026 federal tax drops by $660. If you'd already taken $4,000 of gains earlier in the year, the same $5,000 loss would cancel those gains and leave $1,000 to deduct against other income, with nothing to carry.

The carryover is figured as if you took the deduction. Pub 550 says the current year's allowable deduction counts "whether or not you claimed it and whether or not you filed a return," so leaving the $3,000 off your return doesn't, by itself, save it for later. The carryover worksheet also looks at taxable income, which is why someone with very little taxable income can end up carrying more.

## Dec. 31 is a trade date, and the holiday weeks have short days

For stock sold on an exchange, the date that counts is the trade date, not the settlement date. Pub 550 says your holding period ends "on the trade date you sold them" and warns not to confuse the two, and brokers enter the trade date as the date sold on Form 1099-B. Pub 550's own example is a Dec. 31, 2025 sale paid for in January 2026, and it says to "report your gain or loss on your 2025 return, even though you received the payment in 2026."

Since May 28, 2024, most stock trades settle one business day after the trade, under the SEC's [T+1 cycle](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/general-resources/news-alerts/alerts-bulletins/investor-bulletins/new-t1-settlement-cycle-what-investors-need-know-investor-bulletin). A sale on Wednesday, Nov. 25 settles Friday, Nov. 27, because Thanksgiving isn't a business day. A sale on Thursday, Dec. 31 settles Monday, Jan. 4, 2027, since New Year's Day falls on a Friday. It's still a 2026 sale.

The [NYSE holiday calendar](https://www.nyse.com/markets/hours-calendars) closes the market on Thursday, Nov. 26 and Friday, Dec. 25, and it has two 1 p.m. Eastern early closes: Friday, Nov. 27, the day after Thanksgiving, and Thursday, Dec. 24. Dec. 31 is a normal full session, so the last day to sell for 2026 isn't one of the short ones.

## Where the money waits, and what a month earns

For a $15,000 sale, the gap between parking spots over a month is small next to a $660 change in tax. The table puts rough numbers on four of them over about the length of the window after the sale. The bill row is a recent example, not something you could buy with the money from a Nov. 25 sale.

|---|---|---|---|
| Savings at the national average | 0.39% | about $5 over 31 days | FDIC coverage at an insured bank, within limits |
| 1-month CD at the national average | 0.23% | about $3 over 31 days | early-withdrawal terms vary, a penalty may apply |
| High-yield savings at a hypothetical 4% | 4% a year, simple interest | about $51 over 31 days | rate can change |
| 4-week T-bill, Nov. 19 auction, held to maturity | $99.685 per $100 | $47.25 over 28 days | no state income tax on the interest |

The two national averages are from the [FDIC](https://www.fdic.gov/national-rates-and-rate-caps)'s monthly table published on November 16, 2026. The bill is the 4-week issue in [Treasury's Nov. 19 auction results](https://www.treasurydirect.gov/instit/annceresult/press/preanre/2026/R_20261119_1.pdf), issued Nov. 24 and maturing Dec. 22. It sold at $99.685 per $100, a 4.050% discount rate and a 4.119% investment rate. At that price, $15,000 of face value cost $14,952.75 and pays $15,000 at maturity. Money from a later sale would go into a later auction or a bill bought on the market, at whatever price applies then. The savings and CD figures are simple interest on the average rate, so they're estimates.

Uninvested cash in a brokerage account is handled under the firm's cash terms, which may mean a bank sweep, a money market fund sweep or a cash balance held by the broker, and what it earns and what protects it depend on which one you have. If a large sale is headed for one bank, deposits you hold there in the same ownership category are added together against the $250,000 limit, and [this post on going past $250,000](/2026/09/02/insuring-more-than-250000-without-opening-ten-accounts/) has the ways around it.

The interest is taxed as ordinary income. For most people, bank interest counts in the year it's credited and available to withdraw, and a T-bill held to maturity generally reports its discount at maturity, so a bill bought in December that matures in January puts its interest in 2027. ## What the 1099-B will and won't catch

Box 1g of Form 1099-B shows a disallowed wash-sale loss. As a general rule, brokers are only required to report it when the sale and the purchase happen in the same account and involve covered securities with the same CUSIP number. The [Form 1099-B instructions](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i1099b) allow them to report more than that, and they carve out a few exceptions. A buyback in your spouse's account, your IRA or at another firm may not show up in box 1g at all.

Pub 550 doesn't leave room on this: "you cannot deduct a loss from a wash sale even if it is not reported on Form 1099-B." You'd report the sale on Form 8949, enter "W" in column (f) and put the disallowed amount as a positive number in column (g).

## What I'd check before selling

The $3,000 is a deduction from income, not a payment, and at a 22% rate it's worth $660. To me that makes it the smaller number in this decision. Over a month, a stock can move by more than that in either direction, and the tax rule doesn't change it. If you've already decided to sell at a loss, the checks are short. Look across the whole 61-day window for any acquisition of the same or substantially identical stock, or options and contracts to buy it, in your accounts, your spouse's, your IRA or Roth IRA and any corporation you control, with automatic reinvestments included. Count forward to the first day you could buy back without a wash sale. For a CD, the maturity date and the early-withdrawal terms decide when the cash is free, and nothing in the wash-sale rule requires you to reinvest the day the window ends.

Savings rates, and the rates on new CDs and bills, can change before the window closes. The FDIC's next national averages are due Dec. 21.
