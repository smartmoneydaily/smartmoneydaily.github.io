Maya's grandparents hand her parents a check for $10,000 the week she comes home from the hospital, with a note that says it is for college. That afternoon the parents open a bank's online application and reach a drop-down menu with three choices that look interchangeable: an individual account in a parent's name, a joint account with a minor, and a custodial account under the Uniform Transfers to Minors Act.

They are not interchangeable. Each answers three questions differently, and the product page answers none of them: who legally owns the $10,000, how the [FDIC](https://www.fdic.gov/resources/deposit-insurance) counts it if the bank fails, and whose tax return the interest lands on.

The short version first. A custodial account holds money that already belongs to the child, with a parent managing it. The FDIC insures it as the child's own single account, not the parent's. The interest is reported under the child's Social Security number, and for tax year 2025 it becomes a tax question only when the child's unearned income passes two lines: $1,350, where a dependent child generally has to file a return, and $2,700, where the excess can be taxed at the parent's rate. At a hypothetical 4% rate, the second line takes a balance above $67,500. Maya's $10,000 on its own never gets close.

## Three titles, three different owners

**The custodial account.** [IRS Publication 550](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p550) states the ownership rule plainly: "Property you give as a parent to your child under the Model Gifts of Securities to Minors Act, the Uniform Gifts to Minors Act, or any similar law becomes the child's property." The parent is the custodian, deciding where the money sits and signing the paperwork, but the balance is not the custodian's savings, and treating it as a family reserve to pull back later misreads what the title did.

The same passage sets a limit on spending. Income from the property "is taxable to the child, except that any part used to satisfy a legal obligation to support the child is taxable to the parent or guardian having that legal obligation." Paying for food and housing out of custodial interest moves that interest onto the parent's return. What a custodian may spend the money on at all is a matter of state law.

**The joint account with a parent.** Here the child is a co-owner rather than the sole owner. The [FDIC's joint-account rule](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-12/chapter-III/subchapter-B/part-330/section-330.9) describes a qualifying joint account as one where each co-owner "possesses withdrawal rights on the same basis." That is the feature and the risk at once: either name on the account can move the money. For tax purposes, Publication 550 says that when people hold a savings account as joint tenants, tenants by the entirety, or tenants in common, "each person's share of any interest or dividends from the property is determined by local law." The state, not the federal rules, settles whose share is whose.

**The parent's own account.** A savings account titled to the parent alone, even one nicknamed for the child in the banking app, is the parent's money in the bank's records. The FDIC's pass-through rules for money held on someone else's behalf start with the requirement that the relationship be "expressly disclosed in the bank's deposit account records," and a nickname on a dashboard does not meet it.

Publication 550 also covers a hybrid, an account "placed in the name and subject to the order of the parents as trustees." Its interest is taxable to the child only if state law says "the savings account legally belongs to the child" and "the parents are not legally permitted to use any of the funds to support the child."

## How the FDIC counts each title

The regulation for custodial money is [12 CFR 330.7](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-12/chapter-III/subchapter-B/part-330/section-330.7). Paragraph (b) says funds "held by a guardian, custodian, or conservator for the benefit of his or her ward, or for the benefit of a minor under the Uniform Gifts to Minors Act," and deposited in the custodian's name, "shall, for purposes of this part, be deemed to be agency or nominee accounts." Paragraph (a) says what that means: such funds "shall be insured to the same extent as if deposited in the name of the principal(s)."

The principal is the child. The FDIC brochure [Your Insured Deposits](https://www.fdic.gov/resources/deposit-insurance/brochures/insured-deposits) lists Single Accounts as including "An account established for one person by an agent, nominee, guardian, custodian, or conservator, including Uniform Transfers to Minors Act Accounts." It also addresses the custodian's signing authority: "If an account title identifies only one owner, but another person has the right to withdraw funds from the account (e.g., as Power of Attorney or custodian), the FDIC will insure the account as a Single Account."

So the custodial balance does not use up the parent's single-account coverage at that bank. It does combine with any other single accounts the child owns there, because "The FDIC adds together the balances in all Single Accounts owned by the same person at the same bank and insures the total up to $250,000."

The joint account works differently. Under 330.9, every co-owner must be a natural person, each must have signed a signature card or the bank's records must show co-ownership another way, "such as evidence that the institution has issued a mechanism for accessing the account to each co-owner," and withdrawal rights must be equal. When those conditions hold, the brochure says "each co-owner's shares of every joint account that he or she owns at the same insured bank are added together and the total is insured up to $250,000."

That stacking is the part to watch. A parent's half of a joint account with a child is added to that parent's share of every other joint account at the bank, including one shared with a spouse. If the account fails the qualifying test, paragraph (d) treats each named owner's "actual ownership interest" as part of that person's single accounts.

A parent's own account falls under [12 CFR 330.6](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-12/chapter-III/subchapter-B/part-330/section-330.6): funds "owned by a natural person and deposited in one or more deposit accounts in his or her own name shall be added together and insured up to the SMDIA in the aggregate," the standard maximum deposit insurance amount of $250,000. If that account names the child as payable-on-death beneficiary, the brochure says "the account would be insured as a Trust Account," a category rewritten in 2024 and covered in [Trust Account Coverage After the 2024 FDIC Rule Change](/2026/09/06/trust-account-coverage-after-the-2024-fdic-rule-change/).

| Title at the bank | FDIC ownership category | Whose limit it counts against |
|---|---|---|
| Parent as custodian under UTMA or UGMA | Single Account of the child | The child's single accounts at that bank |
| Joint account, parent and child | Joint Account | Each co-owner's share of all joint accounts at that bank |
| Parent's individual account | Single Account of the parent | The parent's single accounts at that bank |
| Parent's account naming the child as POD beneficiary | Trust Account | The parent's trust-account coverage at that bank |

For a $10,000 gift none of these limits is near. They matter where a family already keeps large balances at the same bank.

## Whose Social Security number goes on the account?

The number the bank collects decides whose name appears on the Form 1099-INT, and Publication 550 says which number to give.

For a custodial account: "If your child is the actual owner of an account that is recorded in your name as custodian for the child, give the child's TIN to the payer."

For a joint account, the rule follows the money. "Generally, if the funds in a joint account belong to one person, list that person's name first on the account and give that person's TIN to the payer." The publication gives the family case directly: "if you open a joint savings account with your child using funds belonging to the child, list the child's name first on the account and give the child's TIN."

Getting this wrong costs more than a mismatched form. Publication 550 warns that "if you do not provide a certified TIN on Form W-9, the payer must backup withhold on your interest payments at a rate of 24%." Withholding also closes a filing option later, because the parent's Form 8814 election is unavailable when federal income tax was withheld from the child's income.

The [Instructions for Forms 1099-INT and 1099-OID](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i1099int) tell payers to report bank deposit interest in amounts "of $10 or more." Maya's $10,000 at 4% earns about $400 in its first year, so a 1099-INT under her own number arrives each January.

## The dollar lines for tax year 2025

The child's interest is the child's income, and the kiddie tax governs how it is taxed once it grows. [IRS Topic 553](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc553), last reviewed June 10, 2026, gives the headline number: "If your child's interest, dividends, and other unearned income total more than $2,700, it may be subject to a specific tax on the unearned income of certain children."

The [Instructions for Form 8615 (2025)](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i8615) state the mechanism: "unearned income over $2,700 is taxed at the parent's rate if the parent's rate is higher than the child's." Gifted money gets no separate treatment. A child's unearned income "includes all income produced by property belonging to your child," "regardless of when the property was transferred or purchased or who transferred it."

A lower line comes first. [Publication 501](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p501), in Table 2, "2025 Filing Requirements for Dependents," says a single dependent who is neither 65 nor blind must file if "Your unearned income was more than $1,350." Topic 553 adds that for either the Form 8615 rules or the parent's election to apply, "your child must be required to file a return." A dependent child whose only income is $900 of savings interest has no federal return to file.

These figures are the ones printed for tax year 2025. Check the current year's instructions before relying on them for a later year.

| 2025 line for a dependent child with only interest income | What it triggers | Balance at a hypothetical 4% |
|---|---|---|
| More than $1,350 | A federal return is generally required | Above $33,750 |
| More than $2,700 | Form 8615, with the excess taxed at the parent's rate if higher | Above $67,500 |
| Less than $13,500 | Upper limit for the parent's Form 8814 election | Below $337,500 |

Run Maya's account forward at a steady 4%, a round assumption and not a forecast. The $10,000 left untouched earns about $780 in its eighteenth year and never reaches the $1,350 line.

Add $5,000 a year from birthday and holiday gifts and the picture changes. Interest reaches about $1,570 in year six, past the filing line, and about $2,990 in year eleven, past the $2,700 line, leaving roughly $290 exposed to the parent's rate if that rate is higher. For a savings account, the kiddie tax follows large and steady contributions, not one generous check.

## Form 8615 or the parent's Form 8814

Once a return is required, a family has two ways to report the interest.

**Form 8615 on the child's own return.** Topic 553 says to use it "if the child is under age 18, and in certain situations if the child is older." The 2025 instructions spell out those situations: a child who was 18 at the end of 2025 and "didn't have earned income that was more than half of the child's support," or a full-time student at least 19 and under 24 meeting the same support condition. At least one parent must have been alive at the end of 2025, and the rules "apply whether or not the child is a dependent." The form uses the parent's taxable income, so the child's return often waits on the parents' numbers.

**Form 8814 on the parent's return.** Topic 553: "If your child's only income is interest and dividend income (including capital gain distributions) and totals less than $13,500, you may be able to elect to include that income on your return rather than file a return for your child." The [Instructions for Form 8814 (2025)](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i8814) add conditions: the child was under 19, or under 24 as a full-time student, at the end of 2025; no estimated tax payments were made for the child; and there was "no federal income tax withheld from the child's income." A teenager with a summer job fails the only-income test and files separately.

Under the election, "The amounts at $1,350 and below are not taxed," and "the tax rate on the child's income between $1,350 and $2,700 is 10%." The form subtracts a $2,700 base amount, and only the interest above it is added to the parent's income. With Maya's year-eleven interest of about $2,990, the parents would owe $135 on the middle slice and add roughly $290 to their own income.

The election has costs that do not show on the form. The parent cannot deduct the "Penalty on early withdrawal of child's savings," which matters for CDs, and the added income can reduce the parent's deductions and credits, including the child tax credit and education tax credits. The instructions' method is the right one: figure the tax both ways, then "compare the methods to determine which results in the lower tax."

## When the custodianship ends

None of the federal sources quoted here sets the age when a custodial account ends. That age comes from the Uniform Transfers to Minors Act or Uniform Gifts to Minors Act of the state whose law governs the account, which is why this post gives no single number. The account agreement should name the state, and that statute supplies the age.

What changes at that point is control. The money was the child's from the day of the gift, so the handover moves management without changing who owns the balance. Once retitled into the adult child's own name, it becomes an individual account under 330.6.

The federal tax age tests do not move with the state's custodial age. Form 8615 can still reach an 18-year-old or a full-time student under 24 who meets its support condition, after the account has been turned over. A joint account has no end date of this kind at all.

## Kids' savings, a custodial CD ladder, or a parent-held account

The product is a separate decision from the title. A kids' savings account, a single CD, or a ladder of CDs with staggered maturities can each be held in a custodial title, a joint title, or the parent's name.

| | Custodial savings or CD | Joint account with parent | Parent's own account |
|---|---|---|---|
| Legal owner | The child | Both, with shares set by state law | The parent |
| Who can withdraw | The custodian, for the child | Each co-owner | The parent |
| FDIC category | Child's single account | Joint account | Parent's single account |
| Number on the 1099-INT | Child's | First-listed owner's, the child's if the funds are the child's | Parent's |
| Kiddie tax exposure | Yes, once past the 2025 lines | Yes, on the child's share | None, the interest is the parent's |

A **kids' savings account** keeps the money available for frequent small deposits. A **custodial CD ladder** fixes rates for set terms and fits a lump sum like Maya's, though an early withdrawal penalty is deductible only on the child's own return. For joint CDs, the FDIC's signature-card requirement "does not apply to CDs or accounts established by an agent, nominee, guardian, custodian, executor, or conservator."

A **parent-held account** keeps full control and keeps the interest out of the child's tax picture. The trade-off is that nothing in it belongs to the child, and it counts against the parent's single-account limit at that bank. [The Tax Bite on Savings Interest](/2026/08/26/the-tax-bite-on-savings-interest-what-your-real-yield-is/) covers how that interest is taxed as ordinary income.

## Before opening the account

1. **Read the exact title the bank will print.** A parent named as custodian for the child under a named state's Uniform Transfers to Minors Act is a custodial account. Two names joined by "and" or "or" is joint. A nickname in the app is neither.
2. **Give the right Social Security number and certify it.** Use the child's number for a custodial account and for a joint account funded with the child's money. A missing or uncertified number can trigger 24% backup withholding.
3. **Confirm which state's act governs.** That statute sets when the custodianship ends and what the custodian may spend.
4. **Check the other accounts at the same bank.** A custodial balance stacks with the child's other single accounts; a joint balance stacks with each parent's other joint accounts.
5. **Keep a record of every gift.** The IRS [gift tax FAQ](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/frequently-asked-questions-on-gift-taxes) lists the annual exclusion per donee as $19,000 for both 2025 and 2026, with each spouse entitled to it, and gifts not more than that amount are generally not taxable gifts. The grandparents' $10,000 sits well inside it.
6. **Save each January's 1099-INT.** Publication 550 calls those forms "an important part of your records," and the running total shows the year interest passes $1,350 or $2,700.
7. **Keep support spending in the parent's accounts.** Custodial income used for the child's basic support is taxable to the parent.

The drop-down menu offered three titles that look alike on a screen. One makes the money the child's, one makes it shared, and one leaves it with the parent, and the FDIC and the IRS read the title exactly that way.

Run your own numbers instead: your balance, the APY on offer, and the period you plan to hold.
