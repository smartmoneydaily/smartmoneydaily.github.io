The five-year CD holding your IRA matures in March 2030. You turn 73 in November 2026. Somewhere between those two dates the IRS expects money out of an account your bank has agreed to keep shut.

That collision is why an IRA CD is not simply a CD with a label attached. The deposit itself behaves normally — fixed term, fixed rate, a penalty schedule printed in the disclosure. What surrounds it is a tax wrapper with its own calendar, its own rules about moving money, and its own line in the deposit insurance tables. Every one of those differences is cheaper to settle before the term starts than after.

## The $250,000 that sits in its own bucket

Deposit insurance sorts accounts by ownership category, and retirement deposits have a category of their own. [12 CFR 330.14](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/12/330.14) defines it as any individual retirement account described in section 408(a) of the Internal Revenue Code, any eligible deferred compensation plan described in section 457, and any individual account plan defined in section 3(34) of ERISA to the extent that participants and beneficiaries have the right to direct the investment of assets held in individual accounts. Everything in that description is aggregated and insured up to $250,000 per participant.

The [FDIC](https://www.fdic.gov/resources/deposit-insurance)'s [guide for bank employees](https://www.fdic.gov/financial-institution-employees-guide-deposit-insurance/certain-retirement-accounts) translates the citation into account names: traditional, Roth, SEP, and SIMPLE IRAs, section 457 plans, self-directed Keogh plans, and self-directed defined contribution plans. Deposits in all of those owned by the same depositor at the same insured institution are added together, and the total is insured to $250,000.

| Also at your bank | Shares the IRA CD's $250,000? |
|---|---|
| A Roth IRA savings account | Yes |
| A SEP or SIMPLE IRA CD | Yes |
| A self-directed Keogh deposit | Yes |
| A self-directed 401(k) deposit | Yes |
| Your personal savings in your own name | No — the single account category, with its own limit |
| A joint account with your spouse | No — a separate category again |
| A 403(b), a non-self-directed 401(k), or a Coverdell ESA | No — these sit outside this category entirely |

The left column is where people go wrong in both directions. A depositor who assumes the IRA CD eats into the same $250,000 as the checking account is giving up coverage they already have. A depositor who opens a second IRA at the same bank to spread risk has not spread anything.

Beneficiary designations do no work here either. The [FDIC](https://www.fdic.gov/resources/deposit-insurance)'s own worked example is a depositor holding two IRAs at one institution — a $100,000 Roth and a $180,000 traditional, with the same two children named as beneficiaries on both. The insured amount is $250,000 and the uninsured amount is $30,000. Naming beneficiaries does not increase coverage for this category, which is the opposite of how a payable-on-death savings account behaves, and the reason the habit transfers badly.

Credit unions draw the lines differently, and the difference is not cosmetic. Under [12 CFR 745.9-2](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/12/745.9-2), [NCUA](https://ncua.gov/consumers/share-insurance-coverage) insurance combines IRA and Roth IRA accounts and insures them in the aggregate up to $250,000, while a Keogh account is separately insured from an IRA account, a Roth IRA account, or aggregated IRA and Roth accounts. A self-employed member with both at one credit union has two limits. The same member with both at one bank has one. If you are running close to the line, the [ownership category rules](/2026/08/21/how-fdic-ownership-categories-stack-coverage-at-one-bank/) are worth reading against the specific charter holding your money rather than in general.

## Transfer and rollover are not two words for the same thing

An IRA CD matures, another bank is paying more, and what happens next depends on which of two procedures the paperwork sets in motion.

A trustee-to-trustee transfer moves the money between custodians without it passing through your hands. The [IRS](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/plan-participant-employee/rollovers-of-retirement-plan-and-ira-distributions) is direct about the status of that move: this type of transfer isn't a rollover. Nothing about it is limited by count, nothing is reported as a distribution, and no clock starts.

A rollover is the other path. You take the money, then put it somewhere else, and two constraints apply at once. You have 60 days from the date you receive the distribution to roll it over to another plan or IRA. And beginning after January 1, 2015, you can make only one rollover from an IRA to another — or the same — IRA in any 12-month period, regardless of the number of IRAs you own. The limit aggregates all of your IRAs, SEP and SIMPLE included alongside traditional and Roth.

Withholding is the detail that sinks 60-day rollovers of deposits. An IRA distribution paid to you is subject to 10% withholding unless you elect out of withholding or choose a different amount. Take $50,000 out of a matured IRA CD without electing out and $45,000 arrives. Rolling over $45,000 leaves $5,000 that was never rolled over, and closing the gap means finding $5,000 somewhere else inside the 60 days. The withheld amount comes back at tax time, not at the moment you need it.

There is also a rule about which dollars can move at all. [IRS Publication 590-B](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p590b) states that amounts that must be distributed during a particular year — required minimum distributions — aren't eligible for rollover treatment. From the year you reach RMD age onward, the year's required amount has to come out and stay out before the rest of the balance goes anywhere.

For a CD the safe route is narrow enough to write down.

1. Open the receiving IRA first. The new custodian needs an account number on its side before it can request anything from the old one.
2. Ask that institution for a trustee-to-trustee transfer form, not a rollover form. The wording on the paperwork is what decides which set of rules applies to the money.
3. Send it before the maturity date rather than during the grace period. Grace periods run somewhere around a week to ten days, which is not much room to discover the form was the wrong one.
4. Put the maturity instruction in writing separately — transfer out, do not renew — so the certificate cannot roll into a new term while the transfer is in transit.
5. Check both statements afterward. The old institution should show the balance leaving; the new one should show it arriving as a transfer, not as a contribution. A transfer miscoded as a contribution can breach the annual contribution limit on paper.

If the money lands in your hands anyway, the 60-day clock and the one-per-12-months limit are both already running, and the withholding has already been taken.

## The withdrawal the calendar orders

Required minimum distributions are where a locked term and a tax rule meet head-on.

The [IRS](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/retirement-plan-and-ira-required-minimum-distributions-faqs) sets the starting point at 73: you generally must start taking withdrawals from your traditional IRA, SEP IRA, SIMPLE IRA, and retirement plan accounts when you reach that age. The first one is for the year you reach 73, and it can be delayed until April 1 of the following year.

That delay is less of a favor than it looks. Turn 73 in November 2026 and postpone, and the 2026 distribution is due by April 1, 2027 while the 2027 distribution is due by December 31, 2027. Both land on the same return. For anyone sitting near the top of a bracket, one year of deferral can cost more than it saves.

Roth IRAs are outside all of this during the owner's lifetime. Withdrawals from Roth IRAs are not required until after the death of the account owner, so a Roth IRA CD has no RMD date to design around.

Missing the deadline is expensive but not catastrophic if you catch it. The amount not withdrawn may be subject to an excise tax of 25%, reduced to 10% if the RMD is timely corrected within two years.

The escape from the locked-term problem is an aggregation rule most people never hear about. An IRA owner must calculate the RMD separately for each IRA they own, but can withdraw the total amount from one or more of the IRAs. Publication 590-B puts it the same way: determine a separate required minimum distribution for each traditional IRA, then total those minimum amounts and take the total from any one or more of them.

That single sentence is what lets a CD stay intact. Keep part of your IRA money in an IRA savings account or a short CD, calculate the required amount across everything, and satisfy the whole obligation out of the liquid piece. The five-year CD then runs to maturity untouched, having never been the account the withdrawal came from.

Sizing the liquid piece is the part worth doing on paper before the term starts. An RMD is calculated for each account by dividing the prior December 31 balance of that IRA by a life expectancy factor the IRS publishes in the Publication 590-B tables, and the factor gets smaller as you get older. The required dollar amount therefore climbs even in a year the balance does not. A liquid reserve sized for the first year of a five-year term is undersized by the fifth. The safer approach is to hold back enough for several years of withdrawals rather than one, or to stagger maturities so a certificate comes due roughly every year and the cash it releases covers the next distribution — the same logic behind [a CD ladder](/2026/08/19/building-a-cd-ladder-when-12-month-cds-pay-the-most/), applied to a deadline instead of a rate forecast.

The structural mistake is the reverse: one IRA, one bank, one long CD, one hundred percent of the balance. There is nowhere for the RMD to come from except the certificate, and breaking it is the only option left.

## Two penalties, written by two different authorities

When money does have to leave an IRA CD early, two separate charges can apply, and they are easy to blur together because both get described as "the penalty."

| | The bank's early withdrawal penalty | The IRS additional tax |
|---|---|---|
| Who sets it | the bank, in the account contract | the Internal Revenue Code |
| What triggers it | taking money out before the maturity date | a distribution before age 59½, unless an exception applies |
| How much | months of interest, as stated in the disclosure | 10% of the taxable amount |
| Where it appears | deducted from the amount the bank pays you | Form 5329 and Schedule 2 of your Form 1040 |
| Can it be waived | yes, at the bank's discretion | only by qualifying for a listed exception |

The federal rules governing time deposits actually address the retirement case. A footnote to [12 CFR 204.2(c)(1)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/12/204.2) covers the situation where the depository institution pays all or a portion of a time deposit representing funds contributed to an individual retirement account or a Keogh (H.R. 10) plan when the individual for whose benefit the account is maintained attains age 59½ or is disabled. Read the structure of that provision rather than the age. It permits the bank to pay without imposing the penalty. It does not require the bank to do so.

Which means that at 73, with an RMD due, federal regulation has already cleared the way and the answer still comes from your bank's disclosure. Some institutions waive the penalty on RMD withdrawals as published policy. Some waive on request. Some charge it. The account agreement is the document that settles it, and the time to read that paragraph is before the term starts, since [running the break-even](/2026/08/25/when-breaking-a-cd-actually-pays-run-the-break-even/) after the fact only tells you how much the omission cost.

There is a timing effect worth knowing about when the waiver does not come. This year's required amount was fixed by last December 31's balance, so a penalty charged in March changes nothing about what has to come out by December. It only lowers the base the following year's calculation starts from. Worth asking the bank one narrow question before you sign the withdrawal slip: does the penalty come out of the amount paid to you, or off the remaining balance? If it comes out of the payment, a withdrawal sized to the exact required figure arrives short of it — and the shortfall is measured against the 25% excise tax, not against the bank's disclosure.

The other side of the table is [Topic 557](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc557): a 10% additional tax on early distributions from traditional and Roth IRAs unless an exception applies, reported on Form 5329. Rollovers and trustee-to-trustee transfers to another IRA sit on the exception list, which is why a 60-day rollover that fails to complete costs more than a missed deadline suggests. The portion that never arrives is simply a distribution — ordinary income in that year, and the additional 10% on top if you are under 59½.

## The one piece of mail that matters

Auto-renewal is the quiet risk in an IRA CD, because the renewal notice is usually the only warning that a new multi-year term is about to begin.

[12 CFR 1030.5(b)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/12/1030.5) governs time accounts longer than one month that renew automatically, and it fixes when that warning has to reach you. The disclosures must be mailed or delivered at least 30 calendar days before maturity of the existing account, or alternatively at least 20 calendar days before the end of the grace period, provided a grace period of at least five calendar days is allowed. Where the term runs longer than a year, the notice has to carry the full account disclosures for the new account along with the date the existing one matures. Where the new rate is not yet known, it must say so, state when the rate will be determined, and give a telephone number consumers may call to obtain the rate and the annual percentage yield.

| Your certificate | What has to reach you before maturity |
|---|---|
| Longer than one year, renews automatically | Full account disclosures for the new term, 30 days before maturity — or 20 days before the end of a grace period of at least five days |
| Over one month to one year, renews automatically | Same timing; the notice may be the shorter form, covering the maturity date, the new rate, and any terms that differ from the existing account |
| Longer than one year, does not renew | The maturity date and whether interest will be paid after maturity, at least 10 calendar days before maturity |
| One month or less | Outside the scope of this section |

So the notice arrives, and on a CD of any real length it arrives before you know what the renewal will pay. That gap is written into the rule rather than an oversight by your bank, which is why the regulation requires a phone number in the notice: the last step of the decision is a call, not a letter.

For anyone at or near 73, the renewal question has a second half. A certificate that rolls into another five-year term has put the next four RMDs back inside a locked account. If the rest of your IRA money cannot cover those withdrawals, the notice in your hand is the last convenient moment to change the term, shorten it, or move part of the balance to something liquid.

## What the tax forms look like

One familiar piece of paperwork stops arriving, and this is where the IRA wrapper works in your favor.

Interest credited inside an IRA CD generates no annual Form 1099-INT. Amounts in an IRA, including earnings and gains, generally aren't taxed until distributed. The compounding runs without an annual tax bill against it, which is a different arithmetic from [the tax on ordinary savings interest](/2026/08/26/the-tax-bite-on-savings-interest-what-your-real-yield-is/), where interest credited in a year is taxable in that year whether or not you touched it.

What arrives instead, and only when money comes out, is Form 1099-R. A year with no distribution produces no form at all. A year with an RMD produces one covering what was distributed, not what was earned.

That difference shows up when comparing quoted rates across account types. A 4% taxable CD and a 4% IRA CD do not put the same amount in your pocket, and the gap runs in the IRA's direction during the accumulation years and reverses when traditional IRA money comes out as ordinary income. The comparison worth making is between the IRA CD and the other places IRA money could sit, not between the IRA CD and a taxable one.

An IRA CD asks two institutions to agree about your money: a bank that holds a term to a date and a tax code that names a different date. Neither will adjust for the other, and the only point at which you can align them is the day you choose the term.
