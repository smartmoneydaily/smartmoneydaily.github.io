A nurse in Texas who moved from Seoul a few years ago still has a savings account in Korea with about 10 million won in it at its peak last year, plus a small euro account left over from a year working in Germany. Neither balance looks large in dollars, and neither account has ever paid her more than a few hundred dollars of interest. Her question this week is whether she owes the government a form called the FBAR, and whether it is too late.

A U.S. person who owns or can sign on foreign financial accounts has to file FinCEN Form 114, the Report of Foreign Bank and Financial Accounts (FBAR), when the combined maximum values of those accounts went over $10,000 at any time during the calendar year. The report for calendar year 2025 was due April 15, 2026, and every filer got an automatic extension to October 15, 2026, without asking. In her case, the two accounts add up to $10,453 under the Treasury exchange rates the instructions require, so a report would be needed even though neither account alone came close. The FBAR is separate from the tax return, and the interest on those accounts is reported on the tax return in its own way.

The rules here come from the IRS page [Report of Foreign Bank and Financial Accounts (FBAR)](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/report-of-foreign-bank-and-financial-accounts-fbar), FinCEN's [BSA Electronic Filing Requirements for the FBAR](https://www.fincen.gov/sites/default/files/shared/FBAR%20Line%20Item%20Filing%20Instructions.pdf) (the line-by-line instructions), the IRS [Instructions for Schedule B](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i1040sb), the IRS [Comparison of Form 8938 and FBAR requirements](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/comparison-of-form-8938-and-fbar-requirements) and Treasury's [Reporting Rates of Exchange](https://fiscaldata.treasury.gov/datasets/treasury-reporting-rates-exchange/treasury-reporting-rates-of-exchange). This is general information about how the reporting rules work, not tax or legal advice for any household.

## Who has to file

The IRS FBAR page states the rule in two parts. A U.S. person "must file an FBAR to report: a financial interest in or signature or other authority over at least one financial account located outside the United States if the aggregate value of those foreign financial accounts exceeded $10,000 at any time during the calendar year reported."

"U.S. person" covers more than citizens. The FinCEN instructions define it as "United States citizens (including minor children); United States residents; entities, including but not limited to, corporations, partnerships, or limited liability companies created or organized in the United States or under the laws of the United States; and trusts or estates formed under the laws of the United States." The instructions say residency is decided by the tax residency tests in 26 U.S.C. section 7701(b), so a green card holder living in Ohio with a childhood savings account in Manila is generally a U.S. person for this purpose. So is a U.S.-citizen child whose grandparents opened an account for her abroad.

Two kinds of connection to an account count:

- **Financial interest.** The most common case is that you are "the owner of record or holder of legal title, regardless of whether the account is maintained for the benefit of the United States person or for the benefit of another person." The instructions extend this to accounts held by an agent or nominee for you, and to accounts held by entities you control: a corporation in which you own, directly or indirectly, more than 50 percent of the stock's value or voting power; a partnership in which you hold more than 50 percent of the profits or capital; and, under separate tests, certain trusts and other entities.
- **Signature authority.** This is "the authority of an individual (alone or in conjunction with another individual) to control the disposition of assets held in a foreign financial account by direct communication (whether in writing or otherwise) to the bank or other financial institution that maintains the financial account." An adult child who can sign on an elderly parent's account in Toronto has signature authority over it, even with no ownership.

Whether the account earned anything does not matter. The IRS page says: "Whether the account produced taxable income has no effect on whether the account is a foreign financial account for FBAR purposes." A non-interest checking account counts the same way a savings account does.

## What counts as a foreign financial account

The FinCEN definition of a financial account "includes, but is not limited to, a securities, brokerage, savings, demand, checking, deposit, time deposit, or other account maintained with a financial institution (or other person performing the services of a financial institution)." It also reaches some things savers do not think of as accounts: "an insurance policy with a cash value (such as a whole life insurance policy), an annuity policy with a cash value, and shares in a mutual fund or similar pooled fund (i.e., a fund that is available to the general public with a regular net asset value determination and regular redemptions)."

Location is about where the account is kept, not who owns the bank. The instructions give both directions:

- "An account maintained with a branch of a United States bank that is physically located outside of the United States is a foreign financial account."
- "An account maintained with a branch of a foreign bank that is physically located in the United States is not a foreign financial account."

So a time deposit at the Seoul branch of a U.S. bank counts, while a savings account at the New York branch of a Korean bank does not.

The same location rule matters for deposit insurance. Under the [FDIC](https://www.fdic.gov/resources/deposit-insurance)'s rules at [12 CFR 330.3(e)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-12/chapter-III/subchapter-B/part-330/section-330.3), an obligation of an insured bank "carried on the books and records of an office of that institution located outside any State" is not an insured deposit, "notwithstanding that the obligation may also be payable at an office of that institution located within any State." The rule carves out overseas military banking facilities and branches in a few Pacific nations. A savings account at a U.S. bank's branch abroad is generally not FDIC-insured, even though it's a U.S. bank.

The IRS page lists accounts that don't need to be reported, and the FinCEN instructions add the conditions. They include a foreign account held in an IRA you own or are a beneficiary of; a foreign account held by or for a retirement plan "described in Internal Revenue Code section 401(a), 403(a), or 403(b)" in which you participate; accounts "Maintained on a U.S. military banking facility"; accounts of "any governmental entity of the United States"; accounts of an international financial institution "if the United States government is a member"; and some trust and correspondent accounts. The plan exception is tied to those U.S. Code sections, so it isn't a general exemption for a pension or retirement account kept abroad.

## The $10,000 test: maximum values, added together

The test is not your balance on December 31 and not your average balance. It is the highest value each account reached during the year, converted to dollars, and then added across accounts.

The FinCEN instructions lay it out in two steps:

1. "Determine the maximum value of each account (in the currency of that account) during the calendar year being reported. The maximum value of an account is a reasonable approximation of the greatest value of currency or nonmonetary assets in the account during the calendar year." Periodic statements can be used "provided that the statements fairly reflect the maximum account value during the calendar year."
2. "In the case of non-United States currency, convert the maximum account value for each account into United States dollars."

Then: "If the maximum account value of a single account or aggregate of the maximum account values of multiple accounts exceeds $10,000, an FBAR must be filed."

Two smaller rules come with the arithmetic. Dollar amounts are "rounded up to the next whole dollar," and the instructions' own example is that $15,265.25 is entered as $15,266. And if an account's value works out negative, such as an overdrawn account, the instructions say to "enter zero (0)."

Because the test adds maximums, two accounts can trigger a filing even if their balances were never high on the same day. A savings account that peaked in March and a second account that peaked in August are added at their separate peaks.

**Worked example: the nurse's two accounts.** The balances are hypothetical. The exchange rates are Treasury's published rates for December 31, 2025: 1,443.75 won per dollar and 0.851 euro per dollar.

| Account | Highest balance in 2025 | Treasury rate, Dec. 31, 2025 | Dollar value (rounded up) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Savings account in Korea | 10,000,000 won | 1,443.75 won per $1 | $6,927 |
| Savings account in Germany | 3,000 euros | 0.851 euro per $1 | $3,526 |
| Aggregate of maximum values | | | $10,453 |

The math: 10,000,000 ÷ 1,443.75 = 6,926.41, rounded up to $6,927. 3,000 ÷ 0.851 = 3,525.26, rounded up to $3,526. The total of $10,453 is over $10,000, so the 2025 report is required and both accounts go on it.

A smaller peak changes the answer. If the Korean account had peaked at 9,000,000 won instead, it would convert to $6,234 (9,000,000 ÷ 1,443.75 = 6,233.77, rounded up), and the total would be $9,760. That is under the threshold, so no FBAR for that year, though the Schedule B question discussed below still applies.

If you can't tell whether you crossed the line, the instructions address that too. Filers with fewer than 25 accounts "that are unable to determine if the aggregate maximum account values of the accounts exceeded $10,000 at any time during the calendar year" complete the account sections and check the "amount unknown" box.

## Which exchange rate to use

The FBAR instructions don't let you pick any rate. They say to convert "by using the Treasury's Financial Management Service rate ... for the last day of the calendar year. If no Treasury Financial Management Service rate is available, use another verifiable exchange rate and provide the source of that rate."

Treasury publishes these rates as the Treasury Reporting Rates of Exchange on its Fiscal Data site, which describes them as "the U.S. government's authoritative exchange rates to ensure consistency for foreign currency units and U.S. dollar equivalents across all reporting done by agencies of the government." The rates are stated as units of foreign currency per one U.S. dollar, so you divide the foreign balance by the rate.

For calendar year 2025, here is what the $10,000 line equals in several currencies at Treasury's December 31, 2025 rates. These are simple conversions ($10,000 × rate), shown so a reader can compare a statement balance to the threshold at a glance.

| Currency | Treasury rate, Dec. 31, 2025 (per $1) | $10,000 equals |
|---|---|---|
| Korean won | 1,443.75 | 14,437,500 won |
| Euro | 0.851 | 8,510 euros |
| Mexican peso | 17.956 | 179,560 pesos |
| Canadian dollar | 1.369 | 13,690 Canadian dollars |
| British pound | 0.743 | 7,430 pounds |
| Indian rupee | 89.854 | 898,540 rupees |
| Philippine peso | 58.911 | 589,110 pesos |

The test is on the aggregate. A single Canadian account with a peak of 12,000 Canadian dollars is under the line by itself, but if you also have a UK account, both peaks get converted and added.

The tax return uses a different conversion rule. For income such as interest, the IRS says to "Use the exchange rate prevailing when you receive, pay, or accrue the item," and its yearly average rate page adds that "The Internal Revenue Service has no official exchange rate. Generally, it accepts any posted exchange rate that is used consistently." That is why the dollar figure for the interest on your return may not match a conversion of the same won or euros on the FBAR.

## Joint accounts, spouses and children

A joint account is "a financial account type listed above owned jointly by two or more persons." The instructions say "Each joint owner must report the entire value of the account as determined under Item 15," the maximum-value item. Two siblings who are both U.S. persons and jointly own a 20,000-euro savings account in Italy each report the full 20,000 euros converted to dollars, not half.

Married couples get one shortcut. A spouse doesn't need to file a separate FBAR if all three conditions in the instructions are met: all the accounts the non-filing spouse must report are jointly owned with the filing spouse, the filing spouse reports those joint accounts on a timely filed FBAR, and both have completed and signed FinCEN Form 114a, the "Record of Authorization to Electronically File FBARs." If any one of those fails, "both spouses are required to file separate FBARs, and each spouse must report the entire value of the jointly owned accounts."

Two conditions are easy to miss:

- The exception requires that *all* the accounts the non-filing spouse is required to report are joint with the filing spouse. The filing spouse can have solo accounts and report them on the same FBAR. But if the spouse who wants to skip filing has a reportable solo account abroad, the exception doesn't apply, and both spouses file separate reports that each show the joint accounts at full value.
- The IRS page says "Income tax filing status, such as married-filing-jointly and married-filing-separately, has no effect on your qualification for this exception." Filing a joint 1040 doesn't, by itself, cover a spouse's FBAR obligation.

Form 114a isn't sent in. The IRS page says you "just keep it for your records and make it available to FinCEN or IRS upon request."

Children are U.S. persons too. The FinCEN instructions say: "Generally, a child is responsible for filing his or her own FBAR report. If a child cannot file his or her own FBAR for any reason, such as age, the child's parent, guardian, or other legally responsible person must file it for the child." When a parent signs, the filer title is entered as "Parent/Guardian filing for child." A savings account opened abroad by a grandparent in a U.S. child's name can create a filing for the child once the maximum value crosses $10,000.

## The deadline: April 15, with an automatic move to October 15

The IRS page puts it plainly: "The FBAR is an annual report, due April 15 following the calendar year reported. You're allowed an automatic extension to October 15 if you fail to meet the FBAR annual due date of April 15. You don't need to request an extension to file the FBAR."

FinCEN's instructions explain where that comes from. The Surface Transportation and Veterans Health Care Choice Improvement Act of 2015 set the April 15 due date and "mandated a maximum six-month extension of the filing deadline," and FinCEN "will grant filers failing to meet the FBAR annual due date of April 15th an automatic extension to October 15th each year."

For most filers reporting accounts held during calendar year 2025, that makes **October 15, 2026** the last day before the report counts as late. Separate relief applies in some cases: the IRS page says the government "continues to extend the FBAR due date for certain employees or officers with signature or other authority over, but no financial interest in certain foreign financial accounts," and disaster relief is covered below. The October 15 date is the extended deadline for most 2025 individual income tax returns: Form 4868 says the IRS generally "can't extend the due date of your return for more than 6 months (October 15, 2026, for most calendar year taxpayers)." The two are separate filings that happen to share a date.

The IRS page also notes that for people affected by a natural disaster, "the government may further extend your FBAR due date," and points to FBAR relief notices for the details.

How it's filed matters:

- "You must file the FBAR electronically through FinCEN's BSA E-Filing System. You don't file the FBAR with your federal tax return."
- Paper filing requires calling FinCEN's Resource Center for an exemption from e-filing first.
- If someone else files for you, such as a tax preparer, Form 114a is the authorization, and again it stays in your records.

## Interest on the account is a separate tax question

The FBAR is an information report. It doesn't calculate any tax. The interest those foreign accounts earn is a different matter, and it goes on the regular tax return.

IRS Publication 54 states the starting point: "If you are a U.S. citizen or resident alien, your worldwide income is generally subject to U.S. income tax, regardless of where you are living." Interest from a savings account in Seoul, Guadalajara or Frankfurt is taxable interest, just like interest from a U.S. bank, whether or not the foreign bank provides a Form 1099-INT. Publication 550 adds: "You must report all your interest income even if you do not receive a Form 1099-INT."

The amounts are reported in dollars. The IRS page on foreign currency says: "You must express the amounts you report on your U.S. tax return in U.S. dollars." For the yearly average method, the IRS instruction is to "divide the foreign currency amount by the applicable yearly average exchange rate."

**Worked example: the interest.** Suppose the Korean account paid 250,000 won of interest during 2025, credited in small monthly amounts through the year (hypothetical). The general IRS rule is the rate "prevailing when you receive" the income, and the IRS also says it accepts any posted rate "that is used consistently." Using the 2025 yearly average on the IRS table, 1,421.779 won per dollar, gives 250,000 ÷ 1,421.779 = 175.84, or about $176 of taxable interest. A single large payment would be converted at the rate on the day it was paid instead. Either way, this dollar figure is for the tax return only. The FBAR reports each account's maximum value, not its interest.

Schedule B is where the foreign account shows up on the return, and the trigger is broader than the FBAR's. The Schedule B instructions say to use the schedule if "You had a financial interest in, or signature authority over, a financial account in a foreign country," even if your total interest is far below the $1,500 level that normally requires it.

The foreign-account questions in Part III of Schedule B are:

- **Line 7a, question 1:** "Check the 'Yes' box if at any time during 2025 you had a financial interest in or signature authority over a financial account located in a foreign country." The instructions add: "Check the 'Yes' box even if you aren't required to file FinCEN Form 114." In the 9,000,000-won version of the example, the answer is still yes.
- **Line 7a, question 2:** whether you're required to file FinCEN Form 114.
- **Line 7b:** "If you are required to file FinCEN Form 114, list the name(s) of the foreign country or countries in the space provided on line 7b."

Line 8, the last question in Part III, is about foreign trusts rather than accounts.

If the foreign bank withheld tax on the interest, the foreign tax credit rules may apply, and the [Instructions for Form 1116](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i1116) cover when that form is needed. Claiming the credit without Form 1116 requires all of the following: all foreign source gross income was "passive category income" (which includes most interest); "All the income and any foreign taxes paid on it were reported to you on a qualified payee statement," such as a Form 1099-INT; and total creditable foreign taxes "aren't more than $300 ($600 if married filing a joint return)." A foreign bank statement may not count as a qualified payee statement, and the Form 1116 instructions define which statements do.

How the interest is taxed once it's in dollars follows the same rules as domestic interest, which we cover in [The Tax Bite on Savings Interest: What Your Real Yield Is](/2026/08/26/the-tax-bite-on-savings-interest-what-your-real-yield-is/).

## Form 8938 is a different test with higher thresholds

Some savers confuse the FBAR with Form 8938, Statement of Specified Foreign Financial Assets. They overlap, but they are separate requirements, and filing one doesn't satisfy the other. The IRS comparison page says: "The Form 8938 filing requirement does not replace or otherwise affect a taxpayer's obligation to file FinCEN Form 114."

The main differences, from that IRS comparison page:

| | FBAR (FinCEN Form 114) | Form 8938 |
|---|---|---|
| Filed with | FinCEN, through BSA E-Filing | The income tax return |
| Threshold, single or married filing separately, living in the U.S. | Aggregate over $10,000 at any time, tested for each filer | Over $50,000 on the last day of the tax year, or over $75,000 at any time |
| Threshold, married filing jointly, living in the U.S. | No joint threshold; each spouse is tested on their own accounts | Over $100,000 on the last day, or over $150,000 at any time |
| Threshold, single or married filing separately, qualifying as living abroad | Same $10,000 test for each filer | Over $200,000 on the last day, or over $300,000 at any time |
| Threshold, married filing jointly, qualifying as living abroad | Same $10,000 test for each filer | Over $400,000 on the last day, or over $600,000 at any time |
| Due | April 15, automatic extension to Oct. 15 | With the return, including extensions |

The higher "living abroad" thresholds don't apply just because someone lives overseas. The Form 8938 instructions require a tax home in a foreign country plus a presence abroad test: "A U.S. citizen who has been a bona fide resident of a foreign country or countries for an uninterrupted period that includes an entire tax year," or "A U.S. citizen or resident who is present in a foreign country or countries at least 330 full days during any period of 12 consecutive months that ends in the tax year being reported."

The table on the same page also shows where the two differ by account type. A financial account at a foreign branch of a U.S. bank is reportable on the FBAR but not on Form 8938. An account over which you have only signature authority is reportable on the FBAR (subject to exceptions) but not on Form 8938, "unless you otherwise have an interest in the account." Foreign currency held directly, such as cash in a drawer, is reportable on neither.

For the nurse in the example, the $10,453 total triggers the FBAR. Assuming those two accounts are her only specified foreign financial assets, it is far below the Form 8938 thresholds for someone living in the U.S. Form 8938 counts some foreign assets that aren't accounts, so a household with larger holdings abroad may owe both. The Form 8938 instructions also say: "If you do not have to file an income tax return for the tax year, you do not have to file Form 8938, even if the value of your specified foreign financial assets is more than the appropriate reporting threshold."

## If October 15 passes

Missing the deadline doesn't end the obligation. The IRS FBAR page says: "Filing an FBAR late or not at all is a violation and may subject you to penalties. If the IRS hasn't contacted you about a late FBAR and you're not under civil or criminal investigation by the IRS, you should file late FBARs as soon as possible to keep potential penalties to a minimum."

The e-filing form has a place for the explanation. FinCEN's instructions say that if the report is "filed after October 15th of the year following the reporting year," the filer selects a reason from a drop-down list, or picks "other" and writes one in.

Where foreign income also went unreported on tax returns, the IRS points to compliance options such as the [Streamlined filing compliance procedures](https://www.irs.gov/individuals/international-taxpayers/streamlined-filing-compliance-procedures), which are "available to taxpayers certifying that their failure to report foreign financial assets and pay all tax due in respect of those assets did not result from willful conduct on their part." Those procedures have their own eligibility rules. One example from the IRS page: if the IRS has already started a civil examination of a taxpayer's returns for any year, the taxpayer isn't eligible.

On penalties, the Schedule B instructions summarize the base amounts in the statute: "If you are required to file FinCEN Form 114 but don't properly do so, you may have to pay a civil penalty up to $10,000. A person who willfully fails to report an account or provide account identifying information may be subject to a civil penalty equal to the greater of $100,000 or 50% of the balance in the account at the time of the violation. Willful violations may also be subject to criminal penalties." The IRS FBAR page adds that the civil maximums "are adjusted annually for inflation" and that "Assertion of penalties depends on facts and circumstances." FinCEN's penalty table at [31 CFR 1010.821](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-31/subtitle-B/chapter-X/part-1010/subpart-H/section-1010.821), as it read in October 2026, lists adjusted maximums of $16,536 for a non-willful violation and $165,353 for the fixed-dollar willful amount, for penalties assessed on or after January 17, 2025. How a late report is handled depends on the facts, including whether income from the accounts also went unreported and whether a compliance option applies.

## Records to keep

For each account on an FBAR, the IRS page lists what must be kept:

- Name on the account
- Account number
- Name and address of the foreign bank
- Type of account
- Maximum value during the year

"The law doesn't specify the type of document to keep with this information. Documents may include bank statements or a copy of a filed FBAR if they have the required information." The retention period, in the IRS page's words: "Generally, you must keep these records for five years from the due date of the FBAR." The FinCEN instructions put it as 5 years "from April 15th of the year following the calendar year reported, or the date filed if after April 15th," so a late report starts the clock on the date it is filed.

The FinCEN instructions allow periodic statements to establish the maximum value when they "fairly reflect the maximum account value during the calendar year."

## Starting the count for 2026

Calendar year 2026 is already a reporting year. Under the same rule, the FBAR for accounts held during 2026 is due April 15, 2027, with the automatic extension to October 15, 2027. The 2026 count will use Treasury's exchange rates for December 31, 2026, which aren't published yet, so the dollar value of a foreign balance can change between now and then even if the balance doesn't.

Three features of the rules matter for the 2026 count:

- The test uses each account's peak, not its year-end balance. A one-time transfer that briefly pushed an account up counts.
- Money moving between your own foreign accounts can raise the aggregate, because the instructions add each account's maximum, not unique dollars.
- Signature authority counts on its own, so an account you can sign on, such as a parent's account where you're a co-signer, is part of the count even with no ownership.

Separately, the tax return needs the dates and amounts of interest paid abroad so they can be converted to dollars.

Here is the moving-money case in numbers. Say 8,000,000 won sits in Account A until June, then is moved to Account B and stays there. Each account had a maximum of 8,000,000 won during the year, so the aggregate of maximums is 16,000,000 won. At the December 31, 2025 rate, each account converts to 8,000,000 ÷ 1,443.75 = 5,541.13, rounded up to $5,542, for an aggregate of $11,084. That is over the threshold even though the saver never had more than about $5,542 abroad at any one time. Nothing in the instructions removes the double count, so that is how the test works.

## Common questions

**Do I need to file if my foreign account paid no interest?**

The income doesn't matter. The IRS says whether the account "produced taxable income has no effect on whether the account is a foreign financial account for FBAR purposes." The test is the $10,000 aggregate of maximum values.

**Is the FBAR filed with my tax return?**

No. It's filed electronically through FinCEN's BSA E-Filing System. The IRS page says "You don't file the FBAR with your federal tax return." What appears on the return is the Schedule B Part III question and, if required, Form 8938.

**I'm a green card holder. Does this apply to me?**

The FinCEN instructions include "United States residents" in the definition of U.S. person and say to apply the tax residency tests in 26 U.S.C. section 7701(b). The IRS [FBAR Reference Guide (Publication 5569)](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/p5569.pdf) gives the example of a U.K. citizen who is a permanent legal resident of the U.S. and elects treaty residence in the U.K.: he is still "a U.S. person for FBAR purposes," because "Tax treaties with the U.S. do not affect FBAR filing obligations." If your residency status changed during the year, the residency tests are the place to start.

**My account abroad is at a branch of a U.S. bank. Is it foreign?**

Yes. The FinCEN instructions say an account "maintained with a branch of a United States bank that is physically located outside of the United States is a foreign financial account."

**Does an FBAR mean I owe tax?**

No. The FBAR reports accounts; it doesn't compute tax. Any tax comes from the income, mainly interest, which is reported on your return in dollars.

**Can my spouse file one report for both of us?**

Only if all the foreign accounts the non-filing spouse is required to report are jointly owned with the filing spouse, the filing spouse reports them on a timely filed FBAR, and both sign Form 114a. Filing status on the tax return doesn't change this.

The FBAR is a report, not a tax. What matters this week is the date: October 15, 2026, for calendar year 2025 accounts. For anything that doesn't fit the common cases, such as entity accounts, signature authority through an employer or years of missed reports, the FinCEN instructions and the IRS FBAR Hotline listed on the IRS page are the places to confirm the details.
