![Savings Interest and the Jan. 15, 2027 Estimated Tax: The $1,000 Test](/assets/pin-images/2026-10-07-savings-interest-and-the-jan-15-2027-estimated-tax-the-1000-test.png)

A saver who moved a large cash balance into a high-yield account or a run of CDs this year may be looking at thousands of dollars of 2026 interest with nothing set aside for the tax on it. Wages come with withholding; savings interest generally does not, and the IRS lists interest among the kinds of income that can call for estimated tax payments. Three of the four 2026 installment dates have already passed. In short: if you expect to owe at least $1,000 for 2026 after withholding and credits, and your withholding and credits will fall short of the smaller of 90% of your 2026 tax or 100% of your 2025 tax (110% if your 2025 AGI was more than $150,000, or $75,000 if you are married filing separately for 2026), you have an estimated tax requirement. Three options remain: a payment by Friday, Jan. 15, 2027, more withholding from your remaining 2026 paychecks, or filing the full return by Jan. 31, 2027, and paying the balance with it. Only the withholding route can reach back to the April, June and September periods, because of how the IRS dates each kind of payment.

The rules come from five IRS pages. [Publication 505 (2026), Tax Withholding and Estimated Tax](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p505) sets the thresholds, due dates and the January filing alternative. The IRS [Estimated taxes](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/estimated-taxes) page states the same tests in shorter form and names interest as a trigger. The [Instructions for Form 2210 (2025)](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i2210) explain how withholding is dated and how payments are applied when the penalty is figured. The news release [Interest rates remain the same for the fourth quarter of 2026](https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/interest-rates-remain-the-same-for-the-fourth-quarter-of-2026) gives the current underpayment rate. And [About Form 1040-ES](https://www.irs.gov/forms-pubs/about-form-1040-es) is where the payment form and its corrections are posted. This is general information about how the rules work, not advice on any individual return.

## Why savings interest creates an estimated tax gap

The federal system expects tax to be paid during the year. The Estimated taxes page puts it plainly: "Taxes must be paid as you earn or receive income during the year, either through withholding or estimated tax payments." It then names where withholding tends to fall short: "If the amount of income tax withheld from your salary or pension is not enough, or if you receive income such as interest, dividends, alimony, self-employment income, capital gains, prizes and awards, you may have to make estimated tax payments."

A salaried saver's withholding was most likely set up around wages. When interest grows from a few hundred dollars to several thousand in one year, withholding does not follow, and the tax on the extra interest builds up as a balance due. That is a gap, not yet a penalty. It becomes a penalty problem only if the shortfall crosses the thresholds below and nothing is done before the deadlines.

Two related topics are covered elsewhere on this site. Publication 505 notes that backup withholding "can apply to most kinds of payments that are reported on Form 1099," including interest; that separate mechanism is explained in [this site's post on backup withholding on savings interest](/2026/09/21/backup-withholding-on-savings-interest-why-the-bank-took-24/). Which year CD interest counts in, including interest on a multiyear CD you cannot yet withdraw, is covered in [Why you owe tax on a 5-year CD you cannot touch yet](/2026/09/25/why-you-owe-tax-on-a-5-year-cd-you-cannot-touch-yet/). This post starts from the point where you know your 2026 interest and need to know whether the tax on it must be paid before you file.

## The two tests and the 110% rule

Publication 505 states the general rule in two parts. You must pay estimated tax for 2026 if both apply:

1. "You expect to owe at least $1,000 in tax for 2026 after subtracting your withholding and tax credits."
2. "You expect your withholding and tax credits to be less than the smaller of: 90% of the tax to be shown on your 2026 tax return, or 100% of the tax shown on your 2025 tax return. Your 2025 tax return must cover all 12 months."

Both must be true. A saver who will owe $3,000 at filing, is not subject to the 110% rule below, and whose withholding already covers 100% of last year's tax fails the second test and has no estimated tax requirement under this rule, even though a balance will be due in April. A saver well short of last year's tax who will owe only $600 fails the first test.

For savers, the details matter.

**The $1,000 is tax, not income.** Interest of $1,000 triggers nothing by itself. The question is whether your 2026 tax, after subtracting withholding and credits, reaches $1,000. Publication 505's flowchart adds: "Don't subtract any estimated tax payments."

**The prior-year test is already fixed.** It uses the tax shown on the 2025 return, which does not move when 2026 interest goes up. For a saver whose income jumped this year, that fixed number is often the easier target because it is smaller than 90% of the new, higher 2026 tax.

**"Whichever is smaller" works in your favor.** The Estimated taxes page frames it the same way: most taxpayers avoid the penalty if they paid "at least 90% of the tax for the current year, or 100% of the tax shown on the return for the prior year, whichever is smaller." Once withholding and credits reach the lower figure, the general rule no longer requires estimated payments, however much interest arrives late in the year.

Higher-income households get a tougher prior-year target. Publication 505 says: "If your AGI for 2025 was more than $150,000 ($75,000 if your filing status for 2026 is Married filing a separate return), substitute 110% for 100%" in the prior-year part of the general rule. It names the line: "For 2025, AGI is the amount shown on Form 1040 or 1040-SR, line 11b." The test looks at 2025 AGI, so a household that first crosses $150,000 this year because of interest still uses 100% for 2026. The trigger is "more than," so AGI of exactly $150,000 stays at 100% unless you are married filing separately. And the $75,000 line depends on 2026 filing status, not 2025.

There is also a full exemption. You don't have to pay estimated tax for 2026 if you had no tax liability for 2025, were a U.S. citizen or resident alien for the whole year, and your 2025 tax year covered a 12-month period. Publication 505 defines no tax liability as a 2025 total tax of zero, or not having been required to file. Someone who had little income last year and lives on savings interest this year should check that first. Special rules apply to farmers and fishermen; they are outside this post.

| Your situation | Prior-year target | Current-year target | Withholding and credits must reach |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2025 AGI of $150,000 or less (not married filing separately) | 100% of 2025 tax | 90% of 2026 tax | The smaller of the two |
| 2025 AGI more than $150,000 | 110% of 2025 tax | 90% of 2026 tax | The smaller of the two |
| Married filing separately for 2026, 2025 AGI more than $75,000 | 110% of 2025 tax | 90% of 2026 tax | The smaller of the two |
| No 2025 tax liability, citizen or resident all year, 12-month 2025 tax year | Not applicable | Not applicable | No estimated tax required |
| Expect to owe less than $1,000 after withholding and credits | Not applicable | Not applicable | No estimated tax required |

Source: Publication 505 (2026).

## Worked example: a saver under the $150,000 line

The numbers here are hypothetical and chosen to keep the arithmetic easy to follow.

A single filer moved $200,000 into savings early in 2026. At a hypothetical 4.5% yield, that produces $9,000 of interest for the year: $200,000 × 0.045 = $9,000. The 2025 return shows total tax of $14,000 and AGI of $120,000, so the 100% target applies. A projection for 2026, including the interest, shows total tax of $16,000. Withholding for all of 2026 is projected at $13,000.

**Test 1:** $16,000 − $13,000 = $3,000 expected at filing. That is at least $1,000, so the test is met.

**Test 2:**

- 90% of 2026 tax: $16,000 × 0.90 = $14,400
- 100% of 2025 tax: $14,000
- The smaller amount is $14,000, and $13,000 of withholding falls short of it.

Both tests are met, so estimated tax is required. The shortfall against the safe harbor is $14,000 − $13,000 = $1,000. That is not the $3,000 the filer will owe. The rule only asks the filer to reach the safe harbor during the year; the other $2,000 can be paid with the return without triggering the estimated tax rules, as long as the $14,000 target is met in time.

Now spread the target across the year. Publication 505 says the regular installment method divides the annual amount by 4, and the Form 2210 instructions say that for most taxpayers the required installment is "one-fourth of the required annual payment."

- Required installment per period: $14,000 ÷ 4 = $3,500
- Withholding credited per period: $13,000 ÷ 4 = $3,250
- Shortfall per period: $3,500 − $3,250 = $250
- Four periods × $250 = $1,000

That per-period view decides which fix works best.

## Where the 2026 calendar stands now

Publication 505 lists the 2026 payment periods and due dates. Three are in the past.

| Payment period | 2026 due date | Status on Oct. 7, 2026 |
|---|---|---|
| Jan. 1 – March 31 | April 15, 2026 | Passed |
| April 1 – May 31 | June 15, 2026 | Passed |
| June 1 – Aug. 31 | Sept. 15, 2026 | Passed |
| Sept. 1 – Dec. 31 | Jan. 15, 2027 | Open |

Source: Publication 505 (2026).

Publication 505's Table 2-1 adds that if income subject to estimated tax first arises "After Aug. 31," the only payment is due Jan. 15 of the next year.

The publication is clear that "The penalty is figured separately for each payment period," and it warns: "If you figure your payments using the regular installment method and later refigure your payments because of an increase in income, you may be charged a penalty for underpayment of estimated tax for the period(s) before you changed your payments."

For a saver who has made no estimated payments, that warning applies directly. Paying everything on Jan. 15 handles the fourth period but does not reach back to April, June or September. The Form 2210 instructions explain how late money is treated: "Your payments are applied first to any underpayment balance on an earlier installment even if you designate a payment for a later period," and "The penalty is figured for the number of days that each underpayment remains unpaid." A January payment ends the clock on earlier shortfalls; it does not erase the time they were outstanding.

If a due date falls on a Saturday, Sunday or legal holiday, Publication 505 says a payment is on time if made on the next day that isn't one. Jan. 15, 2027, is a Friday.

## Why more withholding can cover earlier quarters

Withholding is dated differently from estimated payments, and for anyone with a paycheck this is the most useful rule in the whole topic.

The Form 2210 instructions state: "For withheld federal income tax and excess social security or tier 1 RRTA, you are considered to have paid one-fourth of these amounts on each payment due date unless you can show otherwise."

Withholding from a December paycheck is not treated as a December payment. By default it is spread evenly across all four 2026 due dates, including the three that have passed. An estimated payment is dated the day it is made.

Back to the worked example, with $250 short in each period:

**Fix 1: a $1,000 estimated payment on Jan. 15, 2027.** Applied first to the earliest shortfall, it leaves the April 15 shortfall of $250 unpaid from April 15, 2026, to Jan. 15, 2027. The June and September shortfalls stay unpaid from their own due dates to Jan. 15. Only the fourth $250 is on time.

**Fix 2: an extra $1,000 of withholding from the remaining 2026 paychecks.** Total withholding becomes $13,000 + $1,000 = $14,000. Treated as paid one-fourth on each due date, that is $14,000 ÷ 4 = $3,500 per period, which equals the $3,500 required installment. Shortfall per period: $0.

The same $1,000 clears all four periods. Both IRS pages also describe the employer route for wage earners. The Estimated taxes page says: "If you receive salaries and wages, you can avoid having to pay estimated tax by asking your employer to withhold more tax from your earnings. To do this, file a new Form W-4 with your employer. There is a special line on Form W-4 for you to enter the additional amount you want your employer to withhold." It also points to the IRS Tax Withholding Estimator for checking the amount.

Practical notes on this route:

- **The payroll calendar limits it.** The extra amount has to come out of 2026 paychecks. Divide the target by the pay dates left and enter that per-paycheck figure on the new W-4. A W-4 filed in late December may reach one paycheck or none.
- **The default helps.** The Form 2210 instructions let you date withholding by when it was actually withheld instead, but that is an election: "If you treat withholding as paid on the dates it was actually withheld for estimated tax purposes, you must check box D in Part II and complete and attach Form 2210 to your return." Someone adding withholding late in the year generally benefits from the even-spread default.
- **Pensions count too.** The Estimated taxes page refers to tax "withheld from your salary or pension," and the Form 2210 rule covers withheld federal income tax in general. Publication 505 explains that pension withholding is set by the Form W-4P (or similar form) you gave the payer and stays in effect unless you submit a new Form W-4P, so a retiree can raise it with a new form.

## Paying by Jan. 15 or filing by Jan. 31

Publication 505 offers a second way to handle the fourth installment: "If you file your 2026 Form 1040 or 1040-SR by January 31, 2027, and pay the rest of the tax you owe, you don't need to make the payment due on January 15, 2027."

Its example: "You do not pay any estimated tax for 2026. You file the 2026 income tax return and pay the balance due shown on the return on January 26, 2027. Your estimated tax for the fourth payment period is considered to have been paid on time. However, you may owe a penalty for not making the first three estimated tax payments, if required. Any penalty for not making those payments will be figured up to January 26, 2027."

So this route replaces the Jan. 15 payment and stops the clock on the first three periods when you file and pay. It requires a complete return and full payment, and it does not cure those earlier periods.

For savers, the obstacle is paperwork. Filing by Jan. 31 means having accurate figures for all 2026 income, including interest from every account, in the last weeks of January. If you won't have those numbers by late January, the Jan. 15 payment is the more workable path. Also note that Jan. 31, 2027, falls on a Sunday, and Publication 505's weekend rule is written for estimated payment due dates. Anyone relying on this alternative should file a few days early rather than test how the weekend date is treated.

For the Jan. 15 payment itself, the Estimated taxes page says: "You may send estimated tax payments with Form 1040-ES by mail, or you can pay online, by phone or from your mobile device using the IRS app," and you can pay through your online account, "where you can see your payment history." Anyone mailing a voucher should check the About Form 1040-ES page, which lists a "Correction to the mailing addresses in the 2026 Form 1040-ES -- 23-FEB-2026" and a "Correction to state and local income tax deduction amount in the 2026 Form 1040-ES -- 25-FEB-2026." A copy saved before late February may not reflect them.

| Route | What it covers | Main requirement | Main limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Extra withholding from remaining 2026 pay | All four periods, one-fourth each by default | A paycheck or pension with withholding | Limited by pay dates left in 2026 |
| Estimated payment by Jan. 15, 2027 | Fourth period on time; applied first to earlier shortfalls | Payment by the due date | Earlier shortfalls count as unpaid until paid |
| File and pay all tax by Jan. 31, 2027 | Fourth period treated as paid on time | Complete return and full payment | Earlier shortfalls count as unpaid until filing; needs all forms early |
| Annualized income installment method | Can lower required installments for early periods | Form 2210 with the return | More calculation; depends on when income arrived |

Sources: Publication 505 (2026); Instructions for Form 2210 (2025).

The routes can be combined, such as extra withholding plus a smaller Jan. 15 payment.

## Interest that arrived late: the annualized method

The regular installment method assumes income arrives evenly; Publication 505 says to use it "only if your income is basically the same throughout the year." Savings interest often doesn't. Interest on a new account builds only after the deposit, so money moved into savings partway through the year produces most of its interest in the later months. (Which year CD interest belongs to is a separate question, covered in the CD post linked above.)

For uneven income, Publication 505 describes the annualized income installment method, which "annualizes your tax at the end of each period based on a reasonable estimate of your income, deductions, and other items relating to events that occurred from the beginning of the tax year through the end of the period." The Estimated taxes page summarizes the benefit: "if your income is received unevenly during the year, you may be able to avoid or lower the penalty by annualizing your income and making unequal payments."

Each installment is measured against income from Jan. 1 through the end of that period: March 31, May 31, Aug. 31 and Dec. 31. Interest that showed up after Aug. 31 sits outside the first three measuring periods. That has two effects:

- **Early installments can be smaller.** If most of the year's interest came in the fall, the tax figured on income through August can be lower than an even one-fourth share, and the required installments for those periods shrink.
- **The late income still has to be covered.** The method shifts the requirement to the period in which the income arrived, which for late-year interest is the Jan. 15 period.

The method adds paperwork. Publication 505: "If you use the annualized income installment method to figure your estimated tax payments, you must file Form 2210 with your 2026 tax return." In the Form 2210 instructions the method is Schedule AI, and they say it "may be to your benefit to figure your required installments by using the annualized income installment method."

One pattern where annualizing helps is little extra income in the first eight months and most of it after. It generally does less for someone whose interest built steadily from January, because that income was present in every period. For that saver, extra withholding is usually the cleaner fix.

## Worked example: no withholding to adjust

Also hypothetical. A retired single filer lives on savings and has no paycheck and no withholding. The 2025 return shows total tax of $6,000 and AGI under $150,000. Projected 2026 tax is $9,000. No estimated payments have been made.

**Test 1:** $9,000 − $0 = $9,000 owed after withholding, which is at least $1,000.

**Test 2:** 90% of 2026 tax is $9,000 × 0.90 = $8,100. 100% of 2025 tax is $6,000. The smaller is $6,000, and $0 of withholding falls short.

Estimated tax is required. Under the regular method, each installment is $6,000 ÷ 4 = $1,500.

| Due date | Required | Paid by due date | Shortfall |
|---|---|---|---|
| April 15, 2026 | $1,500 | $0 | $1,500 |
| June 15, 2026 | $1,500 | $0 | $1,500 |
| Sept. 15, 2026 | $1,500 | $0 | $1,500 |
| Jan. 15, 2027 | $1,500 | Depends on route | Depends on route |

The first three shortfalls total $1,500 × 3 = $4,500, and they already exist. With no withholding to spread, nothing done in October can backdate a payment. What the filer still controls:

- **Pay sooner rather than later.** Because penalty time runs for "the number of days that each underpayment remains unpaid," paying the $4,500 now instead of in January shortens the count for all three periods. The Estimated taxes page notes that paying weekly, biweekly or monthly is fine "as long as you've paid enough in by the end of the quarter."
- **Cover the fourth installment** by Jan. 15, 2027, or file and pay everything by Jan. 31, 2027.
- **Check Schedule AI** if most of the 2026 interest arrived late in the year.

This post does not put a dollar figure on any penalty; the Form 2210 worksheet for the 2026 return does that.

## What the 7% rate means for waiting

The IRS sets its interest rates quarterly. For the quarter that began Oct. 1, 2026, it announced that rates would stay the same: "For individuals, the rate for overpayments and underpayments will be 7% per year, compounded daily." The release explains the formula: "For taxpayers other than corporations, the overpayment and underpayment rate is the federal short-term rate plus 3 percentage points," computed "from the federal short-term rate determined during July 2026."

The Form 2210 instructions describe the penalty worksheet as "applying the appropriate rate against each underpayment" for the days it remains unpaid. In the 2025 worksheet, each rate period's line multiplies the underpayment by the number of days, divided by 365, and by that period's rate. The rate is applied separately to each rate period an underpayment spans.

That makes the cost of a shortfall depend on time and on the rate for each period it spans. A shortfall from April 15 that is paid in October has run for fewer days than the same shortfall paid on Jan. 15.

The money that could pay the tax often sits in the same high-yield account that created the problem. Leaving it there until April means weighing that account's yield against a penalty figured at the IRS rate for each period. Rates for later quarters are announced separately, so this post does not assume them.

## A decision checklist for October

A saver with substantial 2026 interest can work through these in order:

1. **Pull the 2025 numbers.** Total tax and AGI (Form 1040 or 1040-SR, line 11b). If AGI was more than $150,000, or more than $75,000 when married filing separately for 2026, the prior-year target is 110%.
2. **Check the no-liability exemption** described above.
3. **Project 2026 tax and withholding,** including all interest expected through Dec. 31.
4. **Run the two tests.** Is the expected balance after withholding and credits at least $1,000? Are withholding and credits below the smaller of 90% of 2026 tax and 100% or 110% of 2025 tax?
5. **Measure the gap against the safe harbor,** not the full balance due.
6. **Look at withholding if you have a paycheck or pension,** since it is treated as paid one-fourth on each due date by default.
7. **Cover what is left** with a Jan. 15 payment, or file and pay in full by Jan. 31, 2027.
8. **Consider Schedule AI** if the interest arrived mostly after Aug. 31.
9. **Reset withholding in January** if 2026 was a one-time spike.

Anyone with self-employment income, large capital gains or a filing status change should check with a tax professional, because the general rule is only one part of Publication 505.

## Common questions

**Does $1,000 of savings interest mean I have to pay estimated tax?**

No. The $1,000 threshold in Publication 505 applies to tax owed after withholding and credits, not to the amount of interest. A saver earning several thousand dollars of interest may still owe far less than $1,000 at filing if wage withholding covers most of the tax. Even with an expected balance of $1,000 or more, estimated tax is required only if withholding and credits also fall below the smaller of 90% of 2026 tax or 100% (or 110%) of 2025 tax.

**I missed the April, June and September dates. Can I still fix those quarters?**

Not with an estimated payment, because a payment is dated when it is made and is applied first to the earliest shortfall. Withholding works differently. The Form 2210 instructions treat withheld federal income tax as paid one-fourth on each due date unless you show otherwise. Adding withholding from your remaining 2026 paychecks can cover earlier periods in a way a January payment cannot.

**If I file early, do I still need to send the Jan. 15 payment?**

Not if you file your complete 2026 Form 1040 or 1040-SR by Jan. 31, 2027, and pay all the tax you owe, according to Publication 505. The fourth installment is then treated as paid on time. A penalty can still apply to the first three periods if those payments were required, figured up to the date you file and pay. Filing that early also means having accurate interest figures for every account.

**My 2025 AGI was just under $150,000. Do I use 100% or 110%?**

Publication 505 switches to 110% only if 2025 AGI was "more than $150,000," or more than $75,000 if your 2026 filing status is married filing separately. AGI at or below the line keeps the 100% figure. Check line 11b of your 2025 Form 1040 or 1040-SR, not projected 2026 income. A jump in 2026 interest does not change the multiplier for 2026.

**Should I use the annualized method?**

It is worth checking if most of your extra interest arrived late in the year. The method measures each installment against income through the end of that period, so interest received after Aug. 31 does not raise earlier installments. It requires attaching Form 2210 with Schedule AI to your 2026 return. If your interest built up evenly from January, it usually changes little, and extra withholding or a prompt payment is simpler.

**Will the IRS tell me if I owe a penalty?**

Generally, yes. The Form 2210 instructions say the IRS will generally figure the penalty and send a bill, and that you should not file Form 2210 in that case. The exceptions include checking box B, C or D in Part II, such as when using the annualized method or dating withholding as actually withheld. A waiver can apply in limited circumstances listed on the Estimated taxes page, which points to the Form 2210 instructions for how to request one; those instructions say to check box A or box B in Part II, and with box A you attach only page 1 of Form 2210.

The prior-year target is fixed by last year's return, withholding can still be added from this year's pay, and both the Jan. 15 payment and the Jan. 31 filing alternative remain open. For shortfalls covered by a direct payment rather than withholding, the penalty count runs until the day the money is paid.
