The rates the IRS uses to test family and other private loans rose for November by 0.30 to 0.47 percentage point. Revenue Ruling 2026-21, which the IRS had posted on its website as of Oct. 26, sets the November 2026 applicable federal rates, or AFRs: 4.67% for short-term loans, 5.08% for mid-term loans and 5.52% for long-term loans, all stated with annual compounding. The October figures in Revenue Ruling 2026-19 were 4.25%, 4.61% and 5.22%. The short-term rate rose 0.42 percentage point in one month.

For anyone thinking about lending savings to a child, a parent or another relative, the AFR is the benchmark. Under section 7872 of the tax code, a loan that charges less than the AFR is a "below-market loan." For a loan between family members, the interest the lender didn't charge can be treated as a gift to the borrower and as interest income to the lender, even though no interest changed hands. Two exceptions, one at $10,000 and one at $100,000, remove or limit those effects for many smaller family loans, but each has conditions.

For a household that keeps cash in savings accounts and CDs, the comparison is also unusual this month. In the FDIC's national rate table dated October 19, 2026, the average rate on a 12-month CD was 1.76% and the average on savings was 0.38%. A family loan made in November at the minimum short-term AFR would carry a higher rate than either average. That doesn't make a loan a better place for money. A loan to a relative isn't insured and can go unpaid, and the tax rules apply while it is outstanding.

## The November 2026 rates

Rev. Rul. 2026-21 opens by saying it "provides various prescribed rates for federal income tax purposes for November 2026 (the current month)." Table 1 gives the short-term, mid-term and long-term AFRs, each in four versions depending on how often interest compounds.

| Rate | October 2026, annual | November 2026, annual | November 2026, semiannual |
|---|---|---|---|
| Short-term AFR | 4.25% | 4.67% | 4.62% |
| Mid-term AFR | 4.61% | 5.08% | 5.02% |
| Long-term AFR | 5.22% | 5.52% | 5.45% |

The columns aren't different rates in any economic sense. They are the same yield stated with different compounding. A rate of 5.02% compounded twice a year works out to about 5.08% over a full year, which is the annual figure in the table. For family loans the semiannual column is the one the statute names. Section 7872 says the applicable federal rate for a term loan is the rate in effect "as of the day on which the loan was made," "compounded semiannually," and it uses the same compounding for demand loans. The other columns state the same rate for loans that compound annually, quarterly or monthly.

The same ruling sets the section 7520 rate, used to value annuities, life interests and remainders, at 6.20% for November, up from 5.60% in October. That rate matters mostly for estate planning and isn't covered further here.

Where the AFRs come from explains the increase. Section 1274(d) says the short-term rate "shall be the rate determined by the Secretary based on the average market yield (during any 1-month period selected by the Secretary and ending in the calendar month in which the determination is made) on outstanding marketable obligations of the United States with remaining periods to maturity of 3 years or less." The mid-term and long-term rates follow the same principle for longer Treasury maturities. The AFRs are Treasury yields, measured over a recent month, and they move when Treasury yields move.

The statute also sets the calendar: "During each calendar month, the Secretary shall determine the Federal short-term rate, mid-term rate, and long-term rate which shall apply during the following calendar month." The November rates apply to loans made from Nov. 1 through Nov. 30, 2026. December's rates will come in a later ruling.

The short-term rate has risen 1.04 percentage points since January, and the 0.42-point rise from October to November is the largest one-month move in that list. Most earlier monthly changes were less than a tenth of a point; the next largest was a 0.23-point rise from April to May.

The history matters because of how the two kinds of loans use the rates. A five-year term loan made in January 2026 was tested against January's mid-term rate, 3.77% with semiannual compounding, and keeps that rate for its whole term. A demand loan has no locked rate. For a demand loan with a fixed balance outstanding all year, the IRS uses a single "blended annual rate," and Rev. Rul. 2026-12 sets it at 3.82% for 2026. A demand loan made in November enters the rate calendar at November's short-term rate, which is well above that 2026 blended figure.

## Which rate applies to which loan

Section 1274(d) sorts loans by term. A debt with a term "Not over 3 years" uses the short-term rate. One "Over 3 years but not over 9 years" uses the mid-term rate. One "Over 9 years" uses the long-term rate.

Section 7872 then splits loans into two kinds, and the kind decides how long a given month's rate sticks.

- **Term loans.** A term loan is "any loan which is not a demand loan," meaning it has a set repayment schedule. For a term loan the AFR is fixed as of the day the loan is made. A five-year loan made in November 2026 is measured against the November mid-term rate for its whole life, even if rates fall later.
- **Demand loans.** A demand loan is "any loan which is payable in full at any time on the demand of the lender." For a demand loan the test rate is "the Federal short-term rate in effect under section 1274(d) for the period for which the amount of forgone interest is being determined." It isn't locked at the loan date. The IRS's 1985 proposed regulations describe two methods: for a demand loan "of a fixed principal amount that remains outstanding for an entire calendar year," forgone interest uses the blended annual rate, which they say "will be published annually by the Commissioner"; for a loan outstanding for only part of the year, a separate formula uses the short-term rates for the periods involved.

A loan with no repayment schedule, which the lender can call at any time, fits the demand-loan definition. Under the statute's definitions, the way the loan is structured decides which rate applies.

## What makes a loan "below-market"

The statute's definition depends on the loan type. A demand loan is below-market if "interest is payable on the loan at a rate less than the applicable Federal rate." A term loan is below-market if "the amount loaned exceeds the present value of all payments due under the loan." For a term loan the present value is figured using the AFR as the discount rate. A zero-interest loan and a loan at 2% when the AFR is above 5% are both below-market. So is a loan at 4% made in November, when the short-term semiannual AFR is 4.62%.

The measure of the shortfall is "forgone interest," which the statute defines as the interest that "would have been payable on the loan for the period if interest accrued on the loan at the applicable Federal rate and were payable annually," minus the interest actually payable for that period.

Publication 550 puts it in plain terms. Forgone interest is "The amount of interest that would be payable for that period if interest accrued on the loan at the applicable federal rate and was payable annually on December 31, minus Any interest actually payable on the loan for the period."

## How a family gift loan is taxed

Section 7872 applies to several kinds of below-market loans, including loans between employers and employees and between corporations and shareholders. For families the relevant one is the gift loan: "any below-market loan where the forgoing of interest is in the nature of a gift."

For gift loans and demand loans, the statute creates two transfers that never happen in cash. The forgone interest is treated as "(A) transferred from the lender to the borrower, and (B) retransferred by the borrower to the lender as interest." The timing is fixed: forgone interest for a year is treated as transferred "on the last day of such calendar year," Dec. 31.

Publication 550 describes what follows. The lender is treated as having made a loan at the AFR plus "An additional payment to the borrower in an amount equal to the forgone interest. The borrower generally is treated as transferring the additional payment back to the lender as interest. The lender must report that amount as interest income." In a family setting, the lender's deemed payment to the borrower is a gift. For a demand gift loan, that gift is the forgone interest for each year. A term gift loan is handled differently for gift tax: the gift is measured once, up front, as described below, and the yearly income tax imputation isn't an additional gift. The Form 709 instructions list the same situation among transfers the gift tax can reach: "The gift tax may also apply to forgiving a debt, to making an interest-free or below-market interest rate loan."

So a parent who lends a child money at no interest can face two things. The parent may have taxable interest income that was never received. On a demand loan, the parent is also treated as making a gift equal to the forgone interest, which counts toward the annual gift tax exclusion discussed below. The borrower's side can matter too. Publication 550 says the borrower "may be able to deduct that foregone interest as an expense if the loan proceeds are used for business or investment." Money used for personal purposes is a different case: section 163(h) says that for individuals "no deduction shall be allowed under this chapter for personal interest," with exceptions such as qualified residence interest.

**A worked example.** A parent lends a grown child $50,000 at no interest as a demand loan in November 2026. For 2026, forgone interest covers only the weeks from the loan date to Dec. 31, under the part-year method. For 2027, with the full $50,000 outstanding all year, forgone interest would be the 2027 blended annual rate times $50,000. That rate hasn't been published, so take a hypothetical round 4%: forgone interest would be $2,000. Without an exception, the parent would report $2,000 of interest income for 2027 and be treated as giving the child $2,000 on Dec. 31, 2027. The exceptions below change that picture for a loan this size.

## The $10,000 exception

Section 7872(c)(2) takes small family loans out of the rules entirely: "In the case of any gift loan directly between individuals, this section shall not apply to any day on which the aggregate outstanding amount of loans between such individuals does not exceed $10,000."

Three limits come with it.

- **The total counts.** The test is the "aggregate outstanding amount of loans between such individuals" on a given day. Two $6,000 loans to the same person add up to $12,000, which is over the line.
- **Income-producing assets are excluded.** The exception "shall not apply to any gift loan directly attributable to the purchase or carrying of income-producing assets." Lending a relative $8,000 to buy a car can fit. Lending $8,000 that the relative puts into a CD or a brokerage account doesn't.
- **A married couple counts as one.** The statute says "A husband and wife shall be treated as 1 person," so a loan from each spouse to the same child is aggregated, and so are loans to a married couple. The same aggregation applies to the $100,000 rule below.

Term loans have one more rule. Section 7872(f)(10) says: "If this section applies to any term loan on any day, this section shall continue to apply to such loan notwithstanding paragraphs (2) and (3) of subsection (c). In the case of a gift loan, the preceding sentence shall only apply for purposes of chapter 12." So once a term gift loan has been above $10,000, paying it down to $10,000 or less doesn't take it back out of the gift tax rules. Publication 550 compresses this into a shorter statement: "This exception does not apply to term loans. The general below-market loan rules will continue to apply even if the outstanding balance is reduced to $10,000 or less."

**A worked example.** A grandparent lends a grandchild $8,000, interest-free and repayable on demand, to cover a car purchase. No other loans are outstanding between them. On every day the balance stays at or below $10,000, section 7872 doesn't apply, so there is no imputed interest and no deemed gift from the forgone interest. If the grandparent later lends another $5,000 for any purpose, the total reaches $13,000 and the exception stops applying on those days.

## The $100,000 rule and the borrower's investment income

For larger family loans, section 7872(d)(1) limits the income tax effect instead of removing it. "For purposes of subtitle A, in the case of a gift loan directly between individuals, the amount treated as retransferred by the borrower to the lender as of the close of any year shall not exceed the borrower's net investment income for such year."

In plain terms, the interest income the lender has to report for the year can't be more than the borrower's net investment income for that year. And there is a floor: "If the net investment income of any borrower for any year does not exceed $1,000, the net investment income of such borrower for such year shall be treated as zero." A borrower with $1,000 or less of net investment income produces no imputed interest for the lender. If the borrower has more than one gift loan outstanding, the statute says the borrower's net investment income "shall be allocated among such loans," so two relatives can't each use the full amount.

Three conditions limit the rule.

- **The $100,000 ceiling.** It "shall not apply to any loan made by a lender to a borrower for any day on which the aggregate outstanding amount of loans between the borrower and lender exceeds $100,000."
- **Tax avoidance.** It doesn't apply "to any loan the interest arrangements of which have as 1 of their principal purposes the avoidance of any Federal tax."
- **Income tax only.** The limit is "For purposes of subtitle A," the income tax part of the code. It doesn't shrink the deemed gift. For gift tax purposes, the forgone interest on a demand loan is still treated as transferred to the borrower.

Net investment income has the meaning in section 163(d)(4): "the excess of— (i) investment income, over (ii) investment expenses." Investment income there starts with "gross income from property held for investment," which generally includes interest income such as interest from savings accounts and CDs. Qualified dividends count "only to the extent the taxpayer elects to treat such income as investment income," and net capital gain is handled the same way, by election. For a borrower whose only investment income is bank interest, net investment income is close to the interest on Form 1099-INT, less any deductible investment expenses.

Section 7872 adds a detail that matters to savers: interest that would accrue on certain "deferred payment obligations" counts as if received. It names them: "The term 'deferred payment obligation' includes any market discount bond, short-term obligation, United States savings bond, annuity, or similar obligation." A borrower who holds savings bonds and doesn't report their interest each year still has that accruing interest counted toward net investment income for this test. The same applies to short-term obligations such as Treasury bills.

**A worked example.** Take the parent's $50,000 demand loan from earlier, the illustrative $2,000 of forgone interest for 2027, and a child with no other gift loans.

| Child's 2027 net investment income | Interest the parent reports | Deemed gift to the child |
|---|---|---|
| $600 from a savings account | $0 (treated as zero under the $1,000 rule) | $2,000 |
| $1,500 from savings and I bond accruals | $1,500 (capped at net investment income) | $2,000 |
| $4,000 | $2,000 (forgone interest is below the cap) | $2,000 |

The deemed gift column doesn't change, because the $100,000 rule doesn't apply to the gift tax. The annual gift tax exclusion is $19,000 per recipient for 2026, according to the IRS gift tax FAQs; as of Oct. 26 the 2027 figure hadn't been published. A $2,000 deemed gift would be under the 2026 figure unless the parent also made other gifts to the same child.

## Term gift loans and the up-front gift

Term loans get different gift tax treatment. Section 7872(d)(2) says that "In the case of any gift loan which is a term loan, subsection (b)(1) (and not subsection (a)) shall apply for purposes of chapter 12." Chapter 12 is the gift tax. Subsection (b)(1) treats the lender as transferring, on the day the loan is made, cash equal to the excess of "the amount loaned" over "the present value of all payments which are required to be made under the terms of the loan."

The gift happens all at once, at the start, and it is measured by how far below the AFR the loan is over its whole term.

**A worked example.** In November 2026, a parent lends a child $150,000 interest-free, to be repaid in a single payment after five years. A five-year term falls in the mid-term bucket, so the test rate is November's mid-term AFR, 5.02% compounded semiannually. The present value of $150,000 due in five years at that rate is $150,000 ÷ (1 + 0.0502 ÷ 2)¹⁰, or about $117,065.50. The difference, about $32,934.50, is treated as a gift in November 2026. That is the only gift tax measurement for the loan's forgone interest; it isn't repeated each year.

That exceeds the $19,000 annual exclusion for 2026. The 2025 Form 709 instructions, the latest final version as of Oct. 26, say that if you gave gifts to someone in 2025 "totaling more than $19,000 (other than to your spouse), you must generally file Form 709," and the IRS gift tax FAQs list the same $19,000 exclusion for 2026. The instructions add that a gift tax return is required "whether or not any tax is ultimately due." Form 709 is generally due "not later than April 15, of the year after the gift was made," which for a 2026 gift is April 15, 2027, unless the time is extended. The instructions also say some gifts, called future interests, require a return even below $19,000.

The income tax side of the same loan runs year by year under subsection (a), because it is a gift loan. The 1985 proposed regulations compute it for a gift term loan as if it were a demand loan, but with the AFR "based on annual compounding in effect on the day the loan is made" for a full year. On $150,000 at November's 5.08% annual mid-term rate, that is $7,620 for each full year. The 2026 amount covers only the part of the year after the loan is made. The $100,000 cap doesn't help here, because the loan is over $100,000, so the parent would report that imputed interest each year without receiving any of it.

## Forgiving payments later

Some lenders later decide to forgive part of a loan instead of collecting it. The Form 709 instructions treat forgiveness as a gift in its own right: "The gift tax may also apply to forgiving a debt." Forgiving principal doesn't undo the loan's history under section 7872 either. Forgone interest for the years the loan was outstanding was already treated as transferred on each Dec. 31. These examples assume a genuine loan and a later decision to forgive. An understanding from the start that repayment won't be required can raise a separate question: whether the money was a loan at all, or a gift when it was handed over.

**A worked example.** A parent makes the $50,000 demand loan described above and, at the end of 2027, forgives $10,000 of principal. The forgiveness is a $10,000 gift. The deemed gift from forgone interest for 2027, $2,000 under the illustration above, is a separate gift to the same child. Together they come to $12,000, which would be under the exclusion if the 2027 figure is at least 2026's $19,000 and there were no other gifts to that child that year. The amount still owed drops to $40,000, and forgone interest in later years is figured on the lower balance.

If the parent also gave the child $8,000 in cash that year, the total would be $20,000, over a $19,000 exclusion. Gifts above the year's exclusion generally mean filing a gift tax return, though filing isn't the same as owing tax.

## Charging the AFR instead

A loan that charges at least the AFR isn't below-market, so section 7872 doesn't create imputed interest or a deemed gift from forgone interest. The interest is taxable income like any other. A lender on the cash method who receives regular interest payments generally reports interest when it is received. A loan that defers all interest to the end can fall under the original issue discount rules instead; Publication 550 says "You generally include OID in your income as it accrues over the term of the debt instrument, whether or not you receive any payments from the issuer."

**A worked example.** The same parent lends $150,000 for five years at 5.02% compounded semiannually, November's mid-term AFR, with interest paid every six months and principal at the end. That loan isn't below-market. Each payment is $150,000 × 0.0502 ÷ 2 = $3,765, or $7,530 a year, and the parent reports interest as received. The child can deduct it only if a deduction rule applies, such as business or investment use; personal interest generally isn't deductible.

For comparison, $150,000 earning the [FDIC's national average rate](https://www.fdic.gov/national-rates-and-rate-caps) for a 60-month CD, 1.41% in the October 19, 2026 table, would earn about $2,115 in a year. At the 12-month CD average of 1.76%, it would be about $2,640.

The gap is large, but the two aren't the same kind of asset. The CD is a bank deposit insured by the FDIC up to $250,000 per depositor, per bank, per ownership category. The loan is an IOU from a relative. It has no insurance, its repayment depends on the borrower, and it can be awkward to enforce. The AFR is a tax floor, not a measure of what a loan is worth to the lender.

## How the AFRs compare with savings rates

The FDIC publishes national average deposit rates each month for its rate-cap rules. Its table dated October 19, 2026 showed these national rates, which the FDIC describes as averages "weighted by each institution's share of domestic deposits":

| Product | FDIC national rate (Oct. 19, 2026 table) | Benchmark in the same table |
|---|---|---|
| Savings | 0.38% | 3.88% (effective federal funds rate) |
| 12-month CD | 1.76% | 4.54% (Treasury yield) |
| 36-month CD | 1.39% | 5.00% (Treasury yield) |
| 60-month CD | 1.41% | 5.09% (Treasury yield) |

The FDIC notes that "All published rates and yields are based on information available on the last business day of the prior month end," so this table reflects data from the end of September. For savings, which has no matching Treasury maturity, the FDIC uses "the effective federal funds" rate as the benchmark. The Treasury yields in the table are much closer to the AFRs than the deposit averages are, which follows from how the AFRs are built.

Two points follow for savers. Because the averages are weighted by deposits, the rates of the banks holding the most deposits count the most. And the AFR tracks Treasury yields, not deposit rates, so it can sit well above what a typical savings account pays, as it does in this table.

## Reporting the interest

Interest a lender actually receives on a family loan is interest income. Imputed interest under section 7872 is reported as interest income too, as Publication 550 says: "The lender must report that amount as interest income."

The income is reportable whether or not a tax form reports it to you. In the $150,000 examples, a full year means $7,530 of interest received at the AFR, or $7,620 of imputed interest on the interest-free version, and either amount adds to interest from savings accounts and CDs. If that pushes total interest high enough, it can also affect estimated tax. Our guide to [savings interest and the Jan. 15, 2027 estimated tax](/2026/10/07/savings-interest-and-the-jan-15-2027-estimated-tax-the-1000-test/) covers that test.

Records help on both sides. The lender needs to know the loan's date, amount, rate and whether it is a demand or term loan, because each of those decides which AFR applies. The borrower's net investment income matters for loans of $100,000 or less, so a lender relying on that cap needs the borrower's figure for each year.

## Common questions

**Do I have to charge my child interest on a loan?**

The tax code doesn't require it, but a gift loan below the AFR can produce imputed interest income for the lender and a deemed gift to the borrower. A demand gift loan with total balances of $10,000 or less between the two of you is outside the rules on the days it stays at or under that amount, if it isn't used for income-producing assets. For loans up to $100,000, the imputed interest for income tax is capped at the borrower's net investment income and is zero if that income is $1,000 or less.

**If I lend money in November, does the rate change in December?**

For a term loan, no: the rate is fixed as of the day the loan is made. For a demand loan, the rate isn't locked. A balance outstanding all year uses the IRS's blended annual rate for that year, 3.82% for 2026, and a part-year loan uses the short-term rates for the periods it was outstanding.

**Does the $100,000 rule protect me from gift tax?**

No. It limits only the income tax result. The forgone interest on a demand gift loan is still treated as a gift, and on a term gift loan the up-front discount is the gift.

**My child holds I bonds and doesn't report the interest yet. Does that count as investment income?**

For this test, yes. Section 7872(d)(1)(E) treats interest that would accrue on deferred payment obligations, including "United States savings bond[s]," as interest received when figuring the borrower's net investment income.

**Is lending money to family a substitute for a CD?**

Not in the protection it offers. A CD at an insured bank is covered by the FDIC up to the limit. A loan to a relative has no insurance, and the tax rules apply while it is outstanding, whether or not payments arrive on time.

For a family that keeps its cash in savings, the November rates mostly change the arithmetic of the tests. A below-market loan is measured against a higher bar this month than last, and the bar is set by Treasury yields, not by what the family's own savings account pays.
