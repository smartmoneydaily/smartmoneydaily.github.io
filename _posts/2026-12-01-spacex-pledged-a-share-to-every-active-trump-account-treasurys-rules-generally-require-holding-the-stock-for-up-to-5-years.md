[SpaceX announced on Nov. 25](https://ir.spacex.com/updates/releases-details/2026/SpaceX-to-Invest-in-the-Next-Generation-of-Americans-2026-r_LYQ5cH94/default.aspx) a commitment to contribute one newly issued share of its Class A common stock to "every currently active Trump Account," and said Elon Musk will personally match the number of shares the company gives. According to CNBC's report that day, Musk wrote on X that the company's share would be transferred in December and that his own would follow "when my lockup expires in June next year." On Nov. 26 he went further on X: "I will be donating another share of SpaceX to all kids in America every year."

SpaceX trades on Nasdaq as SPCX. It closed at $133.59 on Nov. 27, after the 4.83% gain CNBC reported for the shortened session after Thanksgiving. At that price, a child who got both the company's share and Musk's match would hold about $267 in SpaceX stock. Whether a particular child gets either one depends on what "currently active" means, and as of Nov. 28 nobody had explained that. The [White House said on Oct. 7](https://www.whitehouse.gov/releases/2026/10/a-financial-stake-in-the-future-for-nearly-70-million-american-children-trump-accounts-made-it-possible/) that nearly 70 million Trump Accounts had been created, more than 60 million of them through automatic enrollment. CNBC wrote on Nov. 25 that it's unclear how many of those are considered active, and that SpaceX and Treasury didn't respond to its requests for details.

## What "currently active" could mean

The word "active" doesn't appear in Treasury's temporary regulations for these accounts, [T.D. 10056](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/09/30/2026-20026/trump-accounts), published Sept. 30. They do describe activation. In the explanation that accompanies the rules, Treasury and the IRS say activation, which includes signing the trustee's account agreement, "is generally the last step" in setting up an account a parent opens or claims, and contributions can't be made until the account is established. For the accounts Treasury opened on its own, which the rules call auto accounts, the Treasury secretary, as the party responsible for them, "is deemed to have activated each auto account," so nobody has to do anything for those to exist.

In the regulation's terms, then, every auto account already counts as activated. In everyday app terms, "active" could mean an account a parent has opened or claimed and finished setting up, which would leave out every auto account nobody has claimed. SpaceX's release doesn't say which it means, and nothing I found from SpaceX or Treasury as of Nov. 28 defines it.

Gifts like this one follow their own rules. Under 26 U.S.C. 530A(f), a donor's gift goes in equal amounts to every child in a "qualified class," and the statute lists three kinds: all account beneficiaries who haven't turned 18 by the end of the year the contribution is made, those children who also live in particular states or in Treasury-designated areas with at least 5,000 account beneficiaries, or those born in particular years. The regulations also allow an "approved class" that combines place and birth year, as long as it includes at least 5,000 account beneficiaries. One or more record dates in Treasury's written agreement with the donor fix who's in the class. Claim status isn't one of the criteria, and auto accounts can receive these gifts without being claimed. If SpaceX's shares go through this route, a class limited to claimed accounts isn't one the rules describe. I haven't seen an agreement showing how the company's wording fits.

Gwynne Shotwell's separately announced gift is one example of a targeted class. SpaceX's president said on Oct. 30 that more than 2 million SpaceX shares from her and her husband would be distributed through Trump Accounts in December, starting with children aged 11 to 17 in ZIP codes where the median family income is $57,000 or less.

Some children won't be in any class, whatever "active" turns out to mean. A child needs a Social Security number to have a Trump Account at all. And since every class is limited to children who haven't turned 18 by the end of the year the contribution is made, a child who turns 18 at any point in 2026 is left out of gifts made this year. That rule matters for Musk's match too. A child born in 2009 could be in a class for a December 2026 contribution, but turns 18 in 2027, so if Musk's June 2027 share goes through Treasury the same way, that child would be outside every class for it. For a baby born this fall, timing matters. Treasury creates auto accounts "on or about October 1, 2026, and periodically thereafter," so the child may not have an account by the record date SpaceX's gift uses.

## How a single stock fits in an index-fund account

Until the year a child turns 18, money in a Trump Account generally has to be invested in mutual funds or exchange-traded funds that track the S&P 500 or another qualifying index of mostly U.S. stocks, don't use leverage and charge no more than 0.1% a year. Those are the eligible-investment rules in Section 530A(b)(3), and one company's stock doesn't fit them. The regulations make room for donated shares with a "qualified stock contribution." Holding that stock doesn't break the investment rule, the preamble explains, "because no funds in the account are used to acquire the contributed stock." For these contributions only, the rules also waive the usual IRA requirement that contributions other than rollovers be made in cash. Parents and relatives still contribute cash.

Qualified stock has to be issued by a U.S. corporation, listed on a registered national securities exchange and free of pre-existing transfer restrictions, such as being a restricted security under the SEC's Rule 144, judged when it's handed to Treasury. The giver has to be an eligible donor: a federal, state or local government, an Indian tribal government or a tax-exempt 501(c)(3) organization. SpaceX, a for-profit company, isn't one, and neither is Musk personally. The donor gives to Treasury under a written agreement, and Treasury makes the deposits. The preamble spells out what that means for people with stock to give: "an individual who wants to fund a general funding contribution must act through an eligible donor, which would generally be a 501(c)(3) organization."

SpaceX's release doesn't say how its shares will get into the accounts. Its forward-looking statement lists risks that include "third-party and government approvals." The preamble also says a contribution is made when the account receives it, "and not when the contribution is announced by the eligible donor." Until a share lands, it's a pledge.

## Five years, or the end of the year a child turns 17, whichever comes first

Under the regulations' minimum holding period, qualified stock can't be sold before the earlier of five years after the account receives it or the end of the child's growth period, which runs through Dec. 31 of the year the child turns 17. If the stock is sold in violation of that rule, the trustee has to buy back the same number of shares of the same class "as soon as practicable."

Under the rules in force now, a share received in December 2026 would play out differently by birth year:

- A child born in 2009 turns 17 in 2026, so the growth period ends Dec. 31, 2026, and so does the hold. Depending on when the share arrives, that's days or weeks.
- A child born in 2012 turns 17 in 2029, so the growth period ends Dec. 31, 2029, before the five years are up.
- A child born in 2016 turns 17 in 2033, so the five-year mark in December 2031 comes first.

These are temporary regulations, and they expire Sept. 30, 2029, so the later examples assume whatever replaces them keeps the same holding period. A June 2027 share from Musk, if it's handled the same way, would start its own clock when it arrives.

The hold has a few exceptions. The trustee must sell promptly once it learns a notice has been filed to remove the stock from its exchange listing. Otherwise the stock can be sold during the hold only for four reasons: to accept a tender offer when the person responsible for the account directs it, if the company is acquired, to turn fractional shares into cash when they can't move in kind during a rollover, or to make a qualified ABLE rollover. That last one moves the entire account directly to the child's ABLE account in the year the child turns 17, and it's open only to a child who qualifies for an ABLE account, which requires a qualifying disability. Moving the account to another trustee doesn't restart the clock.

Once the hold ends, the shares can be sold, and during the growth period the money has to go into an eligible fund. An account a parent or guardian runs, whether claimed or opened before automatic enrollment began, can also keep the shares. Unclaimed auto accounts don't get that choice. Their holdings sit in a pooled master group trust. Its trustee has to sell qualified stock within a reasonable period after the hold ends and promptly reinvest the proceeds in an eligible investment, and reinvesting within 30 calendar days of the sale counts as prompt. The preamble says neither that trustee nor Treasury is expected to have discretion over voting the auto accounts' shares.

## Outside the $5,000 limit, but taxable when withdrawn

A gift that reaches the account through Treasury this way doesn't use any of the child's $5,000 annual contribution limit, which applies until the year the child turns 18 and is indexed for inflation after 2027. Section 530A(c)(2)(B) lists qualified general contributions as exempt, along with rollovers and the $1,000 pilot deposit for eligible U.S. citizen children born from 2025 through 2028, which needs its own election. Contributions from parents, grandparents and friends do count, and so do employer contributions excluded from the employee's income under Section 128, up to $2,500 per employee a year, also indexed after 2027. Those deposits can't go into an unclaimed auto account, which can take only Treasury-facilitated gifts and, if someone made the separate election, the pilot deposit. CNBC reported on Nov. 25 that friends and family had put in more than $1 billion since the July 4 launch, and that deposits from all sources had passed $9 billion.

Getting the share isn't a taxable event. Section 139J keeps qualified general contributions out of the child's gross income when they're made, and as with other IRAs, buying and selling inside the account isn't taxed as it happens. Withdrawals are another matter. Under Section 530A(d)(2), the account's investment in the contract, the after-tax basis that comes back tax-free, doesn't include qualified general contributions, the pilot deposit or excluded employer money. A relative's after-tax contribution adds basis, but a donated SpaceX share adds none.

Apart from rollovers and a few other exceptions in the statute, nothing can be withdrawn before Jan. 1 of the year the child turns 18. After that, the IRS says, the account generally follows the distribution rules for other traditional IRAs. Withdrawals are taxed as ordinary income except for the part that counts as basis, and the taxable part of a withdrawal before 59½ may also owe the 10% additional tax on early distributions unless an exception applies.

SpaceX closed at $143.50 on Nov. 18 and at $125.81 on Nov. 24, about 12% lower four trading sessions later, then got back to $133.59 by Nov. 27. A one-company holding can move that fast, and it isn't a savings balance. At the Nov. 27 price, two shares come to about $267, and by the time each share's hold ends they could be worth well above or below that.

## What I'd look for in December

If I had a child with a Social Security number who stays under 18 through the end of 2026, I'd make sure the child has an account and finish the setup through activation by [claiming the auto account Treasury opened](/2026/11/06/your-childs-trump-account-may-already-be-open-claiming-it-and-getting-the-1000-are-separate-steps/). If there's no account yet, as can happen with a newborn, a parent can still make the election, but the account it creates will be an auto account, because the rules treat every initial account set up after automatic enrollment began as one. The claim comes after that. Claiming isn't just a way to look. It moves the whole auto-account balance into an account the parent runs and makes the parent the responsible party. SpaceX hasn't said claiming is a condition of its gift, and I can't tell whether it matters for the share. It does let you see what the account holds.

Then I'd check the holdings during December for a SpaceX line and write down the date each share arrived. That date starts its five-year clock, unless the end of the year the child turns 17 comes first. The SpaceX release doesn't ask families to do anything, so I'd ignore any text or email that wants a fee or a Social Security number to release a share. The next date on Musk's side is June 2027, when he says his lockup ends.
