![ABLE Accounts in 2026: Age 46, a $20,000 Limit and SSI's $100,000 Rule](/assets/pin-images/2026-10-17-able-accounts-in-2026-age-46-a-20000-limit-and-ssis-100000-rule.png)

A 38-year-old who has received SSI since a spinal injury at 31, a hypothetical case, couldn't open an ABLE account until this year, because eligibility required a disability that began before age 26. Since January 1, 2026, the cutoff is age 46. For someone on SSI, the account type matters because of how savings are counted: SSI's resource limit is $2,000 for an individual and $3,000 for a couple, and it hasn't changed since 1989. A regular savings account generally counts toward it. An ABLE account balance doesn't, up to $100,000.

For 2026, an ABLE account can be opened by someone whose blindness or disability began before their 46th birthday and who meets the program's disability test. Total contributions from everyone combined are capped at $20,000 for the year, plus an extra amount a working beneficiary can add from their own pay in some cases. Earnings grow federally tax-free when spent on qualified disability expenses. For SSI, the first $100,000 of the balance is excluded from countable resources, distributions aren't counted as income, and timing matters for money taken out for housing.

The rules below come from the Social Security Administration's operations manual for ABLE accounts, [POMS SI 01130.740](https://secure.ssa.gov/poms.nsf/lnx/0501130740), along with its sections on the [resource limit](https://secure.ssa.gov/poms.nsf/lnx/0501110003), the [first-of-the-month rule](https://secure.ssa.gov/poms.nsf/lnx/0501110600) and [joint bank accounts](https://secure.ssa.gov/poms.nsf/lnx/0501140205); IRS [Publication 907](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p907); [Revenue Procedure 2025-32](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-drop/rp-25-32.pdf) for the 2026 contribution limit; [Notice 2025-67](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-drop/n-25-67.pdf) for the 2026 saver's credit income limits; and the text of [Public Law 119-21](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/PLAW-119publ21/html/PLAW-119publ21.htm). Medicaid rules vary by state and aren't covered in detail. This is general information, not legal, tax or benefits advice for any person.

## Why a regular savings account is a problem on SSI

SSI's resource limits are written in dollars that don't move. The POMS table shows $2,000 for a single individual and $3,000 for an individual with a spouse, effective January 1, 1989, followed by a note: "The statutory limits have not changed since 1/1/89."

Resources are measured once a month. POMS SI 01110.600 says: "We make all resources determinations as of the first moment of a calendar month." Checking and savings balances on the first of the month generally count, after any deductions or exclusions that apply; POMS SI 01140.200 describes the value as "the FOM balance less any applicable deductions or exclusions" in those cases. SSA verifies bank balances electronically through its Access to Financial Institutions process, according to the same section.

Joint accounts don't split the count by default. POMS SI 01140.205 says that when an SSI claimant or recipient "co-owns an account with someone who is not eligible for SSI benefits, we assume that all the funds in the account belong to the SSI claimant or recipient." The recipient can try to rebut that assumption with evidence, but the starting point is that a savings account owned jointly by a parent who isn't eligible for SSI and an SSI-receiving adult child is treated as entirely the child's, against the child's $2,000 individual limit.

An ABLE account is treated differently. Under the SSI rules described below, most of its balance is set aside from that count. (Other programs count savings under their own tests; Medicare Savings Programs, for example, are covered in [Medicare Savings Programs: The $9,950 Resource Limit and What Counts](/2026/10/06/medicare-savings-programs-the-9950-resource-limit-and-what-counts/).)

## Who can open one in 2026

POMS SI 01130.740 describes an ABLE account as "a tax-advantaged savings account" that is "used by eligible individuals to pay for qualified disability expenses," "owned by the eligible individual and designated beneficiary of the ABLE account," and established by someone who is "blind or disabled by a condition that began before the individual's 46th birthday." It adds: "Prior to 1/1/2026, individuals with blindness or a disability were eligible only if their condition began before age 26."

The manual lists four ways to qualify. The person must be:

- receiving SSI based on blindness or disability that began before age 46;
- in SSI suspense "due solely to excess income or resources, but otherwise eligible for SSI based on blindness or disability that began before age 46";
- receiving Social Security disability insurance benefits, childhood disability benefits or disabled widow's or widower's benefits based on blindness or disability that began before age 46; or
- "the subject of a disability certification."

The disability certification route is the one available to people who aren't receiving SSA disability benefits. Under POMS, the certification states that the person is blind or "has a physical or mental impairment that results in marked and severe functional limitation and that such blindness or disability began before age 46," and that the person has a copy of a physician-signed diagnosis. Publication 907 spells out the underlying test: a "medically determinable physical or mental impairment which results in marked and severe functional limitations, which (a) can be expected to result in death, or (b) lasted or can be expected to last for a continuous period of not less than 12 months," or blindness "within the meaning of section 1614(a)(2) of the Social Security Act." POMS also says conditions on SSA's "List of Compassionate Allowances Conditions" are deemed to meet the certification requirements if the condition produced those limitations before age 46.

What matters is the age when the condition began, not the person's current age. A 58-year-old whose qualifying disability began at 40 can open an account in 2026, provided one of the routes above applies.

IRS Publication 907 for tax year 2025 still describes the age-26 rule, because that was the rule in 2025. The age-46 rule applies from January 1, 2026.

Publication 907 adds two practical points. "A designated beneficiary is limited to only one ABLE account at a time," with narrow exceptions for rollovers and transfers. And if the beneficiary can't open the account, a list of people "in that order" can do it, starting with an agent under a power of attorney and including a conservator or legal guardian, spouse, parent, sibling, grandparent or an SSA representative payee. "However, only you, the designated beneficiary, can have any interest in the account during your lifetime."

## The 2026 contribution limit is $20,000, not $19,000

By statute, the ABLE annual limit is tied to the gift tax annual exclusion. For 2025, Publication 907 says contributions "are limited to the annual gift tax exclusion amount ($19,000 for 2025)."

For 2026, the two numbers split. Revenue Procedure 2025-32 sets the 2026 gift tax annual exclusion at "$19,000," but in a separate section says: "For taxable years beginning in 2026, $20,000 ... is included in the aggregate limitation on contributions to ABLE accounts under § 529A(b)(2)(B)(i)." The SSA manual's own table lists the ABLE limit as $20,000 for 2026.

The reason is in Public Law 119-21, the 2025 tax law. Section 70115 changed the inflation adjustment for the ABLE limit by "substituting '1996' for '1997'" in the formula, effective for taxable years beginning after December 31, 2025. An earlier base year means a larger cumulative inflation adjustment, which produced $20,000 for 2026 while the gift exclusion stayed at $19,000.

The limit is per beneficiary, not per contributor. Publication 907 describes it as a limit on "The total annual contributions to an ABLE account," and that total includes amounts rolled over from a 529 college savings account.

**Worked example: three relatives in one year.** In 2026, a grandparent puts in $10,000, a parent $8,000 and an aunt $3,000. Total: $21,000. The limit is $20,000, so $1,000 is excess. Publication 907 says that if contributions exceed the annual limit, "the excess contributions and the earnings on those contributions must be returned to the contributors," with a deadline of the due date, including extensions, of the beneficiary's income tax return. If that doesn't happen, a 6% excise tax applies. Form 5329, Part VIII, figures it as "6% (0.06) of the smaller of" the excess contributions or the value of the ABLE account on December 31.

There's also a cumulative balance limit. Publication 907 says the "cumulative limit for an ABLE account is set by each state's ABLE program," and that contributions also "may not exceed an annual cumulative limit, which is the same as the state's section 529 qualified tuition program limit." The figure differs by state.

## Extra room for beneficiaries who work

A working beneficiary can contribute more than the $20,000 from their own pay. Publication 907 for 2025 describes the rule: "certain employed ABLE account beneficiaries may make an additional contribution up to the lesser of these amounts: (1) the designated beneficiary's compensation for the tax year, or (2) the poverty line amount of $15,650 in the continental United States, $17,990 in Hawaii and $19,550 in Alaska."

There's a condition. The beneficiary "isn't eligible for the increased contribution limit for the tax year if any contribution is made on behalf of the employee" to a qualified defined contribution plan, a 403(b) plan or a 457(b) plan. The wording covers any contribution made on the employee's behalf, not only the employee's own deferrals, and the list doesn't include IRAs.

This extra room was scheduled to end after 2025. Section 70115 of Public Law 119-21 removed the end date, striking "before January 1, 2026" from the provision, for contributions made after December 31, 2025. For the dollar cap, POMS uses "The poverty level for a one-person household, as determined for the calendar year preceding the calendar year in which the taxable year begins." For 2026 contributions, that points to the 2025 HHS poverty guidelines. HHS's [2025 figure](https://aspe.hhs.gov/topics/poverty-economic-mobility/poverty-guidelines/prior-hhs-poverty-guidelines-federal-register-references/2025-poverty-guidelines-computations) for one person is $15,650, up from $15,060 in 2024, and that is the same continental figure Publication 907 printed for 2025, alongside $17,990 for Hawaii and $19,550 for Alaska. Because the 2025 edition of Publication 907 and the POMS rule point to different years, and the 2026 edition hadn't been issued when this was written, the ABLE program can confirm the figure it applies to 2026 contributions.

POMS adds an SSI point about wages. Putting pay into an ABLE account doesn't stop SSA from counting the pay. "The fact that a person uses their income to contribute to an ABLE account does not mean that their income is not countable for SSI purposes as it normally would be." If contributions are deducted from a paycheck, SSA includes them "in the applicant, recipient or deemor's gross wages."

## How SSI treats the account

Once money is in the account, POMS SI 01130.740 sets out how SSI counts it:

- **Contributions from others.** Third-party contributions "are not income to the designated beneficiary." A grandparent's deposit straight into the ABLE account isn't counted as the beneficiary's income.
- **Earnings.** "Exclude these earnings from the income of the designated beneficiary." Interest or investment gains inside the account aren't SSI income.
- **The balance.** "Exclude up to and including $100,000 of the balance of funds in an ABLE account from the resources of the designated beneficiary."
- **Distributions.** "A distribution from an ABLE account is not income but is a conversion of a resource from one form to another." That holds "regardless of whether the distributions are for a QDE that is not related to housing, for a housing expense, or for a non-qualified expense."

The last point is why the timing of spending matters. Distributions aren't income, but once the money leaves the account, it's subject to the resource rules again.

## Housing money and the first-of-the-month rule

Qualified disability expenses are defined broadly but with conditions. POMS says they are "related to the blindness or disability of the designated beneficiary and for the benefit of the designated beneficiary," and Publication 907 says they must be incurred while the beneficiary is an eligible individual. Within those limits, Publication 907 lists "education, housing, transportation, employment training and support, assistive technology, personal support services, health, prevention and wellness, financial management, administrative services, legal fees, expenses for oversight and monitoring, and funeral and burial expenses." POMS adds basic living expenses, and for ABLE purposes "food is considered a qualified disability expense (basic living expense), but not a housing expense."

Housing gets different SSI treatment from other qualified expenses. POMS defines housing expenses as mortgage (including property insurance required by the mortgage holder), real property taxes, rent, heating fuel, gas, electricity, water, sewer and garbage removal.

For a distribution taken for a qualified expense **not** related to housing, POMS says to exclude it from countable resources "if they retain it beyond the month received," as long as it stays unspent, identifiable and intended for that expense, and the beneficiary "maintains, makes contributions to, or receives distributions from the ABLE account." One of the manual's examples: a $500 distribution taken in June for a health-related expense due in September is excluded from resources in July, August and September.

For housing, the rule is tighter. POMS says to "Count a distribution for a housing expense or for an expense that is not a QDE as a resource, if the designated beneficiary retains the distribution into the month following the month of receipt. If the designated beneficiary spends the distribution within the month of receipt, there is no effect on eligibility."

The manual's own example: Amy takes $500 out in May to pay June's rent, deposits it in checking in May and pays the landlord on June 3. Because the money was in her checking account on June 1, it's a countable resource for June. Taking the same distribution on June 1 and paying rent that month would have kept it out of the count.

Changes of plan have their own timing. If money taken out for an excluded purpose is later spent on housing or a non-qualified expense, POMS counts it as a resource "as of the first moment of the month in which the funds were spent." If the plan changes but the money hasn't been spent, POMS says to "Count the retained funds as a resource as of the first of the following month."

## Over $100,000: suspension, not termination

The ABLE exclusion stops at $100,000. POMS says: "Count the amount by which an ABLE account balance exceeds $100,000 as a resource of the designated beneficiary."

What happens next depends on why the person is over the limit:

- If the ABLE balance above $100,000 is what pushes the person over the SSI resource limit, "alone or with other resources," SSA suspends the SSI payments "without time limit (as long as they remain otherwise eligible)," the person remains "SSI eligible for Medical Assistance (Medicaid)," and eligibility "does not terminate after 12 continuous months of suspension."
- If the person's other resources are over the limit on their own, the special rule doesn't apply. SSI is suspended under the regular rules, Medicaid tied to SSI stops, and eligibility "terminates 12 months later if the suspension continues throughout this period."

The manual's two examples, side by side:

| | Paul | Christine |
|---|---|---|
| ABLE balance on the first of the month | $101,000 | $101,000 |
| Countable amount from the ABLE account | $1,000 | $1,000 |
| Other countable resources | $1,500 in checking | $3,000 in checking |
| Total countable resources | $2,500 | $4,000 |
| Over the $2,000 limit because of | The ABLE excess | The checking account alone |
| Result | Cash SSI suspended, Medicaid kept | SSI suspended, Medicaid stops |

Paul's checking account holds $1,500 and Christine's holds $3,000. Christine's checking balance alone is over $2,000, so the ABLE rule can't protect her.

POMS explains the Medicaid point applies "in States where Medicaid eligibility is based on SSI eligibility," so the details depend on the state.

## Taxes: when earnings become taxable

For federal income tax, Publication 907 says: "Earnings in an ABLE account aren't taxed unless a distribution exceeds a designated beneficiary's qualified disability expenses." It also says contributions "are not tax deductible and must be in cash or cash equivalents," which describes federal tax; state tax treatment is set by each state.

If distributions in a year are more than qualified expenses, part of the earnings becomes taxable. Publication 907's example: an account with $2,400, of which $2,000 is contributions and $400 is earnings, distributes all $2,400 in 2025, and the beneficiary has $1,600 of qualified disability expenses that year. The nontaxable share of the earnings is $1,600 ÷ $2,400 × $400 = $266.67, so $400 − $266.67 = $133.33 is included in income. The publication adds: "The tax on any distribution included in your taxable income is increased by 10%," figured on Form 5329, Part II. That part of the form subtracts distributions "that are not subject to the additional tax" and then says to "Enter 10% (0.10)" of the rest. Assuming no exception applies, the extra tax on these numbers is 10% × $133.33 = $13.33, on top of regular income tax on the $133.33.

The account also reports to the IRS. Publication 907 lists Form 1099-QA for distributions and Form 5498-QA for contribution information.

At death, Publication 907 says a state "may file a claim" for Medicaid paid after the account was opened. That claim is paid "only after all your qualified disability expenses have been paid," and it's reduced by premiums paid to a Medicaid Buy-In program.

## The saver's credit for the beneficiary's own contributions

A beneficiary who contributes to their own ABLE account may qualify for the saver's credit. The IRS saver's credit page says "The maximum contribution amount that may qualify for the credit is $2,000 ($4,000 if married filing jointly), making the maximum credit $1,000 ($2,000 if married filing jointly)." The joint figures assume both spouses make qualifying contributions; one beneficiary's own contributions top out at the $1,000 credit. The credit is nonrefundable, so it can't exceed the tax the person would otherwise owe. Publication 907 notes that SSI payments "aren't taxable," so a beneficiary whose only income is SSI has no income tax for the credit to reduce. The IRS page lists the basic conditions as "Age 18 or older," not a full-time student and not claimed as a dependent on another person's return, and Publication 907 defines a full-time student by enrollment during some part of each of 5 calendar months. The IRS page also says "Rollover contributions do not qualify for the credit" and that eligible contributions "may be reduced by any recent distributions" from a retirement plan, IRA or ABLE account.

The credit rate is "50%, 20% or 10%" of eligible contributions depending on AGI, according to the IRS [saver's credit page](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/plan-participant-employee/retirement-savings-contributions-savers-credit), and above the top limit it's 0%. For 2026, Notice 2025-67 raises the income limits:

| Filing status | 50% rate up to | 20% rate up to | 10% rate up to |
|---|---|---|---|
| Married filing jointly | $48,500 | $52,500 | $80,500 |
| Head of household | $36,375 | $39,375 | $60,375 |
| All other filers | $24,250 | $26,250 | $40,250 |

The credit for ABLE contributions was also set to expire. Public Law 119-21, section 70116, rewrote the definition of contributions that qualify so ABLE contributions are included with no end date, and changed the $2,000 figure in the credit formula to $2,100 for taxable years beginning after December 31, 2026. In the rewritten definition, retirement-plan contributions count only "in the case of any taxable year beginning before January 1, 2027," so from 2027 on, ABLE contributions are what this credit covers.

## 529 rollovers and the annual cap

Public Law 119-21, section 70117, also removed the end date on rollovers from a 529 college savings account to an ABLE account by striking "before January 1, 2026." Publication 907 says such rollovers can go to the ABLE account of the 529's beneficiary or "an eligible member of the family," and that "The limit on annual contributions to an ABLE account ... applies to these rollovers." A $15,000 rollover from a 529 in 2026 leaves $5,000 of room under the $20,000 limit for everyone else's contributions that year.

## Where the account's cash sits

ABLE programs are run by states, and state programs publish their own fees, investment options and account limits. POMS says "An eligible individual can open an ABLE account through the ABLE program in any State, if the State permits it." Programs differ in their investment and cash options, fees and the cumulative limit. How the cash portion of a particular program is held, and whether any part of it is a bank deposit eligible for [FDIC](https://www.fdic.gov/resources/deposit-insurance) insurance, is something the program's own disclosure documents spell out. The FDIC's own [list of products it does not insure](https://www.fdic.gov/resources/deposit-insurance/brochures/insured-deposits) includes stock investments, bond investments and mutual funds, so ABLE money placed in investment options isn't an insured deposit.

SSI and Social Security payments can also go straight into the account. POMS notes that "Social Security and SSI monthly benefit payments can be directly deposited into ABLE accounts because they are considered acceptable types of financial accounts." The deposit doesn't change how the benefit itself is counted as income. Where a representative payee manages the benefits, POMS adds a limit: "If a payee does not have the signature authority over the ABLE account, they cannot deposit SSA benefits to the ABLE account because they do not control the account."

## Common questions

**Can someone whose disability began at 35 open an account now?**

Under POMS, yes, if one of the qualifying routes applies, such as receiving SSI or SSDI based on that disability or filing a disability certification. The condition must have begun before the person's 46th birthday, and the age-46 rule applies from January 1, 2026.

**Does a parent's deposit into an ABLE account count as the beneficiary's income for SSI?**

Not if it's the parent's own money. POMS says third-party contributions "are not income to the designated beneficiary." A deposit of the beneficiary's own wages or benefits is different: that money is counted as income the usual way.

**Is the $20,000 limit per person who contributes?**

No. It's the total for the account for the year. Publication 907 describes it as a limit on "The total annual contributions to an ABLE account."

**Can money from an ABLE account sit in checking until rent is due next month?**

For housing, POMS counts a distribution as a resource if it's still held "into the month following the month of receipt." Money for non-housing qualified expenses can be held longer if it stays unspent, identifiable and intended for that expense while the ABLE account is still being maintained.

**What happens if an account grows past $100,000?**

The amount over $100,000 counts as a resource. If that's what puts the person over the SSI limit, and they remain otherwise eligible, SSA suspends cash payments without a time limit and the person keeps SSI-based Medicaid eligibility in states that tie Medicaid to SSI. If the person's other resources are already over the limit on their own, that protection doesn't apply.

The age change has been in effect since January 1, 2026, and the $20,000 limit and the extended room for working beneficiaries apply to contributions made this year. Individual cases turn on the state program's terms and on SSA's monthly resource count.
