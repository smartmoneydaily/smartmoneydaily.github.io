A money market account and a money market fund are separated by one word on the page and by a legal chasm underneath it. The first is a deposit at a bank or credit union, sitting inside the same insurance framework as your checking account. The second is a mutual fund — a pooled investment holding short-term debt that passes the income through to shareholders. Both are conservative products. Only one of them is insured.

That difference rarely announces itself. It surfaces on the day you need the cash in a hurry, or on the day a bank fails and you learn which pile your balance was in. Plenty of people move an emergency fund into something labeled money market without ever checking which of the two they bought.

This post covers three things: what protection sits behind each product, how each behaves under stress, and a short check you can run on your own statement to find out which one you hold.

## Two Names, Two Legal Structures

A money market account (MMA) is a deposit. You hand money to an insured bank or credit union, the institution owes it back, and the [FDIC](https://www.fdic.gov/national-rates-and-rate-caps) stands behind that obligation up to $250,000 per depositor, per insured bank, per ownership category. Credit unions sit outside the FDIC and are covered instead by the [NCUA](https://ncua.gov/consumers/share-insurance-coverage), with the same headline limit. It is a bank product start to finish: a balance, a rate the bank sets, a regulator watching the bank's books.

A money market fund (MMF) is a registered mutual fund. You own shares. The fund owns short-term instruments — Treasury bills, agency paper, repurchase agreements, commercial paper, bank certificates of deposit — and it answers to the SEC, not to bank regulators. No bank promises you anything, and fund materials are required to say so, which is why they carry some version of "Not FDIC insured. No bank guarantee. May lose value."

Held in a brokerage account, a fund does carry one protection that gets routinely misread. SIPC covers up to $500,000 in securities, including $250,000 in cash, if the brokerage firm fails and customer assets go missing. It does not protect against the investment losing value.

## Side by Side

| | Money market account | Money market fund |
|---|---|---|
| What you hold | A deposit; the bank owes you the balance | Shares of a mutual fund |
| Insurance | FDIC or NCUA, $250,000 per depositor, per institution, per ownership category | Not covered; SIPC addresses broker failure, not investment loss |
| Value | A dollar is a dollar | Priced per share; many funds aim to hold $1.00, some price with a floating value |
| Yield source | A rate the institution sets | Portfolio income, minus the fund's expense ratio |
| Getting cash out | Transfer, check, or debit, subject to any limits the bank sets | Sell shares, wait for settlement, then transfer |
| Oversight | Bank and credit union regulators | SEC |

## Behavior Under Stress

### When a bank fails

The failure path for a deposit is well worn. The FDIC takes over and insured balances are made whole — usually within a few business days, often by moving the account to an acquiring institution so the depositor barely notices. Anything above the limit becomes a claim against the receivership, paid from whatever the failed bank's assets recover, on a timeline nobody can promise. The insured portion is the part that behaves predictably.

### When markets seize

A fund doesn't fail; its portfolio either holds up or it doesn't. Two things can go wrong. The first is value: funds that aim to hold a stable share price do it through accounting convention and portfolio discipline, not a guarantee, and in severe conditions a share price can fall below its target. That happened during the 2008 crisis, which prompted emergency government support for the sector. It is rare — and rare is not impossible, which is the exact case an emergency fund exists for.

The second is access. Fund rules permit certain funds to charge liquidity fees when redemptions surge, so the money can still be there while costing something to reach. On an ordinary day none of that applies. Stress is when the two products stop resembling each other.

## The Emergency Fund Problem

An emergency fund has one job: be there, in full, at an unpredictable moment, without requiring you to think. Judged against that job, the naming confusion causes three distinct failures.

- **Uninsured without knowing it.** Someone believes their cash reserve carries deposit insurance when it never did. The belief costs nothing until it is tested.
- **A longer access chain than expected.** Reaching cash in a fund means a sale, a settlement, and then a transfer, all on business days. A deposit balance is already cash, and many MMAs come with checks or a debit card. Regulation D's six-per-month transfer limit was suspended in 2020, though plenty of banks still enforce limits of their own, so read the account terms instead of assuming either way.
- **Yield chasing for pocket change.** As of August 17, 2026, the FDIC national average is 0.63% APY for money market accounts against 0.38% for savings — a gap of 0.25 percentage points. On a $20,000 reserve that is $50 a year, real money but not enough to justify holding emergency cash in something whose value can move.

None of this makes money market funds bad. They are a reasonable home for cash you don't need this week. The mismatch is specific: emergency money wants a deposit.

## A Five-Minute Statement Check

Pull up the account and look for these tells.

**It's a fund if you see:**
- A ticker symbol, typically five letters ending in X
- A share count and a price per share rather than a single balance
- An expense ratio, a prospectus, or a seven-day yield quoted instead of an APY
- Income described as dividends
- A disclosure reading "Not FDIC insured. No bank guarantee. May lose value."

**It's a deposit if you see:**
- "Member FDIC" or "Federally insured by NCUA" on the statement
- One balance, no share count and no price
- Income posted as interest paid
- An APY quoted for the account
- Terms describing transfer limits, minimum balances, or monthly fees

Brokerage accounts deserve extra attention, because uninvested cash there — the sweep — can be either. Some firms sweep into a money market fund; others sweep into deposits at partner banks, where the disclosures name each program bank. Same screen, same "cash" label, two different answers.

When you want confirmation rather than inference, the [FDIC's BankFind](https://banks.data.fdic.gov/bankfind-suite/bankfind) tool shows whether an institution is insured, and its EDIE calculator runs a specific set of balances through the ownership category rules.

## Coverage Math on a Real Balance

Say you hold $310,000 in a money market account at one bank, in your name alone.

The single ownership category covers $250,000. The remaining $60,000 is uninsured — not lost, not at risk on any ordinary day, just outside the guarantee. A common misreading is to open a second account at the same bank in the same name and assume the coverage doubles. It doesn't. Coverage runs per depositor, per bank, per ownership category, so two single-name accounts at one bank share one $250,000 limit.

Ownership categories are the lever that works. A joint account is a separate category, and each co-owner is insured for $250,000 of the joint balance, so a two-person joint account carries up to $500,000. Move the $60,000 there and the arithmetic becomes:

- Single-name account: $250,000, fully insured
- Joint account: $60,000, of which each owner's share is $30,000 — far below the $250,000 per-owner limit, fully insured
- Uninsured: $0

Between two people at that one bank, the ceiling is $250,000 each in single accounts plus $500,000 joint: $1,000,000 without opening a relationship anywhere else.

One more piece of arithmetic catches people. At the 0.63% national average, a $250,000 balance earns about $1,575 over a year, which is roughly $130 a month in credited interest. APY is worth understanding here: it is the figure that already reflects the effect of compounding over a year, while a stated interest rate on its own does not. Park exactly $250,000 and the first month's credit alone carries the balance past the insured limit. Leave a buffer, or move the interest out on a schedule.

## Matching the Product to the Job

Sort your cash by when you'll need it, then pick the structure that fits.

Money you might need this month belongs in a deposit account, where a dollar stays a dollar and insurance does the worrying. Money with a known date and no chance of an early call can go into term deposits — the FDIC national average for a 12-month CD is 1.71% APY as of August 17, 2026, against 0.63% for money market accounts, and that gap is payment for giving up access, enforced by an early-withdrawal penalty if you change your mind. Cash that is genuinely surplus — held for yield rather than availability — is the money a fund can reasonably hold, because you are not counting on it being there in full on one specific bad day.

Confirm what you hold, confirm the insurance is real, confirm the balance sits under the limit in the right ownership category — then stop thinking about it. Getting that right on a quiet day is what keeps it from mattering on a loud one.
