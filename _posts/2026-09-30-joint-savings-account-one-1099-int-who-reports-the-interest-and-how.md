A joint savings account produces one Form 1099-INT, and the bank issues it under the taxpayer identification number of whoever is listed first on the account. That is why a saver can open a January envelope and find interest that legally belongs to a parent or an adult child. The IRS has a procedure for this. It is not "report your half and hope." The first-named owner reports the full amount on Schedule B, subtracts the co-owner's share on a line labeled "Nominee Distribution," and then issues a Form 1099-INT of their own to the co-owner. In the example the IRS uses in [Publication 550](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/p550.pdf), a joint account paid $1,500 for 2025, the sibling's share was $450, and the person whose Social Security number was on the account had to give the sibling a 1099-INT for that $450 by February 2, 2026 and file a copy with the IRS by March 2, 2026, or March 31, 2026 if filed electronically.

One exception covers most households: spouses do not have to file the nominee form for each other. Anyone else who receives a 1099-INT as a nominee has to use Schedule B whatever the amount, because receiving interest as a nominee is itself one of the conditions that triggers the schedule. The rest of this piece follows three IRS pages: Publication 550, the [Instructions for Schedule B](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i1040sb), and the [General Instructions for Certain Information Returns](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i1099gi). It shows what the rule says, where each entry goes and which deadline attaches to which form. It is not advice for a particular return.

## Why the bank sends one form, in one name

The bank is not judging who owns the money. It is following an instruction about which number goes on the account. Publication 550 tells savers how that number is chosen: "Generally, if the funds in a joint account belong to one person, list that person's name first on the account and give that person's TIN to the payer." The next sentence covers the case most families are in: "If the joint account contains combined funds, give the TIN of the person whose name is listed first on the account."

So the ordering of names at account opening decides whose return the IRS expects to see the interest on. A parent who added an adult child to a savings account and happened to list the child first will find the 1099-INT arriving under the child's number, and the IRS's matching system will look for that interest on the child's Form 1040.

The form is generated on a low threshold. The [Instructions for Forms 1099-INT and 1099-OID](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i1099int) direct payers to file for anyone "to whom you paid amounts reportable in boxes 1, 3, or 8 of at least $10." A joint account with a few thousand dollars in it clears $10 easily. The question is rarely whether a form will come, only whose name is on it.

The IRS's own terminology for the person on the form is a nominee. Publication 550 states the position from the other side of the table: "Generally, if someone receives interest as a nominee, that person must give you a Form 1099-INT showing the interest received on your behalf." The General Instructions for Certain Information Returns make the definition explicit: "if you receive a Form 1099 for amounts that actually belong to another person, you are considered a nominee recipient. You must file a Form 1099 with the IRS (the same type of Form 1099 you received) for each of the other owners showing the amounts allocable to each." The word "must" appears in both passages.

## Whose interest it is, and who decides

Before any form can be filled in, the co-owners need one number: how much of the year's interest belongs to each. The IRS does not supply that number and does not assume an even split. Publication 550 sends the question to local law: "If two or more persons hold property (such as a savings account, bond, or stock) as joint tenants, tenants by the entirety, or tenants in common, each person's share of any interest or dividends from the property is determined by local law."

The account agreement at the bank says who can withdraw; it does not say who is taxed. Local law, which for a bank account generally means the property law of the state the owners live in, sets the default, and the owners' own arrangement about the money can matter within it. The Publication 550 example is built on exactly such an arrangement. In it, the sibling "deposited 30% of the funds in this account, and you and your sibling have agreed to share the yearly interest income in proportion to the amount each of you has invested." The 30% share of the interest follows the 30% share of the deposits, by agreement, and the IRS treats that as the sibling's income.

Two things follow. First, a family that has never discussed the split has nothing to point to if the IRS asks why 30% rather than 50% went on one return. A short written note, dated, stating each owner's deposits and the agreed allocation, is the kind of record the example presupposes. Second, the nominee procedure exists for the mismatch between the TIN on the form and the ownership of the money; an account funded by one person, with that person's name and TIN first, needs none of it.

Joint accounts are not the only place this comes up. A custodial account for a minor is a different structure with a different rule, because the money belongs to the child and the reporting follows the child, which is covered in [Savings Accounts for Kids: Custodial Rules and the Kiddie Tax](https://smartmoneydaily.github.io/2026/09/17/savings-accounts-for-kids-custodial-rules-and-the-kiddie-tax/). The nominee procedure is for two or more adults, or an adult and a child, who hold an account together as co-owners.

## The Schedule B entries, line by line

The person whose TIN is on the 1099-INT is the one who does the subtracting. The Schedule B instructions lay out the sequence in one paragraph. The order matters because the IRS matches the gross figure first.

Report the whole thing. "If you received a Form 1099-INT that includes interest you received as a nominee (that is, in your name, but the interest actually belongs to someone else), report the total on line 1. Do this even if you later distributed some or all of this income to others." The 1099-INT the IRS holds shows the gross amount, and line 1 has to show the same gross amount; netting the figure before it reaches the form is what produces a mismatch.

Then subtotal and subtract. "Under your last entry on line 1, put a subtotal of all interest listed on line 1. Below this subtotal, enter 'Nominee Distribution' and show the total interest you received as a nominee. Subtract this amount from the subtotal and enter the result on line 2."

The same instructions settle whether the schedule is required at all. The list of conditions that trigger Schedule B begins with "You had over $1,500 of taxable interest or ordinary dividends," and the same list includes "You received interest or ordinary dividends as a nominee." A first-named owner with a nominee share on the bank's form therefore files Schedule B even when the gross interest is under $1,500. The co-owner, whose only entry is the reallocated share, is bound by the $1,500 test and the other listed conditions, not by the nominee one.

The co-owner, on their side, reports their share as ordinary interest income. [Topic No. 403](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc403) states the timing rule that applies to both owners: "most interest that you receive or that is credited to an account that you can withdraw from without penalty is taxable income in the year it becomes available to you." The interest was available in 2025, so it is 2025 income for whichever owner it belongs to, regardless of when the nominee form is sent.

## The 1099-INT you have to issue

This is the half with deadlines and a second form attached. Publication 550 sets it out for 2025 interest:

"If you received interest as a nominee in 2025, you must file a Form 1099-INT for that interest with the IRS. Send Copy A of Form 1099-INT with a Form 1096 to your Internal Revenue Service Center by March 2, 2026 (March 31, 2026, if you file Form 1099-INT electronically). Give the actual owner of the interest Copy B of the Form 1099-INT by February 2, 2026. On Form 1099-INT, you should be listed as the 'Payer.' Prepare one Form 1099-INT for each other owner and show that person as the 'Recipient.'"

The roles invert here. On the bank's form, the saver is the recipient. On the nominee form, the saver becomes the payer, and the co-owner becomes the recipient. Form 1096 is the transmittal sheet that goes to the IRS with Copy A; the co-owner never sees it. The Schedule B instructions state the obligation in a single sentence: "If you received interest as a nominee, you must give the actual owner a Form 1099-INT (unless the owner is your spouse) and file Forms 1096 and 1099-INT with the IRS."

| What | Who receives it | Deadline for 2025 interest | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Copy B of Form 1099-INT | The co-owner (actual owner of the interest) | February 2, 2026 | Publication 550 |
| Copy A of Form 1099-INT with Form 1096, on paper | IRS Service Center | March 2, 2026 | Publication 550 |
| Form 1099-INT, filed electronically | IRS | March 31, 2026 | Publication 550 |

The General Instructions for Certain Information Returns, which cover every 1099 series form rather than the nominee case specifically, print the general filing dates as "Paper: February 28, 2026; Electronic: March 31, 2026." February 28, 2026 is a Saturday, and the date Publication 550 gives for the nominee 1099-INT is the following Monday, March 2. The earlier date is the safe one.

One form per co-owner. Publication 550 says to "prepare one Form 1099-INT for each other owner," so an account with three names on it and one TIN generates two nominee forms from the first-named owner, each showing only that recipient's share. The Instructions for Forms 1099-INT and 1099-OID list the nominee role among the form's uses: interest paid in a trade or business "or for which you were a nominee/middleman."

## A worked example with the IRS's numbers

Publication 550's example is short enough to reproduce in full and then lay out on a table.

"You and your sibling have a joint savings account that paid $1,500 interest for 2025. Your sibling deposited 30% of the funds in this account, and you and your sibling have agreed to share the yearly interest income in proportion to the amount each of you has invested. Because your SSN was given to the bank, you received a Form 1099-INT for 2025 that includes the interest income earned belonging to your sibling. This amount is $450, or 30% of the total interest of $1,500. You must give your sibling a Form 1099-INT by February 2, 2026, showing $450 of interest income your sibling earned for 2025."

Here is what each person's paperwork carries.

| Item | First-named owner (SSN on the bank's form) | Sibling |
|---|---|---|
| Deposits into the account | 70% | 30% |
| Bank's Form 1099-INT, box 1 | $1,500 | none |
| Schedule B, Part I, line 1 | $1,500 | $450 from the nominee 1099-INT, on Schedule B only if the schedule is otherwise required |
| Subtotal | $1,500 | none |
| "Nominee Distribution" line | ($450) | none |
| Taxable interest on the return (Schedule B line 2 for the nominee) | $1,050 | $450 |
| Form 1099-INT issued as payer | one, to the sibling, for $450 | none |
| Form 1096 filed with the IRS | yes, with Copy A | no |

The two taxable-interest figures sum to $1,500, which is the number the bank told the IRS. The gross figure appears once under the original TIN, the reallocation is visible on that return, and the co-owner's figure now traces to a 1099-INT carrying the co-owner's own TIN.

The example does not show tax. Here is a second case with assumed figures, chosen to show why the allocation matters. Suppose a parent and an adult child hold a joint savings account that earned $2,400 in 2025, each contributed half the money, and they have agreed to split the interest evenly. The parent is listed first, so the bank's 1099-INT shows $2,400 under the parent's SSN. Assume, purely for illustration, a 22% federal marginal rate for the parent and 12% for the child. These are assumptions for arithmetic, not a description of either person's actual bracket, and state tax is left out.

| Scenario | Parent reports | Child reports | Federal tax on the interest at the assumed rates |
|---|---|---|---|
| Parent reports the whole 1099-INT, no nominee step | $2,400 | $0 | $2,400 × 22% = $528.00 |
| Nominee procedure, 50/50 split | $1,200 | $1,200 | $1,200 × 22% + $1,200 × 12% = $264.00 + $144.00 = $408.00 |
| Difference | | | $120.00 |

The $120 is not a loophole. Under the local-law rule quoted earlier, half the interest was the child's income from the start; reporting all of it on the parent's return overstates the parent's income and understates the child's. The reverse misstatement, a lower-rate parent absorbing a higher-rate child's share, is invisible on the bank's form alone; the nominee 1099-INT is what makes the allocation visible on both sides.

A third variation causes the most confusion: the account is funded entirely by one owner, but the other owner is listed first. Say a parent put every dollar in and the adult child, listed first for convenience, receives the 1099-INT. Under the local-law rule, the interest is the parent's. The child reports the full amount on Schedule B line 1, subtracts the entire figure as a nominee distribution, enters zero on line 2, and issues the parent a 1099-INT for the whole amount. The child ends up with no taxable interest from the account and a filing obligation anyway, purely because of name order.

## Exceptions and edge cases

The spouse exception is the broadest. Publication 550: "you do not have to file Form 1099-INT to show payments for your spouse." The General Instructions repeat it: "A spouse is not required to file a nominee return to show amounts owned by the other spouse." Topic No. 403 phrases the same rule as an instruction to prepare the form "unless that interest (or OID) belongs to your spouse." For a married couple filing jointly, the interest lands on the same return either way, so there is nothing to reallocate. For spouses filing separately, the exception removes the 1099-INT filing but not the question of whose income it is, which goes back to local law and, in community property states, to rules these passages do not cover.

Backup withholding travels with the TIN, not with the ownership. If the bank withheld 24% because the first-named owner's TIN was missing or mismatched, the withholding is reported under that owner's number on the same 1099-INT. The passages quoted here say nothing about reallocating withheld tax to a co-owner, so a saver in that position has two problems to fix in sequence: the withholding, described in [Backup Withholding on Savings Interest: Why the Bank Took 24%](https://smartmoneydaily.github.io/2026/09/21/backup-withholding-on-savings-interest-why-the-bank-took-24/), and then the allocation.

Accounts with more than two owners follow the same rule multiplied. Publication 550's instruction to prepare "one Form 1099-INT for each other owner" means a three-owner account with combined funds and one TIN produces two nominee forms, each showing that recipient's share, and the "Nominee Distribution" line on the first owner's Schedule B shows the total of both.

None of the quoted passages address a co-owner whose share is small. The $10 floor in the 1099-INT instructions is written from the payer's side, and the nominee sentences in Publication 550 and the General Instructions carve out no minimum, so a first-named owner with a co-owner whose share is a few dollars should read the current-year form instructions in full rather than assume the floor applies.

## What to do, in order

Fix the account before the next form is generated. Publication 550's TIN rule is written as advice to the saver, not just the bank: if the funds belong to one person, "list that person's name first on the account and give that person's TIN to the payer." For an account that is really one person's money, reordering the names, or retitling the account, ends the nominee obligation for every future year. Ask the bank whether the primary holder and the TIN of record can be changed.

For an account with genuinely combined funds, write down the split. The Publication 550 example works because the siblings "have agreed to share the yearly interest income in proportion to the amount each of you has invested." A dated note of each owner's deposits and the agreed split, kept with the tax records, turns a 30% figure from a guess into a documented position.

Then handle the form the bank already sent. The first-named owner reports the full box 1 amount on Schedule B line 1, subtotals, enters "Nominee Distribution" with the co-owner's share, and puts the difference on line 2. The same owner prepares a 1099-INT showing themselves as payer and the co-owner as recipient, gives Copy B to the co-owner by the early-February deadline, and sends Copy A with Form 1096 to the IRS by the paper or electronic date for that year. The co-owner reports the amount on the nominee form as their own interest.

The deadlines quoted here are the ones Publication 550 gives for 2025 interest, and they shift by a day or two when a fixed date falls on a weekend. The General Instructions for Certain Information Returns and the Instructions for Forms 1099-INT and 1099-OID for the filing year are where the operative dates are printed, and an owner who missed them should still file rather than skip the step.

## Common questions

### The bank's 1099-INT names the child, but the money is the parent's. Does a form really have to go to the IRS?

Under the passages quoted above, yes, unless the actual owner is the nominee's spouse. Publication 550 says a nominee "must file a Form 1099-INT for that interest with the IRS" and give the actual owner Copy B. The child reports the full amount on Schedule B line 1, subtracts it as a nominee distribution, and issues the 1099-INT with the child as payer and the parent as recipient, with Form 1096 as the transmittal. To avoid repeating this next year, have the account retitled with the parent's name and TIN first.

### Married and filing jointly: is any of this needed?

No nominee 1099-INT is required between spouses. Publication 550 says "you do not have to file Form 1099-INT to show payments for your spouse," and the General Instructions say the same. On a joint return the interest is reported once, and the split between the two spouses does not affect the return.

### Can each owner just report half of the 1099-INT and skip the nominee form?

The instructions do not describe that as an option. The Schedule B instructions say to "report the total on line 1" and then subtract the nominee share, and they say the nominee "must give the actual owner a Form 1099-INT" and "file Forms 1096 and 1099-INT with the IRS." Splitting the figure without the subtotal and subtraction leaves the IRS holding a 1099-INT for the full amount under one TIN with only half of it reported on that return.

### The split is 50/50 by agreement but only one owner put money in. Which controls?

Publication 550 says each person's share "is determined by local law." The example in the publication ties the interest split to the deposits by agreement, but it does not say that deposits are the only permissible basis. Whether an agreement to split evenly is respected when one owner funded the account is a question of the state's property law, and the place to confirm it is a tax professional or the state's rules for joint accounts, not the federal passages quoted here.

The bank's form answers one question, whose TIN is on the account, and the tax law asks a different one, whose money earned the interest. When the two answers match, there is nothing to do. When they do not, the first-named owner carries the full amount onto Schedule B, hands the co-owner's share back on a nominee line and a 1099-INT of their own, and the co-owner reports what was always theirs.
