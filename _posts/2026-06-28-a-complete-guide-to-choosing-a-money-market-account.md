A money market account is a deposit account, and the number printed in the largest font is usually the top rate on a tier table rather than the rate your balance would actually earn. This post is about the rest of the table: how the tiers get applied, which minimum triggers which outcome, what limits sit on transfers, and how a monthly fee can cancel a full year of interest.

Two anchors before the arithmetic. As of August 17, 2026, the FDIC national average is 0.63% APY for money market accounts and 0.38% APY for savings. Those are averages across insured institutions, not offers, and no individual account has to pay either number.

Nothing below names a bank. The tier tables and fees are illustrations, built so you can follow the math with your own disclosure open beside them.

## Money market accounts vs. savings and checking

All three are deposit accounts at an insured institution. The differences that matter to you sit in the terms, not the label.

APY and interest rate are not the same figure. APY includes the effect of compounding; a stated interest rate does not. Compare APY to APY, always.

Some money market accounts come with checks or a debit card and some do not. Your account disclosure states which, and that is worth confirming before you assume the account can double as a bill-paying account.

One more distinction, because the names collide. A money market *account* at a bank or credit union is a deposit. A money market *fund* sold through a brokerage is an investment. Deposits at an insured bank that fails are covered; investments are not, however conservative they look.

For scale: on August 17, 2026 the gap between the two national averages was 0.63% minus 0.38%, or 0.25 percentage points. On a $60,000 balance held for a year, that difference is $150.00.

## Two ways a tier table can work

A tiered account pays different rates at different balance levels. There are two common ways to apply that, and they produce very different numbers from the same table.

| Balance tier | Illustrative APY |
| --- | --- |
| $0 – $9,999.99 | 0.50% |
| $10,000 – $49,999.99 | 1.20% |
| $50,000 and above | 2.00% |

Your disclosure tells you which of these it means:

- **Whole-balance:** reaching a tier applies that tier's rate to every dollar in the account.
- **Split, or blended:** each portion of the balance earns the rate for its own tier.

### The $60,000 test

Assume $60,000 sits in the account for one full year with no deposits or withdrawals. APY already expresses a year of compounding, so the year's interest is simply balance times APY.

Whole-balance reading: $60,000 × 2.00% = **$1,200.00**.

Split reading:

- First $10,000 × 0.50% = $50.00
- Next $40,000 × 1.20% = $480.00
- Final $10,000 × 2.00% = $200.00
- Total: $50.00 + $480.00 + $200.00 = **$730.00**

Two readings of one table, $1,200.00 − $730.00 = $470.00 apart. The split version works out to an effective yield of $730.00 ÷ $60,000 = 1.2167% when rounded to four decimal places, which is a long way from the 2.00% on the poster. For reference, that same $60,000 at the 0.63% national money market average as of August 17, 2026 would earn $378.00 for the year.

### Your own effective yield

Run those same lines with your balance and your table, then divide the total by your balance. That percentage is your effective yield, and it is the only figure that compares cleanly against a flat-rate account or against a national average. A 2.00% headline that delivers 1.2167% is not a 2.00% account for you, and the only way to know which one you are holding is to do the division.

Notice also what the split structure does at the boundaries. Under the illustrative table, the dollar that crosses from $49,999.99 to $50,000 earns 2.00% by itself, while everything beneath it keeps earning 0.50% and 1.20%. Chasing a tier threshold moves the rate on the marginal dollars only, which is a much smaller prize than the tier label implies.

## The three kinds of minimum

A single account can carry three separate minimums, and they are often three different numbers:

1. The minimum to open the account.
2. The minimum daily or average balance to earn a given tier's APY.
3. The minimum balance to avoid the monthly maintenance fee.

Miss the first and you cannot open. Miss the second and you keep the account but drop to a lower rate. Miss the third and you pay. The disclosure states each one, along with whether it is measured as a daily balance or as an average across the statement cycle — a distinction that matters if your balance swings mid-month.

## Transfer and withdrawal limits

Regulation D's six-transfer limit was suspended in 2020. Many banks still set their own limits, and those limits are contractual rather than federal, so they vary from one institution to the next.

Four lines to find in the disclosure:

- How many withdrawals or transfers are allowed per statement cycle.
- Which channels count against that number, and which do not.
- The per-item fee once you go past the limit.
- Whether repeated excess activity can convert or close the account.

If this account is meant to hold an emergency fund you might tap on short notice, these terms matter more than a tenth of a percentage point. Work out how many withdrawals a bad month would actually require, then compare that against the limit in writing. An account that pays well and charges you for the fourth transfer is a poor fit for money you may need in a hurry, and a fine fit for money you will not touch.

## Fees against a year of interest

Fees are subtracted from interest, so put them in the same units before you compare.

A $10 monthly maintenance fee costs $120.00 over a year. On a $10,000 balance earning the illustrative 1.20% tier above, the year's interest is exactly $120.00. The fee cancels all of it, leaving $0.00.

Run that same fee against the 0.63% national money market average as of August 17, 2026. A $10,000 balance earns $10,000 × 0.63% = $63.00, against $120.00 of fees — a net cost of $120.00 − $63.00 = $57.00 for the year. The account pays you less than nothing.

A fee waiver tied to a balance you hold comfortably changes this completely. A waiver tied to a balance you would have to strain to maintain is a rate cut with a delay on it.

## FDIC coverage on a money market account

Standard FDIC coverage is $250,000 per depositor, per insured bank, per ownership category. Credit union accounts are insured by the NCUA at the same standard limit. Ownership categories — single, joint, certain retirement accounts, revocable trust — are insured separately, so one person can be covered above $250,000 at a single institution through more than one category.

Two things to verify rather than assume. Some brands share a single bank charter, which means balances held under both count once toward the same limit; the FDIC's BankFind tool shows which brands share a charter. And the FDIC's rules for trust accounts were revised effective April 1, 2024, so a formula you remember from before that date may no longer apply. For anything involving a trust, use the FDIC's EDIE estimator instead of working from memory.

## A checklist before you open one

Open the disclosure and answer these six before funding anything:

- Is the tier table whole-balance or split? Run your balance both ways.
- What is the minimum to open, to earn your tier, and to avoid the fee?
- Is the balance test a daily balance or a cycle average?
- What is the monthly fee in annual dollars, and what is left after interest?
- How many transfers per cycle, through which channels, at what fee past the limit?
- Is the institution FDIC- or NCUA-insured, and do you hold other balances under the same charter?

If an answer is not in the disclosure, ask before you fund the account. Every number in this post came from arithmetic you can redo on your own statement, which is the point. The headline APY is one input among six, and the other five are the ones you have to go looking for.
