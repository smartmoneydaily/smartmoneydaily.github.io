![A New IRS Fix for IRA Mistakes: Late Rollovers, Missed RMDs and a $3,500 Fee](/assets/pin-images/2026-10-26-a-new-irs-fix-for-ira-mistakes-late-rollovers-missed-rmds-and-a-3500-fee.png)

On Oct. 22, the Treasury Department and the IRS released two revenue procedures on fixing mistakes in retirement accounts. One of them, Rev. Proc. 2026-33, sets up the first IRS correction program built for individual retirement accounts. Called the IRA Compliance Resolution System, or ICRS, it takes effect May 1, 2027. Through it, an IRA owner, or for some errors the bank or other custodian holding the IRA, can ask the IRS to approve a fix for four kinds of errors: a required minimum distribution that came up short, an IRA opened as the wrong type of account, an inherited IRA paid out to a beneficiary who was led to believe it could be rolled over, and a rollover that missed the 60-day deadline. The fee for a single request filed before Jan. 1, 2028 is $3,500.

The release also says requests to waive the 60-day rollover deadline, which now go to the IRS as private letter ruling requests, will move into the new system: "Over the course of 2027 and 2028, these requests will be transitioned to requests under ICRS." The existing automatic waiver and self-certification procedures "remain available, at no cost." The second document, Rev. Proc. 2026-34, updates the correction program for employer plans and took effect the day it was released.

For savers who keep an IRA at a bank, the relevant cases are familiar ones: an IRA CD that matures and is paid out to the owner, a required distribution the bank was supposed to run and didn't, an inherited IRA CD cashed out because someone at the branch said the money could go back in later. In each of them, the no-fee procedures are worth checking before paying for a submission.

## What changed on Oct. 22

Until now the IRS had a formal correction program for employer plans, the Employee Plans Compliance Resolution System (EPCRS), and nothing comparable for IRAs. An individual whose own IRA went wrong had a patchwork: a private letter ruling or a self-certification letter for a late rollover, a Form 5329 waiver request for a missed required distribution, and for some errors nothing at all.

Congress told the Treasury to close that gap in the SECURE 2.0 Act of 2022. Section 305(c) of that law, as Rev. Proc. 2026-33 summarizes it, directs the Treasury to "expand the Employee Plans Compliance Resolution System (EPCRS) to allow custodians of IRAs to address Eligible Inadvertent Failures with respect to an IRA."

The program is narrower than its name. For now ICRS has one component, the IRA Voluntary Correction Program (IVCP), and it covers only the four failures in its Appendix A. "Self-correction of IRA failures is not available under ICRS at this time." Every correction runs through a filing, a fee and an IRS compliance statement. Treasury and the IRS invite comments, including on "additional failures that could be corrected in future versions of the program." Comments are due Jan. 20, 2027, under docket IRS-2026-1387 at regulations.gov.

## The four mistakes it covers

Each failure is defined in section 2 of the revenue procedure, with a permitted correction in Appendix A.

**RMD Failure.** Because of a failure by a service provider, the amount distributed from the owner's IRA or IRAs during the year is less than the required minimum distribution. The custodian distributes the missed amounts, with earnings, for every year the shortfall happened.

**IRA Establishment Failure.** The owner asked a service provider to set up "a specific type of IRA account," and because of the provider's failure got a different type of IRA or "a non-IRA account." The balance moves, with earnings, into a new IRA of the requested type, treated as opened on the original date. A strict filing deadline applies, covered below.

**Nonspouse Beneficiary Distribution or Rollover Failure.** Because of "an inadvertent error by a Service Provider," a beneficiary who isn't the surviving spouse "had reason to believe" money from a decedent's IRA or an inherited IRA could be rolled over without being taxed. Instead of a trustee-to-trustee transfer into an inherited IRA, the beneficiary received a distribution or the money went into the wrong kind of account. The beneficiary can put the money, up to a cap described below, into a new inherited IRA.

**60-Day Rollover Failure.** A distribution isn't rolled over within 60 days, and refusing to waive the deadline "would be against equity or good conscience, including casualty, disaster, or other events beyond the reasonable control of the IRA Owner." It covers distributions from IRAs and from 401(a) plans, 403(a) annuity plans, 403(b) plans and governmental 457(b) plans, so a 401(k) participant can use it too.

Only the last of the four can be fixed without a mistake by a service provider: "A 60-Day Rollover Failure does not need to have occurred due to an error by a Service Provider in order to be eligible for correction under IVCP." A rollover missed because the owner was in the hospital can be considered, the same as one missed because a bank misrouted the check.

## Who counts as a service provider

The revenue procedure defines a service provider as "a service professional who has a service contract or agreement in place with an IRA Owner or with respect to an IRA, including, but not limited to, an IRA Custodian, a certified financial planner, a fiduciary financial adviser, a certified public accountant, or a licensed attorney." An IRA custodian is "a bank or other person (including a nonbank trustee meeting the requirements of Treas. Reg. § 1.408-2(e)) authorized to hold assets on behalf of IRA Owners." A bank offering IRA CDs is the custodian of those IRAs.

For RMD and wrong-account failures, the error must come from a provider with "established practices and procedures (formal or informal)" that were "reasonably designed to promote and facilitate overall compliance." Those procedures "must have been routinely followed," and the failure "must have occurred through an oversight or mistake in applying them." A bank whose working process missed one account through a data-entry slip would fit that test better than a bank with no process at all.

Some failures are excluded whoever made them. An eligible failure "does not include any failure that is egregious, relates to the diversion or misuse of IRA assets, or is directly or indirectly related to an abusive tax avoidance transaction."

## Who can't use it

- **Returns under examination.** If the owner's Form 1040 series return or Form 1041 for any year is under IRS examination, including notice of an impending exam or a case in Appeals or litigation, IVCP "is generally not available." For a late rollover, the IRS can still grant a waiver during an exam under section 4 of Rev. Proc. 2020-46.
- **Unfiled returns.** An owner "must currently be in compliance with all tax filing obligations for the Form 1040 series return or Form 1041."
- **Plan-level failures.** Failures of SEP arrangements and SIMPLE IRA plans as plans belong in EPCRS. Failures in individual SEP IRAs and SIMPLE IRAs can go through ICRS.
- **Anything not on the list.** "At this time, IVCP is not available to correct any failures outside of the failures specified in section 2 of Appendix A."

The IRS "may decline to make available IVCP in the interest of sound tax administration," and it can refuse a compliance statement for a submission that's "seriously deficient." If no substantive processing has happened, the fee is refunded. Otherwise it isn't.

## How a 60-day rollover goes wrong with an IRA CD

The rule comes from [26 U.S.C. 408](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/408)(d)(3): an IRA distribution isn't taxed if it's paid into an IRA or another eligible plan "not later than the 60th day after the day on which he receives the payment or distribution." The same section lets the Treasury waive the deadline "where the failure to waive such requirement would be against equity or good conscience, including casualty, disaster, or other events beyond the reasonable control of the individual subject to such requirement." ICRS doesn't change the 60 days. It changes how a waiver is requested.

At a bank, the usual route into trouble is a maturing IRA CD that the owner asks the bank to close and pay out. Once the money leaves the IRA as a distribution, the clock is running. The IRS rollover page describes the alternative: "you can ask the financial institution holding your IRA to make the payment directly from your IRA to another IRA or to a retirement plan. No taxes will be withheld from your transfer amount."

A check paid to the owner brings in three more rules:

1. **Withholding.** "An IRA distribution paid to you is subject to 10% withholding unless you elect out of withholding or choose to have a different amount withheld." A 401(k) distribution that is eligible for rollover and paid to the participant generally faces what the IRS calls "mandatory withholding of 20%." Rolling over the full amount means making up the withheld part from other money.
2. **One rollover per 12 months.** You "can make only one rollover from an IRA to another (or the same) IRA in any 12-month period, regardless of the number of IRAs you own." Trustee-to-trustee transfers and Roth conversions don't count.
3. **The 10% additional tax.** If the rollover fails and the owner is under 59½, [Topic No. 557](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc557) says the 10% additional tax applies "to the part of the distribution that you have to include in gross income," unless an exception applies.

A waiver fixes only the missed deadline. The IRS FAQ says "the IRS cannot waive the IRA one-rollover-per-year rule." The amount is capped, too. In the revenue procedure's example, if an owner "took a distribution of $30,000 from the IRA Owner's IRA or plan participant's retirement plan, the most that could be deposited into an eligible retirement plan pursuant to a waiver of the 60-day rollover period is $30,000." Interest the money earned in a regular savings account in the meantime can't be added. And a waiver doesn't make a required minimum distribution eligible for rollover; Rev. Proc. 2020-46 says a taxpayer "may not roll over a distribution if it is a required minimum distribution."

## The free routes that stay

Before paying $3,500, check whether a no-cost route fits. The revenue procedure says these routes "remain available and are not affected by the ability to request a waiver of the 60-day rollover requirement under this revenue procedure."

**Automatic waiver.** This covers money that reached a financial institution in time where the institution made the mistake. The IRS FAQ lists the conditions, all of which must apply: the institution "receives the funds on your behalf before the end of the 60-day rollover period"; you followed its procedures for depositing the funds into an IRA or plan within the period, including giving instructions; the funds weren't deposited in time "solely because of an error on the part of the financial institution"; they're deposited "within 1 year from the beginning of the 60-day rollover period"; and it would have been a valid rollover if the deposit had been made as instructed. There's nothing to file. If a receiving bank took a check on day 40 with instructions to open an IRA CD and opened an ordinary CD instead, this is the route to look at first.

**Self-certification.** Under [Rev. Proc. 2020-46](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-drop/rp-20-46.pdf), the owner gives the receiving institution a signed letter, using the model in its appendix or language "substantially similar in all material respects," saying why the deadline was missed. The reason must be one of twelve:

- an error by the financial institution receiving the contribution or making the distribution
- a distribution check that "was misplaced and never cashed"
- a distribution that was "deposited into and remained in an account that the taxpayer mistakenly thought was an eligible retirement plan"
- severe damage to the taxpayer's principal residence
- a death in the taxpayer's family
- serious illness of the taxpayer or a family member
- incarceration
- restrictions imposed by a foreign country
- a postal error
- an IRS levy whose proceeds were returned to the taxpayer
- delay by the paying institution in providing information the receiving plan or IRA required, despite the taxpayer's reasonable efforts
- a distribution made to a state unclaimed property fund

The listed reason must actually have kept the owner from completing the rollover in time. The deposit must then be made as soon as practicable once the reason stops preventing it, a requirement "deemed to be satisfied if the contribution is made within 30 days after the reason or reasons no longer prevent the taxpayer from making the contribution." The IRS must not have denied a waiver for the same distribution. There's no fee.

The letter isn't IRS approval: "A self-certification is not a waiver by the IRS of the 60-day rollover requirement." The taxpayer may report a valid rollover "unless later informed otherwise by the IRS," and on audit the IRS can find the conditions weren't met, with income tax, additional tax, interest and penalties to follow. The receiving bank reports on Form 5498 that it accepted the deposit after the deadline, and it doesn't have to take it: "No, a particular financial institution is not required to accept a late rollover to an IRA."

**Waiver during an exam.** If the IRS is already examining the return, it may find during the exam that the owner qualifies for a waiver.

## What changes for paid waiver requests

The paid route is for an owner who doesn't fit the automatic waiver or the twelve reasons, or who wants an IRS answer first. Today that's a private letter ruling. Under the fee schedule in [Rev. Proc. 2026-4](https://www.irs.gov/irb/2026-01_IRB#REV-PROC-2026-4), the fee for a "Waiver of the 60-day rollover requirement under §§ 402(c)(3) and 408(d)(3)" is $3,500. The IRS FAQ page, last updated July 20, 2026, still cites an earlier $10,000 fee from Rev. Proc. 2022-4, so check the current revenue procedure before paying.

The handoff in Rev. Proc. 2026-33:

- **Through April 30, 2027:** ICRS isn't in effect. Paid requests go through the annual letter ruling procedure, Rev. Proc. 2026-4 now and Rev. Proc. 2027-4 once it's published.
- **2027:** "taxpayers may submit requests for waivers of the 60-day rollover requirement under either ICRS or Rev. Proc. 2027-4," with ICRS available from May 1.
- **2028 onward:** "it is anticipated that requests for such waivers will only be permitted under ICRS."

The ICRS fee through 2027 is $3,500, the same as the current letter ruling fee. The 2027 ruling fee will be in Rev. Proc. 2027-4, and ICRS fees for filings after Dec. 31, 2027 will be set in Rev. Proc. 2028-4, so neither number is known yet.

The facts the IRS weighs stay the same. Under ICRS, the specialist will consider "(1) errors committed by a financial institution; (2) inability to complete a rollover due to death, disability, hospitalization, incarceration, restrictions imposed by a foreign country, or postal error; (3) the use of the amount distributed (for example, in the case of payment by check, whether the check was cashed); and (4) the time elapsed since the distribution occurred." Money spent and later replaced is a weaker case than an uncashed check.

The filing method and privacy change. Letter ruling requests are mailed or delivered to the IRS in Florence, Ky., while ICRS filings are made online through Pay.gov. On privacy, the FAQ notes: "The text of letter rulings is generally open to public inspection." Identifying details are deleted first. ICRS information "is subject to the confidentiality requirements of section 6103 and is not a written determination within the meaning of section 6110."

For a late rollover under ICRS, "the Applicant cannot be an IRA Custodian. The Applicant must be the IRA Owner," though a representative can file for the owner. The filing lists ten items, including "a detailed explanation as to why the 60-day rollover requirement was not met" and, for an IRA-to-IRA rollover, a statement that the owner made no other one in the past 12 months. A request to waive the 10% additional tax goes in the same filing.

## Worked example: an IRA CD check that sat for 80 days

A 58-year-old saver has a traditional IRA holding a CD that matures at $48,000. She plans to move it to another bank's IRA CD, asks the first bank to close it, and doesn't elect out of withholding. Assume the IRA holds only pretax money, no exception to the 10% additional tax applies, and nothing else about her return changes.

- Distribution: $48,000
- Withheld at 10%: $4,800
- Check received: $43,200

Two weeks later she's hospitalized, and the hospital stay is what keeps her from depositing the check until day 80.

**She rolls over the full $48,000 with a self-certification letter.** Serious illness is on the list. If she deposits within 30 days after she's able to act, the deposit is treated as made as soon as practicable. To deposit $48,000 she adds the $4,800 that was withheld from her savings, and the withholding comes back as a credit on her return. If this is her only IRA-to-IRA rollover in 12 months and the receiving bank takes the deposit, nothing is taxable.

**She rolls over only the $43,200 she received.** The $4,800 withheld is a distribution that was never rolled over. It's taxable, and at 58 it carries the additional tax: 10% × $4,800 = $480, unless an exception applies.

**No waiver of any kind.** The whole $48,000 is income, and the additional tax is 10% × $48,000 = $4,800. At a hypothetical flat 22% rate, the income tax would be $10,560, for $15,360 in total before the $4,800 withholding credit.

**Her reason isn't on the list.** Suppose instead she was never hospitalized, knew where the check was, and simply lost track of the deadline. Forgetting a deadline isn't one of the twelve reasons, so self-certification doesn't fit, and a paid request is what's left. (A check that was misplaced and never cashed would be a listed reason, so the facts matter.) An uncashed check helps her case; a reason within her control doesn't. She would be paying $3,500, with no assurance of approval, to avoid up to $15,360 in tax. A tax professional can help her judge how strong her facts are.

Timing adds a catch. For a late-2026 distribution, the 2026 return is due in April 2027, before ICRS opens. A [filing extension](https://www.irs.gov/filing/get-an-extension-to-file-your-tax-return) moves the return to October, but the IRS says "The extension is only for filing your return," so tax owed is still due in April. A pending request isn't an approval. Appendix A says what to do after a correction: an owner who already reported and paid tax files an amended return if the refund period is still open; one who hasn't filed and owes nothing after the correction "should not report the original distribution as taxable." The revenue procedure warns: "The filing of an IVCP submission does not extend the statute of limitations on refunds." A timely protective refund claim can preserve a refund for that year while a request is pending, and the revenue procedure says such a claim "must be perfected once a compliance statement is issued."

## Missed required distributions: check the no-fee waiver first

A shortfall in a required distribution triggers the excise tax in [26 U.S.C. 4974](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/4974), "a tax equal to 25 percent of the amount by which such minimum required distribution exceeds the actual amount distributed." It falls to 10% if the missed amount is taken and a return reflecting the tax is filed within a correction window, which generally ends at the earliest of a notice of deficiency, assessment, or "the last day of the second taxable year that begins after the end of the taxable year in which the tax under subsection (a) is imposed."

The revenue procedure's own example: an owner's RMD is $10,000, and because of the custodian's error only $8,000 goes out.

- Shortfall: $10,000 − $8,000 = $2,000
- Excise tax at 25%: $500
- At 10% inside the correction window: $200

A waiver costs nothing. Under [26 CFR 54.4974-1(g)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/26/54.4974-1), the tax may be waived if the payee shows the shortfall "was due to reasonable error" and that reasonable steps are being taken to fix it. The request goes with Form 5329, as Publication 590-B and the Form 5329 instructions describe. A custodian's processing error is the kind of fact such a request rests on. The same regulation also waives the tax automatically in two situations, one of them a required distribution that an owner who died that year hadn't taken, when the beneficiary takes the missed amount by the deadline the regulation sets. The 25% and 10% rates apply to taxable years beginning after Dec. 29, 2022; a shortfall from an earlier year falls under the prior version of the statute.

In this example, paying $3,500 to fix a $500 problem doesn't add up for one person. The revenue procedure says, "Generally, IVCP is not available to correct failures that can be corrected under the Code and related regulations," but makes an exception so the program stays open for RMD failures even though they "can be corrected outside of IVCP with no user fee." ICRS is more likely to matter when a custodian fixes a systemic error across many accounts, since an approved correction can carry an IRS waiver of the excise tax for every owner included.

For an individual retirement account, the custodian distributes "the missed RMDs (with Earnings from the date of the failure to the date of the distribution) for all previous years in which an RMD Failure occurred." Earnings means "the adjustment of a principal amount to reflect subsequent investment gains and losses." In an IRA CD or savings account, the gains are interest. If earnings can't be reasonably estimated, the rate in the Labor Department's Voluntary Fiduciary Correction Program online calculator "is deemed to be a reasonable interest rate." The custodian issues a Form 1099-R "for the year of correction reflecting the amount of the distribution as taxable."

## Inherited IRA CDs: new relief for nonspouse beneficiaries

This part has no real counterpart today. The IRS FAQ says: "A non-spouse beneficiary of a deceased person's plan or IRA is not eligible to roll over a distribution received from the plan or IRA." The money has to move by trustee-to-trustee transfer into an inherited IRA. If the bank cuts a check to the beneficiary, the full amount is generally taxable, and the 60-day waivers don't help, because the deadline isn't the problem.

Under ICRS, the beneficiary can contribute the money "to a new Inherited IRA that is in the name of the same beneficiary associated with the original Inherited IRA," up to "the amount of the original distribution adjusted for Earnings, minus any RMDs that would have been required" had it moved correctly. The distribution "(minus any RMDs that would have been required) will be treated as a nontaxable event."

Proof is the hard part. The filing must describe "the specific errors made or advice provided by the Service Provider" that led the beneficiary to believe the money wouldn't be taxed. "Contemporaneous written summaries of oral communications with the Service Provider can be submitted as corroborating evidence but may be given less weight." An email from the bank or a form it prepared carries more weight than advice heard at a branch.

A hypothetical: a nephew inherits his aunt's traditional IRA, held as a $60,000 IRA CD of pretax money. A banker tells him he can take a check and deposit it in his own IRA later, and in early 2026 the bank pays him $60,000. The receiving bank refuses the deposit. At a hypothetical 24% rate, the income tax would be $14,400. From May 1, 2027, he could file under ICRS, pay $3,500, and if the IRS agrees, fund a new inherited IRA and claim a refund on an amended 2026 return for the tax on the part treated as nontaxable. Any required distribution he would have had to take stays taxable: if $3,000 had been required for 2026 and earnings are ignored, $57,000 could go back in, and at 24% the refund would be $13,680 rather than $14,400.

The refund window matters. Under [section 6511(a)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/6511), as the revenue procedure summarizes it, a refund claim is due within three years from when the return was filed or two years from when the tax was paid, whichever is later. Past that, the tax can't come back, but the beneficiary "might, however, still be eligible to contribute the amount" to a new inherited IRA and "receive basis for that contribution."

## Opened as the wrong account

The fourth failure fits a branch-level mistake: a customer asks for a traditional IRA CD and gets a Roth IRA, or an ordinary CD that isn't an IRA at all. In the revenue procedure's example, a Roth is opened instead of a traditional IRA, the owner deducts the contribution, and tax is owed when the deduction is disallowed.

The service provider or owner opens the type of IRA originally requested, the custodian transfers the portion of the balance that was originally placed in the wrong account, with its earnings, and the new IRA "will be treated as having been established as of the date the incorrectly established IRA or non-IRA account was originally established."

This failure has its own deadline. A submission "should be made as soon as practicable after discovery of the failure, but in no event will such a submission be accepted after December 31 of the year following the year in which the incorrect account was established." The IRS example: an error on Aug. 15, 2026 can't be submitted after Dec. 31, 2027. Combined with the May 1, 2027 start, that means an account wrongly opened in 2025 or earlier hits its deadline before the program opens. In practice, only accounts opened in the wrong form in 2026 or later can use this correction.

## How a submission works

Filings under IVCP, the program's one component, are electronic only: "Paper IVCP submissions are not permitted and will not be processed."

1. **Ask the custodian first.** When a compliance statement calls for a deposit, "a particular IRA Custodian (that is not the Applicant) is not required to accept the funds, and the IRS cannot compel such IRA Custodian to accept the funds." The revenue procedure recommends contacting a custodian before filing.
2. **File Form 15443 on Pay.gov.** The applicant signs the form online, uploads one PDF of supporting documents (up to 15 MB, with any overflow faxed) and pays the fee. The Pay.gov tracking ID serves as the IRS control number. Form 15745, a model compliance statement, and its Schedules 1 through 3 give standard descriptions and corrections.
3. **No anonymity.** "IVCP submissions may not be submitted on an anonymous basis."
4. **Respond within 21 days.** An applicant asked for more information "will generally have up to 21 calendar days" to provide it, or the case may be closed with the fee kept.
5. **Correct within 90 days.** After the compliance statement is issued, the corrections must be made within 90 days, or 150 days for a custodian's group submission. An extension has to be requested in writing before the period ends and approved by the IRS.
6. **Fix the return.** The program's specialists "do not process income tax refunds." A refund requires Form 1040-X. Tax owed must be reported and paid, or "the compliance statement is not valid."

A compliance statement covers only the listed failures and years; it "does not provide reliance for any other failure or year."

## When the bank files instead of you

A custodian can file for one owner or for a group, for any covered failure except a late rollover, which only the owner can submit. It must first notify each affected owner, and an owner's IRA is included only if the owner responds in time asking to be in. The notice must say owners are ineligible unless their own tax filings are current. For a group, the notice goes out at least 45 days before filing, and the failure must be "a systemic Eligible Inadvertent Failure that affects at least 10 IRA Owners and results in at least 10 IRAs implementing correction."

Fees through 2027 are $3,500 for a regular submission and, for a group, $10,000 for the first 10 owners plus $250 for each additional owner, capped at $50,000. Forty owners cost $10,000 + 30 × $250 = $17,500, or $437.50 each. The cap is reached at 170 owners ($10,000 + 160 × $250 = $50,000). At 200 owners the formula would give $57,500, but the fee stops at $50,000, or $250 each.

Within 30 days after the correction period ends, the custodian sends each owner a cover letter and a copy of the compliance statement. The sample letter warns that "these additional actions might be time sensitive because there could be legal deadlines on your ability to take certain actions." Keep it with your tax records and check whether an amended return is needed.

## Where FDIC coverage fits

None of this changes deposit insurance, but a corrected rollover often lands in a bank IRA. The [FDIC](https://www.fdic.gov/resources/deposit-insurance) puts traditional, Roth, SEP and SIMPLE IRAs in its certain retirement accounts category: "All certain retirement accounts owned by the same person at the same IDI are aggregated and the total is insured up to $250,000." (IDI means insured depository institution.) In the FDIC's example, a depositor with $280,000 in two IRAs at one bank "is insured for $250,000 and uninsured for $30,000," and naming beneficiaries "does not increase her coverage." Spreading IRA CDs across insured banks can keep each bank's total within the limit, and moving them by trustee-to-trustee transfer keeps them clear of the 60-day rule and the 12-month limit. Inherited IRAs need a second look. The FDIC says an IRA still held in the decedent's name and recognized by the IRS as the decedent's IRA is insured separately, but once it is restructured into an inherited IRA in the beneficiary's name, "The funds would be aggregated with any other certain retirement account deposits of the named owner at the same IDI and insured up to the $250,000 limit."

## Common questions

**I missed a 60-day rollover this month. Should I wait for ICRS?**

Not necessarily. If a financial institution received the money in time and made the error, the automatic waiver may already cover it. If a reason on the Rev. Proc. 2020-46 list actually kept you from finishing in time, self-certification lets you redeposit now with no IRS filing, as soon as practicable after the reason ends; a deposit within 30 days meets that test. If neither fits, a paid letter ruling is available today for $3,500, and ICRS becomes another route on May 1, 2027.

**Will ICRS let me do a second IRA rollover inside 12 months?**

No. It waives only the 60-day deadline. The revenue procedure repeats that "a rollover to another IRA is not permitted if the taxpayer has made an IRA rollover of another IRA distribution made in the prior one-year period."

For people with IRA CDs and IRA savings accounts, the practical step comes before any of this: ask for a trustee-to-trustee transfer rather than a distribution paid to you, and keep a copy of the written instructions. From May 1, 2027, the new program offers a way back for four specific mistakes, for a fee, through a filing, and on the IRS's terms.
