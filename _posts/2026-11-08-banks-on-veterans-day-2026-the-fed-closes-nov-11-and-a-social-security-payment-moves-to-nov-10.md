If your Social Security benefit normally arrives on the second Wednesday of the month, November's payment is due a day early, on Tuesday, Nov. 10. This month's second Wednesday is Nov. 11, Veterans Day, and Social Security's payment rule moves a payment day that lands on a weekend or a federal holiday to the closest earlier day that's neither ([20 CFR 404.1807](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/20/404.1807)). The rest of November stays put: Nov. 3 for people paid on the 3rd, then Nov. 18 and Nov. 25. November's SSI payment already went out on Friday, Oct. 30, because Nov. 1 was a Sunday.

Whether your branch opens on Nov. 11 is up to your bank, so check its holiday notice before you go. Branch hours decide less than you'd think, though. The Fed observes the holiday, and the federal rules on check holds and savings accounts don't count Wednesday, Nov. 11, as a business day, even at a branch that's open.

## What stops on Nov. 11

The Fed's holiday list for 2026 puts Veterans Day on Nov. 11, followed by Thanksgiving on Nov. 26 and Christmas on Dec. 25. The [Federal Reserve Banks' holiday schedule](https://www.frbservices.org/about/holiday-schedules/) also gives exact times for FedACH, the Fed's batch system for ACH payments such as direct deposits and standard bank-to-bank transfers. Processing stops at 11:30 p.m. Eastern on Tuesday, Nov. 10, and doesn't start again until 5:30 p.m. Eastern on Wednesday. For payments in that pipeline, Thursday, Nov. 12, is the next business day.

Federal deposit rules follow the same calendar. Regulation CC, which limits how long a bank can hold deposited funds, defines a business day as any day except a Saturday, a Sunday or a short list of holidays, and it names Nov. 11 by date (12 CFR 229.2(g)). It defines a banking day as the part of a business day when a bank office is open to the public for substantially all of its banking functions (12 CFR 229.2(f)). So even a branch with its lights on can't turn Nov. 11 into a banking day under that rule. Regulation DD, the Truth in Savings rule that covers savings accounts, points straight to the federal holiday list in 5 U.S.C. 6103(a) (12 CFR 1030.2(g)). Debit card and electronic transfer disputes are the exception: Regulation E counts any day your bank's offices are open for substantially all business functions (12 CFR 1005.2(d)), so there the answer depends on your bank.

## Moving money out of savings that week

If your bank sends a transfer by ACH and it normally arrives the next business day, a request made Tuesday lands Thursday at the earliest. One made after Tuesday's cutoff may not go out until Thursday. If the transfer screen shows an expected delivery date, plan around that date, not the day you hit send.

So the practical deadline moves up. If a mortgage payment, rent or a card bill is due on Nov. 11 or 12 and the money is in a high-yield account at another bank, I'd start the transfer no later than Monday, Nov. 9, and wait to see it post before counting on it.

The interest at stake is small. For an interest-bearing savings account, Regulation DD says interest has to start accruing no later than the business day Reg CC sets, which is the business day the bank receives credit for the funds (12 CFR 1030.7(c) and 229.14(a)). Going the other way, it has to accrue until the day the money is withdrawn. The [CFPB's official interpretation](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1030/7/) gives the example of a debit on a Tuesday, with interest owed through Monday. So money leaving savings earns through the day before the debit, wherever it sits afterward. Money coming in is the side that can lose a day: a deposit the bank gets credit for on Thursday instead of Wednesday may start earning a day later. On $10,000 at 4% a year, one day is worth a little over $1.

Instant payments skip that pause. The Fed's [FAQ on its FedNow Service](https://www.federalreserve.gov/paymentsystems/fednow_faq.htm) says it lets people send and receive payments within seconds, at any time of day, on any day of the year. Both banks have to be signed up, though, and the Fed says you probably won't even see the FedNow name in your bank's app. If your bank offers an instant transfer option, check before Wednesday whether the receiving bank can take it.

## Check holds around the holiday

Reg CC's hold limits apply to transaction accounts like checking. Its definition of an account leaves out savings deposits (12 CFR 229.2(a)), so a check deposited straight into a savings account isn't covered by this schedule. For a checking deposit, the clock works off two rules. Funds deposited on a day that isn't a banking day, or after the bank's cutoff, can be treated as deposited on the next banking day (12 CFR 229.19(a)(5)). And the deadlines count business days, which skip Nov. 11.

Some checks get next-day availability: a Treasury check deposited into the payee's own account, for instance, or a cashier's check deposited with a teller into the payee's account, on a special deposit slip if the bank requires one (12 CFR 229.10(c)(1)). Take that cashier's check to an ATM instead and the limit becomes the second business day (12 CFR 229.10(c)(2)). The first $275 of other check deposits in a day, counted across all your accounts at that bank, is also due the next business day. For an ordinary local check, the outer limit is the second business day (12 CFR 229.12(b)). For a deposit at your own bank that beats the cutoff, the latest dates line up like this:

| Check deposited | Counts as deposited | Next-day items and first $275 | Ordinary local check |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mon., Nov. 9 | Mon., Nov. 9 | Tue., Nov. 10 | Thu., Nov. 12 |
| Tue., Nov. 10 | Tue., Nov. 10 | Thu., Nov. 12 | Fri., Nov. 13 |
| Wed., Nov. 11 | Thu., Nov. 12 | Fri., Nov. 13 | Mon., Nov. 16 |
| Thu., Nov. 12 | Thu., Nov. 12 | Fri., Nov. 13 | Mon., Nov. 16 |

A Tuesday deposit made after the cutoff lands in the Wednesday row. These are the standard limits. Deposits at an ATM your bank doesn't own and some deposits in Alaska, Hawaii and U.S. territories run on longer schedules (12 CFR 229.12(e)-(f)). For ordinary check deposits, a bank can also add one business day before you can take the money out as cash or by similar means, such as an electronic payment, though $550 of it, on top of the $275, has to be available by 5 p.m. on the regular date (12 CFR 229.12(d)). Banks can release money sooner than the rule requires. They can also hold longer under Reg CC's exceptions, for example for the part of a day's check deposits over $6,725 or for a new account in its first 30 calendar days, unless every owner had another account at that bank, at least 30 days old, sometime in the 30 days before opening this one (12 CFR 229.13). The piece on [funds availability rules](/2026/09/05/why-your-deposit-is-not-available-yet-funds-availability-rules/) walks through those exceptions.

Direct deposits into checking run on a separate clock in the same rule. Money from an electronic payment has to be available by the business day after the banking day the bank receives it, meaning the bank has both the funds and the account details (12 CFR 229.10(b)). A payment the bank receives Thursday has to be available by Friday at the latest.

## Social Security and SSI dates for November

Most Social Security benefits are paid on the second, third or fourth Wednesday of the month, depending on the birthday of the worker whose record you're paid on (20 CFR 404.1807(b)). A spouse collecting on a husband's or wife's record follows that person's birthday, not their own. People whose benefits come from an application filed before May 1997, and some others, including those who also get SSI, are paid on the 3rd. SSI itself is paid on the 1st, with the same rule about weekends and holidays (20 CFR 416.502).

| Group | Regular November date | Actual November date |
|---|---|---|
| Worker born 1st to 10th | Wed., Nov. 11 | Tue., Nov. 10 |
| Worker born 11th to 20th | Wed., Nov. 18 | Wed., Nov. 18 |
| Worker born 21st to 31st | Wed., Nov. 25 | Wed., Nov. 25 |
| Paid on the 3rd | Tue., Nov. 3 | Tue., Nov. 3 |
| SSI | Sun., Nov. 1 | Fri., Oct. 30 |

The early payment isn't extra money. It's November's payment a day sooner, and the next one for that group comes on the second Wednesday of December, Dec. 9. December's own payment dates don't move: SSI on Tuesday, Dec. 1, the 3rd-of-the-month group on Thursday, Dec. 3, and the three Wednesday groups on Dec. 9, Dec. 16 and Dec. 23. January is a different story for two of those groups, because of New Year's Day.

## Treasury bills that week

Treasury's tentative auction calendar keeps auctions and issue dates off the holiday, and those dates can still change. It puts the 13- and 26-week bills on Monday, Nov. 9, as usual, and moves the 6-week bill from its usual Tuesday slot to that Monday as well, with all three issued Thursday, Nov. 12. The 17-week bill, normally a Wednesday auction, moves to Thursday, Nov. 12, alongside the 4- and 8-week bills, and is issued Tuesday, Nov. 17. Because the 6-week bill auctions a day earlier than usual, an order for it has to be in a day earlier too. How the [auction sets your rate](/2026/10/20/how-a-t-bill-auction-sets-your-rate-noncompetitive-bids-and-the-oct-15-rule/) doesn't change.

## Thanksgiving and Christmas work the same way

The pattern repeats twice before the year ends. FedACH stops at 11:30 p.m. Eastern on Wednesday, Nov. 25, and restarts at 5:30 p.m. on Thanksgiving Day. Christmas falls on a Friday, so processing stops at 11:30 p.m. on Thursday, Dec. 24, and restarts at 5:30 p.m. on Sunday, Dec. 27, which makes Monday, Dec. 28, the next business day. Reg CC's list includes the fourth Thursday in November and Dec. 25, so check holds stretch around both. The Wednesday Social Security dates on either side of Thanksgiving, Nov. 18 and Nov. 25, don't touch the holiday.

The next payment shift is a bigger one. New Year's Day 2027 is a Friday and Jan. 3 is a Sunday, so under the same two rules, January's SSI payment and the January payment for the 3rd-of-the-month group both come on Thursday, Dec. 31. Both groups get two deposits in December, and the Dec. 31 one is January's money.
