![I Bonds vs a High-Yield Savings Account: The 12-Month Lock](/assets/pin-images/2026-09-24-i-bonds-vs-a-high-yield-savings-account-the-12-month-lock.png)

The savings account holds $18,400 at an APY a rounding error away from the [FDIC](https://www.fdic.gov/national-rates-and-rate-caps) national average for savings, 0.38% as of August 17, 2026. A year of that is about $70. A Treasury page then quotes 4.26%, and the plan writes itself: empty the savings account into I bonds on Monday.

Three lines on that same Treasury page close the door on that version of the plan. [TreasuryDirect](https://www.treasurydirect.gov/savings-bonds/i-bonds/) sets the holding rule — "You can cash in (redeem) your I bond after 12 months." The exit cost: "if you cash in the bond in less than 5 years, you lose the last 3 months of interest." And the annual ceiling runs to "up to $10,000 in electronic I bonds" per Social Security Number in a calendar year, so $18,400 does not fit in one year no matter how the decision goes.

None of that makes the 4.26% wrong. It applies to bonds "issued May 1, 2026 to October 31, 2026," and the arithmetic behind it is published. What it makes wrong is the category. An I bond is a 30-year Treasury security with a year of complete ineligibility at the front and a penalty tail running to year five. A savings account is money that reaches a plumber this afternoon. Swapping one for the other changes what the money can do, and the account being emptied is usually the one the household relied on to absorb a surprise.

The second thing hidden in that date range is a deadline savers read backwards. A new rate is announced every November 1, and the late-October question is always whether the headline will rise or fall. That is the half of the rate that resets on a six-month clock no matter when the bond is bought. The half genuinely decided by buying before October 31 is the fixed rate, which attaches to the bond permanently.

## The composite rate and its two halves

The 4.26% is not a rate anyone chose. TreasuryDirect publishes the arithmetic as a formula, brackets and all: "Composite rate formula: Fixed rate + (2 x semiannual inflation rate) + (fixed rate x semiannual inflation rate)." The Treasury's [May 1, 2026 announcement](https://www.treasurydirect.gov/news/2026/release-05-01-rates/) gives the two inputs, describing the result as combining "a 0.90% fixed rate of return with the 3.34% annualized rate of inflation." Run those through the formula the way [TreasuryDirect's own worked example](https://www.treasurydirect.gov/savings-bonds/i-bonds/i-bonds-interest-rates/) does, as [0.0090 + (2 x 0.0167) + (0.0090 x 0.0167)], and 4.26% falls out.

The two halves behave nothing alike, and conflating them is where most I bond reasoning goes wrong.

The fixed rate is permanent. TreasuryDirect says it "never changes" and spells out how it is assigned: "We announce the fixed rate every May 1 and November 1. That fixed rate then applies, for the life of the bond, to all I bonds that we issue during the next 6 months." A bond bought in September 2026 carries 0.90% in the first slot of that formula through every future recalculation, all the way to year 30.

The inflation half runs on a clock. "The inflation rate changes every 6 months. We set the inflation rate every May 1 and November 1." Whatever is announced on November 1 replaces the current figure for every outstanding I bond eventually, regardless of when it was purchased. Nobody escapes an inflation reset by buying early, and nobody captures one by buying late.

A floor sits underneath the combination. TreasuryDirect: "if the inflation rate is so negative that it would pull the combined rate below zero, we don't let that happen. We stop at zero." A stretch of deflation stops the bond earning rather than shrinking what it is worth. A savings account has no equivalent published rule; what it has instead is a bank's discretion to reprice, which moves in one direction faster than the other, as covered in [Why Your Savings Rate Drops Faster Than It Rises](/2026/09/01/deposit-betas-why-savings-rates-fall-faster-than-they-rise/).

Accrual and compounding run on separate clocks. Both series "earn interest monthly. Interest is compounded semiannually," and TreasuryDirect describes the mechanic directly: "Twice a year, we add all the interest the bond earned in the previous 6 months to the main (principal) value of the bond... Over the next 6 months, we apply the new interest rate to that entire new value."

So the headline is an annual rate applied in six-month pieces. On $10,000 bought at 4.26%, the first six months add half of that composite, about $213, leaving the bond worth roughly $10,213 at the end of the first rate period. What the second six months adds is unknown on the day of purchase. This is the part the comparison with a savings APY quietly drops: only the first six months of the number are on offer.

## Twelve months, and the one documented exception

The lock is absolute and stated in one sentence: "You can cash in (redeem) your I bond after 12 months."

That is the sharpest structural difference from a certificate of deposit, which gets lumped in with it as "locked money." A CD is not locked. A CD has a price, which federal deposit rules require the bank to put in writing before the account is opened, stating "how it is calculated, and the conditions for its assessment," as worked through in [CD Early Withdrawal Penalties: The Disclosure and the Deduction](/2026/09/22/cd-early-withdrawal-penalties-the-disclosure-and-the-deduction/). An I bond in month seven has no price. The request cannot be made.

One documented exception exists. TreasuryDirect's page for bondholders affected by a disaster says bonds can be cashed "Even if they are less than a year old," and adds the parenthetical that explains why the line is there at all: "(Normally, bonds can't be cashed in their first year.)" Eligibility turns on living in "an area that has had an official disaster declaration," which is checked against FEMA's list rather than described to a customer service representative. A declared disaster is not the same category of event as a transmission failing.

The dating runs slightly in the buyer's favor. "I bonds earn interest from the first day of the month you buy them," so a purchase made on October 28 collects the whole of October. It does not shorten the year of ineligibility.

After the year is up, the bond does not have to be cashed whole. TreasuryDirect permits redeeming "Any amount of $25 or more to the penny," with two conditions: "If you cash only part of what a bond is worth, you must leave at least $25 in your account," and "If you cash only part of what a bond is worth, you only get the interest on the part you cash." That is cleaner than a partial CD withdrawal, which can reprice the balance left behind, and it is available only after the twelve months have run.

The consequence is definitional. An emergency fund is the money available in the week something breaks, not the money that earns the most over a year, and anything with a twelve-month ineligibility window fails that test by construction. That is a different objection from calling it a bad investment. The layering question is covered in [A Beginner's Guide to Creating an Emergency Fund With Different Financial Products](/2026/08/17/a-beginners-guide-to-creating-an-emergency-fund-with-different-financial-products/), and the separate confusion about how freely a savings account can be tapped is in [The Savings Withdrawal Limit Was Repealed. Your Bank Kept It Anyway](/2026/09/10/the-savings-withdrawal-limit-was-repealed-your-bank-kept-it-anyway/).

## The three-month forfeiture in dollars

The second cost is quoted in time, not money: "if you cash in the bond in less than 5 years, you lose the last 3 months of interest." Converting that into dollars is the only way to see how much of the headline it takes.

TreasuryDirect nets the forfeiture out of the number on screen: for bonds under five years old, the values shown in the account and in the savings bond calculator already exclude the last three months of interest. The balance a holder sees at month fourteen is what would actually arrive. That removes one unpleasant surprise and creates another: the account appears to stall for a quarter after every purchase.

Take a $10,000 bond bought at 4.26%. The illustration holds the composite constant across every period, which will not happen in reality, since only 0.90% of it is fixed. Holding it constant isolates what the forfeiture costs rather than blending it with an inflation guess.

| Cashed at | Value at a constant 4.26% | Three months forfeited | Net proceeds | Annualized |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 12 months | $10,430.54 | about $108.77 | about $10,321.77 | about 3.2% |
| 24 months | $10,879.61 | about $113.45 | about $10,766.16 | about 3.8% |
| 60 months | $12,346.20 | none | $12,346.20 | about 4.3% |

Three readings come out of that table.

The forfeiture is not a flat fee. It is three months of whatever the bond is earning at the time, on whatever the bond is worth at the time, so the dollar figure grows while its proportional bite shrinks. On a bond cashed at the earliest possible moment it takes roughly a quarter of everything earned.

The minimum-hold case is the honest comparison. A saver who holds exactly the twelve months required is not buying 4.26%. Under this illustration they are buying something closer to three and a quarter percent, for money that was unreachable throughout. Against a competitive savings account that stayed liquid, that is a narrower contest than the headline suggests, and it can lose outright if the second six-month rate comes in low.

The penalty disappears at five years, which is also the point at which the horizon has stopped resembling a savings account at all. Savers who want it gone without the wait are describing a [no-penalty CD](/2026/08/28/no-penalty-bump-up-and-add-on-cds-when-the-variants-pay/), which trades rate for exactly that.

## The $10,000 ceiling per Social Security Number

The limit is stated per person, per year, per series: "In any one calendar year for one Social Security Number, you may buy up to: $10,000 in electronic EE bonds [and] $10,000 in electronic I bonds." The two are separate buckets, not a shared cap. At the other end, "You can buy an electronic savings bond for any amount from $25 to $10,000 to the penny," so there is no meaningful minimum.

The paper route people remember from tax season is closed. TreasuryDirect states it flatly: "As of January 1, 2025, all new I bonds are electronic." The tax-refund purchase that once allowed an extra allocation in paper form is no longer an available answer to the ceiling.

Who can hold them is broader than the per-SSN framing implies. The owner "must have a Social Security Number" and be a "United States citizen," "United States resident," or "Civilian employee of the United States." Beyond that, "You may also register the bond in the name of a trust or estate. An electronic bond in TreasuryDirect also can be in the name of a corporation, partnership, or other entity." A minor can hold them, with the qualifier that "The child needs a TreasuryDirect account that is linked to the account of a parent or other adult custodian" — a structure with its own tax consequences, set out in [Savings Accounts for Kids: Custodial Rules and the Kiddie Tax](/2026/09/17/savings-accounts-for-kids-custodial-rules-and-the-kiddie-tax/).

The phrase carrying the most weight is "in any one calendar year." The allowance does not accrue and does not roll forward. An allowance unused on December 31 is gone, and two purchases six weeks apart, one in late December and one in early January, draw on two separate years. That is the only lever that moves the ceiling without adding a second person or entity.

Run the ceiling against a real cash position and the shape becomes obvious. A household spending $5,000 a month with a six-month buffer is holding $30,000. Converting that takes three calendar years at $10,000 a year for one SSN, and each tranche is separately unreachable for its own twelve months. That describes a ladder, with rungs opening in sequence, rather than a pile of accessible cash — the same reasoning applied to deposits is in [Building a CD Ladder When 12-Month CDs Pay the Most](/2026/08/19/building-a-cd-ladder-when-12-month-cds-pay-the-most/).

## What the savings side actually pays

The other half of the comparison is usually supplied by a headline rather than a statement, and the FDIC publishes two different numbers that get mixed up constantly.

The first is the national average. The FDIC's Monthly Rate Cap Information as of August 17, 2026 puts savings at 0.38%, money market accounts at 0.63%, and the 12-month CD at 1.71%. The methodology is stated on the same page: these are "The average of rates paid by all insured depository institutions and credit unions for which data is available, with rates weighted by each institution's share of domestic deposits." Weighting by deposit share is what makes the savings figure so low: the largest deposit books belong to branch networks that never repriced.

The second is the rate cap, a supervisory ceiling rather than a market observation. The FDIC sets it as "the higher of: (1) the national rate plus 75 basis points; or (2) 120 percent of the current yield on similar maturity U.S. Department of the Treasury ([U.S. Treasury](https://www.treasurydirect.gov/)) obligations plus 75 basis points." On the same August table, that formula puts the savings cap above four percent, more than ten times the average printed beside it. No bank is obliged to pay the cap and it is not an offer to anyone. What it reveals is that the regulator's own arithmetic assumes deposit rates well above the deposit-weighted average are ordinary.

Which is the practical point. The comparison worth running is not 4.26% against 0.38%. It is 4.26% against the APY printed on the statement of the account the money would leave. The FDIC does not publish what competitive online institutions pay, and the gap between a repriced online account and a legacy branch account is often larger than the gap between either of them and an I bond. A saver who has not looked up their own APY is comparing a verified Treasury figure against a blank.

A savings account is an insured deposit, covered to $250,000 per depositor, per insured bank, per ownership category, with the stacking rules described in [Common Mistakes With Understanding FDIC Insurance Limits and How to Avoid Them](/2026/08/18/common-mistakes-with-understanding-fdic-insurance-limits-and-how-to-avoid-them/). An I bond is not a deposit and is not FDIC-insured. It is a Treasury obligation recorded in a TreasuryDirect account, so the per-bank limits neither cover it nor are consumed by it.

## The November 1 reset and the October 31 question

Now the date on the front of the page. Two separate decisions hide inside "buy before October 31."

An October purchase locks one thing permanently. The fixed rate is 0.90% and it attaches to the bond for life. Every future composite the bond earns, in 2031 and in 2051, is computed with 0.90% sitting in the first slot of the formula. A bond bought on November 1 or later carries whatever fixed rate is announced that morning, also for life. This is the only part of the decision with permanent consequences, and it does not appear in the headline number.

The other decision is when the reset reaches a particular bond, and the answer is not November 1. TreasuryDirect schedules rate changes by issue month, not by announcement date. A bond issued in October has its rates change on "April 1 and October 1." A bond issued in November changes on "May 1 and November 1." Every month of the year has its own pair.

Follow that through. A bond bought in October 2026 earns 4.26% from October 1, 2026 through the end of March 2027, and picks up the rate announced on November 1, 2026 only from April 1, 2027. A bond bought in November 2026 takes the newly announced number immediately, for its first six months.

Restated: the October buyer gets six months at a published rate and then runs six months behind the announcement calendar. The November buyer takes the new number straight away, better or worse, and locks the new fixed rate for life. Both receive every future inflation adjustment.

So the question that matters is not whether the headline will be higher or lower on November 1. It is two narrower questions. Is a known 4.26% for the next six months preferable to an unknown figure for the same six months? And is 0.90% fixed for thirty years better than the fixed rate the Treasury announces that day, which nobody outside the announcement knows in advance?

A forecast of the inflation half is not the input people think it is. TreasuryDirect describes it as changing "every 6 months, based on inflation," a calculation applied to published data rather than a judgment call, and it reaches the bond on schedule regardless of purchase month. The fixed rate is the discretionary piece, and it is the one that cannot be revisited.

Sitting under both questions is the calendar year. October and November purchases both consume the 2026 allowance; the difference between them is six months of rate timing on at most $10,000. A purchase pushed into January consumes 2027's allowance and leaves 2026's unused permanently, which moves more money than the reset debate does.

## The tax line that runs the other way

Interest on an I bond is taxed at one level. TreasuryDirect's comparison table reads "Federal income tax: Yes. State and local income tax: No." Interest on a savings account is taxed at every level that taxes income where the depositor lives.

Timing is the larger structural difference, and it is elective. A holder may "put off (defer) reporting the interest until you file a federal income tax return for the year in which you actually get the interest," or instead "report the interest each year even though you don't actually get the interest then." Most holders take the first option by default.

Savings interest has no such choice. It is credited monthly, reported annually, and taxed annually, so a portion of every year's yield leaves before it can compound. An I bond held under the cash method produces no federal tax event at all until it is cashed. The paperwork follows the cash: "You get a Form 1099-INT for the year in which you get the interest," and for electronic holdings "your 1099-INT is available in your account by January 31 of the following year."

Two consequences follow, and they run opposite ways.

Deferral compounds gross. Every dollar of interest that would have gone to tax in a savings account stays in the bond earning the composite rate for the rest of the holding period, so the after-tax gap between the two products is wider than the stated rate gap. The mechanics of that drag on a taxable account are worked through in [The Tax Bite on Savings Interest: What Your Real Yield Is](/2026/08/26/the-tax-bite-on-savings-interest-what-your-real-yield-is/).

Deferral also concentrates. Years of accrued interest land in a single return in the year of redemption, which can push the total into a worse bracket than a long string of small annual amounts would have occupied. Anyone with a choice about when to trigger a large redemption is making a bracket decision, not only a rate decision, and the annual-reporting election exists partly for that reason.

The state exemption is worth more than it looks in a high-tax state. At a hypothetical 5% state rate, a fully taxable account has to pay roughly 4.5% to leave the same amount in hand as a 4.26% instrument exempt from state tax, before any federal interactions.

An education exclusion sits off to one side of this comparison. TreasuryDirect notes that "Using the money for higher education may keep you from paying federal income tax on your savings bond interest," and routes the eligibility rules and income restrictions to a separate page. A tax professional is the right check on how any of this lands on a particular return.

## Where each one sits

The two products are not competing for the same dollar, which is why the head-to-head framing keeps producing bad answers.

Money that might be needed this week has one home, and the rate on it is secondary. Past twelve months, an I bond, a term deposit, or a mix all come into range, and there the comparison is genuine: 4.26% for six months and an unknown rate after, against the FDIC average of 1.71% for a 12-month CD as of August 17, 2026 or whatever a competitive institution posts. At five years the I bond's structure stops costing anything — the forfeiture has expired, the deferral has had room to compound, and the fixed rate is still working.

The headline comparison fails because the two rates are set by different things. A bank's savings APY is a pricing decision, revisable at the bank's convenience, which is why it can fall the week after it was checked. The I bond's composite is an announcement on a published schedule. Comparing them at a single instant treats both as fixed, and neither one is.

## Common questions

**Can an I bond be cashed before twelve months if the money is needed?** No, outside one documented exception. The rule is "You can cash in (redeem) your I bond after 12 months," and TreasuryDirect's disaster page is the only route around it, allowing redemption "Even if they are less than a year old" for holders in "an area that has had an official disaster declaration." There is no hardship provision and no fee that buys an early exit.

**Does the three-month forfeiture apply at exactly five years?** The wording is "if you cash in the bond in less than 5 years, you lose the last 3 months of interest," so at five years and beyond it stops. Before that point, note that TreasuryDirect already subtracts it from the value displayed for bonds under five years old, so the balance on screen is what would actually be paid.

**Can a couple buy $20,000 in one year?** The limit is written per Social Security Number: "In any one calendar year for one Social Security Number, you may buy up to: $10,000 in electronic EE bonds [and] $10,000 in electronic I bonds." Two people with two SSNs and two TreasuryDirect accounts each have their own I bond allowance, and the EE allowance sits alongside it rather than inside it. Trusts, estates, corporations and partnerships can also be registered owners.

**What happens to an I bond if inflation turns negative?** TreasuryDirect caps the downside: "if the inflation rate is so negative that it would pull the combined rate below zero, we don't let that happen. We stop at zero." A deflationary stretch stops the bond earning for that period rather than reducing what it is worth, and the fixed rate stays attached throughout.

**Do I bonds use up FDIC coverage at a bank?** No. They are Treasury securities recorded in a TreasuryDirect account rather than deposits at an insured institution, so the $250,000 per-depositor, per-bank, per-ownership-category limits neither cover them nor are consumed by them.

Every number on the Treasury side of this comparison is published: 4.26% for bonds issued through October 31, 0.90% fixed for the life of those bonds, twelve months before redemption is possible, three months of interest forfeited before year five, $10,000 per Social Security Number per calendar year. The number on the other side is not published anywhere, because it is specific to one account at one bank. It is on the statement, and it is the only half of this comparison a saver has to go looking for.
