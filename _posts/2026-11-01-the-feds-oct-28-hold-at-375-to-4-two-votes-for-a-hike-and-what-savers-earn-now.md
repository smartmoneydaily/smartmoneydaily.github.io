On Oct. 28, 2026, the Federal Open Market Committee left its target for the federal funds rate at 3.75% to 4%. That's the range it moved to on Sept. 16 with a quarter-point increase. This time the vote was 10 to 2, and both officials who voted no, Lisa D. Cook and Beth M. Hammack, wanted to raise the range by another quarter point.

So the Fed didn't move, but the two dissents were for going up, not down. The statement itself didn't say which way the next decision will go. For savers, the more practical question is how far [national average deposit rates](https://www.fdic.gov/national-rates-and-rate-caps) sit from the rates the Fed now pays banks, and on Oct. 28 that gap was wide.

## A hold that kept the September tone

The October statement says economic activity "is expanding at a strong pace," that job gains have kept pace with the workforce, and that the unemployment rate "has changed little." Then: "Inflation remains elevated."

Compared with September, two lines in the description changed. The Sept. 16 statement, which came with a 12-0 vote for the hike, said economic activity was "expanding at a solid pace," and that day's action "will support a timelier return to the Committee's 2 percent goal." In October, "solid" became "strong," and the line about policy became a wait-and-see sentence: "The accumulation of more information will help the Committee determine the policy stance appropriate to ensure a timely return of inflation to the Committee's 2 percent goal."

Both statements end with the same short sentence: "The Committee will deliver price stability."

My reading is that this was a pause, not a turn. Two votes for a hike and a statement that still calls inflation elevated don't sound like a committee getting ready to cut.

## The rates the Fed set for banks

A separate implementation note puts numbers on the decision. Effective Oct. 29, the Fed pays 3.90% on reserve balances, the money banks keep in their accounts at the Fed. Its overnight reverse repo facility offers 3.75%, with a per-counterparty limit of $160 billion a day, and standing overnight repos run at 4.0%. The primary credit rate, what banks pay to borrow at the discount window, stays at 4.0%.

The 3.90% figure is the one I'd keep in mind. It's what a bank earns on cash it leaves at the Fed overnight. The rate on your savings account is a separate decision each bank makes, and it doesn't have to follow.

## Savings and CD averages from the FDIC

The FDIC's national rates show how wide the gap is. As of October 19, 2026, the FDIC put the national average for savings accounts at 0.38% and for money market accounts at 0.63%.

| Account type (FDIC national average, Oct. 19, 2026) | Rate |
|---|---|
| Savings | 0.38% |
| Interest checking | 0.07% |
| Money market | 0.63% |
| 3-month CD | 1.14% |
| 6-month CD | 1.39% |
| 12-month CD | 1.76% |
| 24-month CD | 1.55% |
| 36-month CD | 1.39% |
| 60-month CD | 1.41% |

The national savings average was well below the 3.90% paid on reserve balances. And the 12-month CD average was higher than the 24-, 36- and 60-month averages, so the longer CD terms listed had lower national average rates.

National averages don't show the rate any one bank offers. They're still a useful check on your own account. If your savings rate is near 0.38%, you're earning about the October national average, whatever your bank paid before September.

## Treasury bill yields since the September hike

Most Treasury bills are auctioned weekly (52-week bills every four weeks), and Treasury publishes their market rates every business day, so they're a quick read on short-term rates. Here are Treasury's daily bill rates, stated as coupon equivalents, on the day before the September hike and on the day of the October decision.

| Bill | Sept. 15, 2026 | Oct. 28, 2026 |
|---|---|---|
| 4-week | 3.85% | 3.97% |
| 13-week | 4.07% | 4.11% |
| 26-week | 4.21% | 4.33% |
| 52-week | 4.36% | 4.47% |

All four yields were higher on Oct. 28 than on Sept. 15, by 0.04 to 0.12 percentage point. On Oct. 28 the longer bills also yielded more than the shorter ones.

To make the gap concrete, compare $10,000 deposited in savings or a CD with a bill carrying a $10,000 face value. If the rate held for a year, $10,000 at the 0.38% savings average earns about $38, and at the 1.76% average for a 12-month CD, about $176. A bill works differently: you buy it below face value and it pays the full face value at maturity. At the Oct. 28 bank discount rate of 4.27% for 52-week bills, a $10,000 bill would have cost about $9,568, and it would pay $10,000 at maturity, a gain of about $432 before federal tax. An actual auction result may differ from that day's market rate.

TreasuryDirect lists federal tax on bill interest and no state or local tax, which matters if you live in a state with an income tax.

## The September projections

In September, Fed officials also published their own projections. The median for the federal funds rate was 4.1% at the end of 2026, 4.1% at the end of 2027, 3.9% at the end of 2028, 3.6% at the end of 2029, and 3.2% over the longer run.

The range set on Oct. 28 has a midpoint of 3.875%. One more quarter-point increase would put the midpoint at 4.125%, close to that 4.1% median for year-end 2026. The only scheduled meeting left this year is Dec. 8-9. The same projections had PCE inflation at 3.7% for 2026 and core PCE at 3.4%.

These medians aren't promises, and they don't say when any single move would happen. Each is the middle of the participants' individual estimates in September, and the year-end medians only came down starting with the 2028 projection.

## Savings, a CD or bills after the hold

None of this tells you what to do with your own cash, but it does change the tradeoffs.

If you lock a fixed-rate CD and the Fed raises rates in December, you'll keep the rate you locked. If you stay in a savings account, you're relying on your bank to raise its rate, and the national savings average was 0.38% on Oct. 19. A bill held to maturity keeps the yield you bought it at, but bills mature in weeks or months, so the next one you buy is priced at the rates of that day.

I don't think the December meeting is the thing to watch first. On Oct. 28, bill yields ran from 3.97% to 4.47%, while the national savings average was 0.38%. That difference is many times larger than a quarter-point move. If my own savings sat near the national average, that gap is what I'd look at first, and December would come second.

Keep the basics in place, too. The FDIC says deposits are insured to at least $250,000 at each FDIC-insured bank. Treasury bills aren't bank deposits, so FDIC insurance doesn't apply to them.

## Common questions

**Did the Fed raise rates in October 2026?** No. On Oct. 28 it kept the federal funds target at 3.75% to 4%, the range it set on Sept. 16. Two of the 12 voters, Lisa D. Cook and Beth M. Hammack, preferred a quarter-point increase.

**Will my savings account rate change because of this?** Not automatically. Banks set their own deposit rates. The FDIC's national savings average was 0.38% on Oct. 19, far below the 3.90% the Fed pays on reserve balances.

**When is the next Fed decision?** At the end of the Dec. 8-9 meeting, which comes with a new set of economic projections.
