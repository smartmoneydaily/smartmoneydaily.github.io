On Nov. 9 the Treasury Department's Financial Crimes Enforcement Network, or FinCEN, and the Fed's Board of Governors [withdrew a proposal](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/11/09/2026-22809/threshold-for-the-requirement-to-collect-retain-and-transmit-information-on-funds-transfers-and) they had published on Oct. 27, 2020. It would have cut the threshold in two Bank Secrecy Act rules from **$3,000** to **$250** for transfers that begin or end outside the United States. One rule covers the records an institution keeps on a transfer; the other covers the information it has to pass to the next institution in the chain. The notice says the agencies "will not take further action" on it.

So a wire from your savings to a relative abroad is still measured against the $3,000 line in rules issued in January 1995. Below $3,000, those two rules don't reach it. At $3,000 or more, they do.

That doesn't settle the matter for good. The agencies say they pulled the proposal "to provide certainty to the public that no final rule will be issued without an additional opportunity for public comment," which leaves room for a new one later. The notice doesn't raise the $3,000 figure, either. And it has nothing to do with the other change people sending money abroad ran into this year, a 1% federal tax on transfers paid for with cash, a money order or a cashier's check.

## What a bank keeps when you wire $3,000 or more

The recordkeeping rule for banks is [31 CFR 1020.410(a)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-31/subtitle-B/chapter-X/part-1020/subpart-D/section-1020.410). On a funds transfer of $3,000 or more, the bank sending it has to keep your name and address, the amount, the execution date, any payment instructions you gave and the identity of the recipient's bank. It also keeps as many of these as come with the order: the recipient's name and address, the recipient's account number and "any other specific identifier of the beneficiary." Under 31 CFR 1010.430(d) the records are kept for five years, and the bank has to be able to find them by your name, and by account number if you're an established customer with an account used for transfers.

Banks collect less from people they already know. Under 31 CFR 1010.100(p), an established customer is someone with an account at the institution, "including a loan account or deposit or other asset account," or someone whose name, address and taxpayer identification number (or, if there's none, alien identification number or passport number and issuing country) the institution keeps on file and relies on to provide financial services. When the sender isn't one, the bank records the name and address of whoever places the order plus an identifying number: a taxpayer identification number or, if there's none, an alien identification number or a passport number and issuing country, or a note that the person has none. An order placed in person also means checking ID and recording the document's type and number. For an order placed some other way, the bank keeps a copy or record of how the transfer was paid for. [How to Open a U.S. Savings Account Without an SSN](/2026/09/18/how-to-open-a-us-savings-account-without-an-ssn/) covers getting an account in the first place.

Some transfers fall outside the rule altogether. A transfer where the sender and the recipient are the same person and both accounts are at the same bank is excepted by 1020.410(a)(6)(ii). And the rule covers only "funds transfers," a term 31 CFR 1010.100(w) defines to leave out electronic fund transfers under the Electronic Fund Transfer Act, along with anything sent through an automated clearinghouse, an ATM or a point-of-sale system. Moving $5,000 from a high-yield savings account to checking by ACH isn't a funds transfer for this purpose. A wire is.

Neither rule sends anything to the government on its own. The 2020 proposal described the recordkeeping rule's job as "preserving an information trail about persons sending and receiving funds through the funds transfer system." Reports come from other rules. A bank files one on a cash transaction of more than $10,000, which [the post on $10,000 cash deposits](/2026/09/13/what-happens-when-you-deposit-more-than-10000-in-cash/) goes through. Buying a cashier's check or money order with $3,000 to $10,000 in currency leaves a purchase record of its own under 31 CFR 1010.415.

## Your name and address go with the payment

The second rule, the travel rule in [31 CFR 1010.410(f)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-31/subtitle-B/chapter-X/part-1010/subpart-D/section-1010.410), is about what travels with the money. It's written in terms of a "transmittal of funds," which the 2020 proposal explains covers a bank's funds transfer and its counterpart at nonbank institutions such as money transmitters. For a transmittal of $3,000 or more, the U.S. institution sending it generally has to put these in the outgoing order:

- your name and address, plus your account number if you paid from an account
- the amount and the execution date
- the identity of the recipient's institution, plus, to the extent they came with your order, the recipient's name and address, account number and any other specific identifier
- its own name and address, or a numerical identifier

A U.S. intermediary that handles the order has to pass along the required items it receives.

Both rules date to the same day. The 2020 proposal says the agencies issued the recordkeeping rule jointly on Jan. 3, 1995, and that "at the same time" FinCEN issued the travel rule. The $3,000 figure hasn't moved since. By the Bureau of Labor Statistics' [consumer price index](https://data.bls.gov/timeseries/CUUR0000SA0) for urban consumers (an average of 152.4 over the 12 months of 1995, and 321.943 for 2025), $3,000 in 1995 money is about $6,340 in 2025 money. By 2025, then, the unchanged threshold had less than half its 1995 buying power. Several commenters on the 2020 proposal made that argument, saying the agencies should raise the threshold for inflation instead of cutting it. The withdrawal leaves it at $3,000.

## Why the $250 plan was withdrawn

The 2020 proposal had two parts. One was the $250 threshold for transfers into or out of the country, under both rules. The other would have made clear that "money" in both rules covers convertible virtual currency and digital assets that have legal tender status.

The agencies received nearly 3,000 comments, and they note that "a significant majority" were form letters. Commenters generally opposed the lower threshold. Many argued it would burden financial institutions, smaller ones in particular, "disrupt the ability of consumers to send remittances overseas," possibly drive consumers "into less regulated marketplaces," and add to institutions' liability for holding personal information. Many also noted that some potentially illicit transactions under $3,000 were already being reported to FinCEN in suspicious activity reports. A number of commenters said the agencies hadn't offered enough analysis or data to justify the change.

The agencies give two reasons for dropping it. More than five years have passed, and they believe more public feedback would be useful before they adopt anything similar. And virtual currency markets have changed: the notice cites the GENIUS Act, the federal framework for payment stablecoins enacted July 18, 2025, and says any future change for virtual currency should be considered in light of those developments. The OCC's final rule under that law [had its own post in October](/2026/10/28/the-occs-stablecoin-rule-no-issuer-interest-no-fdic-coverage-and-2-day-redemption/).

For a household that sends a few hundred dollars home each month, the threshold was the part of the proposal that hit closest. An otherwise covered $400 wire abroad would have crossed the proposed $250 line. It stays below the $3,000 one.

## The 1% remittance tax depends on how you pay

A separate law already puts a price on some transfers abroad. For transfers made after Dec. 31, 2025, [26 U.S.C. 4475](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/4475) imposes a tax "equal to 1 percent of the amount" of a remittance transfer, but only when the sender pays the provider with "cash, a money order, a cashier's check, or any other similar physical instrument (as determined by the Secretary)." Money that comes straight out of an account at a bank, credit union or other listed institution that's subject to the Bank Secrecy Act is exempt, and so is a transfer paid with a debit or credit card issued in the United States. The sender owes the tax, and the provider collects it.

The terms come from the Electronic Fund Transfer Act. A remittance transfer is an electronic transfer a consumer asks a provider to send to someone located in a foreign country. The sender is a consumer in a U.S. state, a territory or D.C. sending money primarily for personal, family or household purposes. A provider is any person or financial institution that sends remittance transfers for consumers in the normal course of its business, whether or not you have an account there, so your bank or credit union can be one, not only a money transmitter.

The IRS filled in details in [proposed regulations](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/04/13/2026-07085/excise-tax-on-remittance-transfers) published April 13. The document lets providers and senders rely on it for transfers made after Dec. 31, 2025, as long as they follow it in full and consistently, until the first calendar quarter that begins on or after the day final rules are published. It sorts the usual ways of paying like this:

| How the sender pays the provider | Is the 1% tax due? |
|---|---|
| Cash | Yes |
| Money order, cashier's check or traveler's check | Yes, even if it was bought with money from a bank account |
| A check made out to you that the provider cashes, with the cash put toward the transfer | Yes, it's treated as cash |
| Direct payment from an account at a bank, credit union or other qualifying institution | No |
| Debit or credit card issued in the U.S. | No |
| Personal check made out to the provider | No |
| General-use prepaid card or store gift card | No, though a prepaid card bought with cash to fund a transfer may be treated as cash when avoiding the tax is a principal purpose |

The tax is figured on the amount sent, not on what you hand over. In the proposal's first example, a sender asks to send $1,000 and pays in cash, the provider adds a $5 promotional bonus and charges a $20 fee, and a $12 state tax applies. The 1% is charged on $1,005, so the tax is $10.05. The fee and the state tax stay out of the base.

For a regular sender the payment method adds up. Someone who sends $500 home every month in cash pays $5 a transfer, or $60 a year. The same transfers paid straight from a checking or savings account, or with a U.S. debit card, owe none of it. One $3,000 transfer paid in cash owes $30.

If the transfer is covered by Regulation E's remittance rules, the provider has to show you, before you pay, "any taxes collected on the remittance transfer by the provider," under Transfer Taxes or a similar label (12 CFR 1005.31(b)(1)(ii)). When the 1% applies, it's one of those taxes. Transfers of $15 or less fall outside Regulation E's remittance rules and, under the proposal, outside the tax.

## The 180-day window when a transfer abroad goes wrong

Regulation E's remittance rules come with their own error process. Under [12 CFR 1005.33](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-12/chapter-X/part-1005/subpart-B/section-1005.33), the provider generally has to receive your notice of an error within **180 days** after the disclosed date of availability. Errors include the sender paying the wrong amount, the recipient getting less than the disclosed amount and the money not being available by the disclosed date, each subject to exceptions in the rule. The provider has 90 days from your notice to investigate and decide, and it has to report the result within three business days after it finishes. A delay caused by a necessary investigation under the Bank Secrecy Act, sanctions requirements or the provider's own fraud screening doesn't count as a late-delivery error.

## What could still change

I don't expect Monday's notice to change anything a person sending money home would see at the counter. What I'd watch is what comes after it: the IRS's final rules on the 1% tax, whenever they replace the April proposal, and any new FinCEN plan for the threshold, which would now have to go out for public comment first.
