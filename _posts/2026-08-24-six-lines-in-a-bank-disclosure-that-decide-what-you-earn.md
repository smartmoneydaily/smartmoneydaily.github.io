Every deposit account comes with a disclosure document, and almost nobody reads it. That is a reasonable response to a wall of small type — but six specific lines in it decide what the account actually pays and what it can cost you. Finding those six takes about three minutes once you know where to look.

The document exists because federal rules require it. Banks must disclose account terms in a standard way before you open, and must tell you about certain changes afterward. The result is that the same six facts appear in every disclosure you will ever read, which makes comparison across banks far easier than the formatting suggests.

## Line 1: The annual percentage yield

The APY is the number to compare, and it is the only rate figure that is standardized across institutions. It reflects the effect of compounding over a year, so a stated rate and its APY differ whenever interest compounds more often than annually — and the APY is the one that already accounts for it.

Two things to check alongside it.

**Whether the rate is fixed or variable.** A CD's rate is fixed for its term. Savings, money market, and interest checking rates are variable, which means the bank can change them at any time. A disclosure will say so explicitly — look for language along the lines of "the rate may change at any time."

**Whether the APY assumes conditions.** Some accounts advertise a rate that applies only if you maintain a minimum balance, receive a direct deposit, or make a set number of transactions. The disclosure states the requirement; the marketing page frequently does not.

## Line 2: The balance tiers

Many accounts pay different rates at different balance levels, and the tiers are not always shaped the way you would expect. Two patterns are common:

- **Higher balance, higher rate** — the intuitive structure
- **Higher balance, lower rate** — a promotional rate capped at a threshold, with the standard rate above it

The second pattern is the one worth catching. An account paying an attractive rate on the first $10,000 and very little above it is a different product than the headline implies if you are placing $50,000.

The disclosure shows the tiers as a table. Find where your intended balance sits and read the rate for that row rather than the top one.

## Line 3: How the balance is computed

This determines what your interest is calculated on, and there are two common methods.

**Daily balance method.** Interest is applied to the balance in the account each day. Money you deposit mid-month earns from the day it arrives.

**Average daily balance method.** The balance is averaged across the period, and the rate is applied to that average.

For a stable balance these produce nearly identical results. For an account with large deposits and withdrawals during the month they can differ noticeably, and the daily balance method is generally the more favorable of the two.

Also check when interest is credited — monthly, quarterly, or at maturity — and whether interest is forfeited if you close the account before the crediting date. That last provision is easy to miss and can cost a full quarter of interest on a closure timed badly.

## Line 4: The fee schedule

Fees convert a good rate into a bad one faster than any rate change does. The ones worth finding:

- **Monthly maintenance fee**, and the specific conditions that waive it
- **Excess transaction fee**, if the account limits certain withdrawals per cycle
- **Below-minimum-balance fee**, and whether it is charged on a single dip or on an average
- **Outgoing transfer or wire fee**, which matters if you move money between institutions regularly
- **Paper statement fee**, an increasingly common charge that is usually waived by going electronic

Run the arithmetic on your own balance. A $12 monthly maintenance fee is $144 a year. On a $5,000 balance, that is close to 3 percent of the balance — enough to wipe out the entire interest on most savings accounts and then some. The waiver conditions matter more than the headline rate at that balance level.

## Line 5: The early withdrawal penalty, for CDs

Stated as a number of days or months of interest — 90 days, 180 days, sometimes 12 months on longer terms. Two details decide how much it can hurt.

**Whether it can reduce principal.** Some disclosures say the penalty "may reduce the principal balance." Others cap the penalty at interest earned. The second is materially better and it is stated in the document, not on the rate page.

**How it is computed.** On a $10,000 CD at 4% with a 180-day penalty: daily interest is $10,000 × 0.04 ÷ 365 = $1.0959, so the penalty is $1.0959 × 180 = $197.26. Break it at month three, having earned about $98.63, and you are roughly $98 short — taken from principal if the disclosure permits that.

## Line 6: Renewal and change terms

For CDs, whether the account renews automatically, into what term, at what rate, and how long the grace period runs. Automatic renewal into the bank's standard rate is the default at most institutions, and the grace period — typically 7 to 10 days after maturity — is the window to withdraw or change terms without penalty.

For variable-rate accounts, what notice you receive before a change. Under federal rules, a bank generally must give advance notice of a change that reduces the APY on an account where the rate was committed for a stated period. For an ordinary variable-rate account with no such commitment, the rate can drop without advance notice, which is why checking your current APY periodically is worth the two minutes.

## Putting it together

Here is what the six lines look like as a comparison worksheet for two accounts:

| Line | Account A | Account B |
|---|---|---|
| APY (at your balance) | | |
| Fixed or variable | | |
| Tier that applies to you | | |
| Balance method | | |
| Monthly fee, and waiver condition | | |
| Penalty / renewal terms | | |

Fill it in for both, and the decision usually makes itself. What the exercise most often reveals is not a rate difference but a fee or a condition that changes the ranking — which is precisely the information the headline rate is designed not to convey.

For context on where any given offer sits, [FDIC national average deposit rates](https://www.fdic.gov/national-rates-and-rate-caps) as of August 17, 2026 were 0.38% APY for savings, 0.63% for [money market account](/2026/08/22/money-market-account-vs-money-market-fund-whats-insured/)s, 0.07% for interest checking, and 1.71% for 12-month CDs. Those averages include institutions paying near zero, so a competitive account should sit well above them.

## One habit worth keeping

Save the disclosure as a PDF when you open the account, with the date in the filename. When a rate changes or a fee appears, the original terms are the reference point, and reconstructing them from a bank's current website is harder than it sounds.

## What the disclosure will not tell you

Two things matter and are not in the document.

**How the bank behaves over time.** A disclosure states that a variable rate may change; it does not say how quickly this bank lowers rates when the market moves, or whether it quietly lets long-standing accounts drift while advertising a better rate to new customers. That pattern only shows up if you check your own APY periodically.

**How the transfer experience works.** Nothing in the terms describes how long an outgoing ACH takes, whether there is a daily transfer cap, or how hard it is to reach a person when something goes wrong. For an account holding emergency money, those operational facts matter as much as the rate. Test a small transfer in and out shortly after opening, while nothing is urgent.

Neither gap is a reason to skip the disclosure. They are a reason not to treat it as the whole picture.

Account terms, fees, and disclosure requirements vary by institution and change over time. The document your bank provides at account opening governs — not this post.
