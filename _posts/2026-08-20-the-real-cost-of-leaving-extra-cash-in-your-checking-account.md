Your checking account is doing a job, and paying you is not part of it. As of August 17, 2026, the [FDIC](https://www.fdic.gov/national-rates-and-rate-caps) national average for interest checking is 0.07% APY. Savings averages 0.38%. Money market accounts average 0.63%. Those are national averages across thousands of insured institutions, so individual accounts sit on both sides of each figure — but in that snapshot, the account you touch most often is the one paying you the least.

The standard advice stops at "don't keep too much in checking," which is useless without a number attached. This post gives you the number. You'll get a method for calculating how much cash genuinely has to sit in checking to cover your month, the arithmetic on what the excess costs, and the settlement details — cutoff times, business days, holds — that quietly push people into carrying a far fatter checking balance than they need.

One thing before the math. The goal is not to squeeze checking down to zero. A buffer that's too thin can cost you more in a single overdraft or returned-payment fee than a year of extra yield on a few thousand dollars will earn. What you want is a deliberate number, sized to your own payment calendar, not a vague instinct that you should "probably move some over."

## Why Checking Pays So Little

Checking is an operations account. It clears debit swipes, absorbs autopay, holds direct deposit, and settles checks on days you can't predict. The usual explanation for the low rate is that banks treat checking as a service rather than as a way to raise funding, and you can see part of that logic from your own side of the counter: moving a checking account means rewiring direct deposit, updating every merchant that has your account number, and living through a few weeks of overlap.

You don't have to accept anyone's theory about why the gap exists, though. The verifiable part is what your own statement says. Look up the APY your checking account actually pays, compare it to the averages above, and treat the difference as the number that matters.

Worth knowing: the 0.07% figure is the average across *interest-bearing* checking accounts. Plenty of checking accounts pay nothing at all. If yours is one of them, the gap you're looking at isn't 0.31 or 0.56 percentage points — it's the full 0.38% or 0.63%.

## The Gap in Dollars

Take a household carrying $18,000 in checking, which turns out to be about $15,000 more than the month actually requires. APY already accounts for compounding, so multiplying a balance by the APY gives you roughly a year's interest if the balance stays put.

At the FDIC national averages as of August 17, 2026:

- **Checking at 0.07%:** $15,000 × 0.0007 = **$10.50 a year**
- **Savings at 0.38%:** $15,000 × 0.0038 = **$57.00 a year** — a gain of $46.50
- **Money market at 0.63%:** $15,000 × 0.0063 = **$94.50 a year** — a gain of $84.00
- **12-month CD at 1.71%:** $15,000 × 0.0171 = **$256.50 a year** — a gain of $246.00, with the money locked up and an early-withdrawal penalty if you break it

Eighty-four dollars a year is seven dollars a month. That is honest arithmetic on national averages, and it's fair to say it's not life-changing. But two things scale it up. First, an average has accounts sitting on both sides of it, so run the numbers again using the rate you are actually offered rather than the national figure — each additional full percentage point on $15,000 works out to another $150 a year. Second, this is a one-time setup that keeps paying. The transfer takes an afternoon; the yield difference recurs for as long as the balance sits there.

## Sizing the Working Buffer

Your checking balance should cover the longest stretch between incoming deposits, plus a cushion for the bills that vary. Here's the count, using a household spending $4,500 a month — $3,100 in fixed obligations (rent, insurance, utilities, subscriptions, card autopay) and about $1,400 in variable spending.

**Step 1 — Daily burn.** $4,500 ÷ 30 = **$150 a day**.

**Step 2 — Longest deposit gap.** Paid on the 1st and the 15th, the longest stretch between deposits runs about 16 days. $150 × 16 = **$2,400**.

**Step 3 — Variance cushion.** Add 25% for the months when the utility bill spikes or an annual premium lands. $2,400 × 1.25 = **$3,000**.

**Step 4 — Floor check.** If your bank waives its monthly fee only above a minimum balance, use the higher of that minimum and your calculated buffer. Don't earn $84 to pay a maintenance fee.

So: $3,000 stays, $15,000 moves. Rebuild the count if your income is irregular. Freelancers and commission earners should size the gap to the longest realistic dry spell, not the average one, and should expect a bigger checking buffer than a salaried household needs. That's not inefficiency; that's paying for the ability to sleep.

Note that this buffer is separate from an emergency fund. The buffer covers timing. The emergency fund covers events, and it belongs in the higher-yield account alongside the rest of the swept cash.

## Settlement Mechanics That Keep Cash Stuck

This is the part that actually causes over-funded checking accounts. People aren't ignorant of rates. They've been burned once by a transfer that took longer than expected, and they've been padding ever since.

**Business days are not calendar days.** ACH transfers between banks typically settle in one to three business days. Weekends and federal holidays don't count. A transfer you start Friday evening can post Tuesday, or Wednesday if Monday is a holiday.

**Cutoff times.** Institutions set a daily cutoff. Submit after it and your transfer is dated the next business day before the settlement clock even starts. Find yours and write it down.

**Push versus pull matters.** Initiating a transfer *from* the receiving bank (pulling) and initiating it *from* the sending bank (pushing) can run on different timelines and different hold policies, even between the same two accounts. Try both directions once with a small amount and record how many days each actually took.

**Holds on incoming deposits.** Money that has arrived is not always money you can spend. A deposit into a savings account may face an availability hold before it can be withdrawn again, which makes the return trip slower than the outbound one.

**Trailing debits.** A debit card authorization can post days after you swipe. Restaurant tips, gas station holds, and hotel authorizations settle on their own schedule. Checks you mailed clear whenever the recipient gets around to depositing them.

**Transfer limits still exist.** Regulation D's six-transfer-per-month limit on savings and money market accounts was suspended in 2020, but plenty of banks kept their own limits in place. Check whether yours does before you plan on frequent moves in both directions.

The fix isn't to keep an extra month in checking. It's to size the buffer to the slowest settlement window you've personally measured, and to make the return trip a known quantity rather than a gamble.

## Where the Swept Cash Belongs

| Account type | FDIC national average APY (as of Aug 17, 2026) | Best role |
|---|---|---|
| Interest checking | 0.07% | Bills, debit, the working buffer only |
| Savings | 0.38% | Buffer overflow and emergency fund |
| Money market | 0.63% | Same role, often with check or debit access |
| 3-month CD | 1.14% | Money with a known near-term date |
| 12-month CD | 1.71% | Money you're confident you won't touch |

The money market average sitting above the savings average is a fact about this snapshot, not a permanent law of nature, so compare the two directly whenever you open something new. One distinction to keep straight: a money market *deposit account* at a bank is FDIC-insured; a money market *mutual fund* at a brokerage is not.

If sweeping means opening an account at a second institution, that's a small side benefit. FDIC coverage is $250,000 per depositor, per insured bank, per ownership category, so spreading balances across two banks raises your total covered amount. Credit unions are insured by the [NCUA](https://ncua.gov/consumers/share-insurance-coverage) rather than the FDIC, at the same standard limit.

## When Sweeping Isn't Worth It

Skip it, or do it lightly, in these cases:

- **The excess is small.** On $2,000, the checking-to-money-market gap at national averages is about $11 a year. One overdraft fee erases years of that.
- **Your income timing is genuinely unpredictable.** Pay for the certainty. A larger buffer is a reasonable purchase.
- **Your checking account has a balance-based fee waiver.** Dropping below the threshold to chase a few dollars of yield is a losing trade.
- **You'd be tempted to overcorrect.** If moving money makes you likely to cut the buffer to the bone and then scramble, the yield isn't worth the stress.

## A Routine That Survives Busy Months

Automate the outbound leg and leave the return leg manual. Set a recurring transfer for the day after each payday — modest enough that it never threatens the buffer — so accumulation happens whether or not you're paying attention. Pulling money back should require a deliberate decision, which is exactly the friction you want.

Then review the buffer twice a year, or whenever a fixed cost changes materially: a new lease, a car payment ending, a switch from biweekly to semi-monthly pay. Buffers drift upward on their own if nobody checks them.

## Where to Start

Open your last three months of checking statements and find the lowest balance in each month. If that floor sits consistently higher than your calculated buffer, the difference is what's been sitting idle, and you now know roughly what it's costing you at the national averages above.

Then run the small test transfer out and back, and size your buffer to the number of business days it actually took rather than to the worst thing you can imagine. Once you have a real figure instead of a fear, the rest of the cash is free to go somewhere it earns.
