A saver with about $10,000 to park this month is looking at three choices: a 6-week Treasury bill, a 13-week bill, or a 6-month bank CD. The examples below use actual bill auction results and a hypothetical CD paying 4% APY. One difference that doesn't show up in the rate is the tax year the interest lands in. A 13-week bill issued on October 15, 2026 matures on January 14, 2027, and its interest belongs to 2027. A 6-week bill issued the same day matures on November 27, 2026, and its interest belongs to 2026. A 6-month CD can go either way, depending on when the bank credits the interest and whether you can withdraw it.

Most individuals report interest in the year they actually or constructively receive it. A Treasury bill's interest is the gap between the price paid and the face value, and IRS Publication 550 says you "generally" report it "when the bill is paid at maturity." For bank CDs with a term of one year or less, interest counts when you receive it or can take it "without paying a substantial penalty," which for a CD that pays all its interest at maturity, with no earlier access short of a substantial penalty, generally means the maturity year. Interest credited to a savings account counts when it's credited and available. Moving interest into 2027 doesn't make it tax-free. It changes which year's return, rates and income thresholds apply.

The rules below come from IRS [Publication 550, Investment Income and Expenses](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p550), the IRS [Instructions for Forms 1099-INT and 1099-OID](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i1099int), TreasuryDirect's pages on [Treasury bills](https://www.treasurydirect.gov/marketable-securities/treasury-bills/), [pricing](https://www.treasurydirect.gov/marketable-securities/understanding-pricing/) and [tax forms](https://www.treasurydirect.gov/marketable-securities/tax-forms-and-withholding/), and Treasury's [auction results data](https://fiscaldata.treasury.gov/datasets/treasury-securities-auctions-data/treasury-securities-auctions-data). The examples assume a calendar-year, cash-method individual holding these investments in a taxable account, not inside an IRA or 401(k); Publication 550 says its rules "do not apply to investments held in individual retirement arrangements (IRAs), section 401(k) plans, and other qualified retirement plans." This is general information about how the timing rules work, not tax or investment advice.

## The basic rule: the year you receive it

Publication 550 starts with accounting method. "Most individual taxpayers use the cash method. If you use this method, you generally report your interest income in the year in which you actually or constructively receive it."

"Constructively receive" is what makes interest taxable before you touch it. The publication says: "You constructively receive income when it is credited to your account or made available to you. You do not need to have physical possession of it." For bank accounts specifically, you're "considered to receive interest ... on any deposit or account in a bank, savings and loan association, or similar financial institution ... when they are credited to your account and subject to your withdrawal."

The publication then lists conditions that don't prevent constructive receipt. You still constructively receive the income "even if you must":

- "Make withdrawals in multiples of even amounts,"
- "Give a notice to withdraw before making the withdrawal,"
- "Withdraw all or part of the account to withdraw the earnings, or"
- "Pay a penalty on early withdrawals, unless the interest you are to receive on an early withdrawal or redemption is substantially less than the interest payable at maturity."

That last item is the key to CDs, covered below. The IRS instructions for banks and other payers describe the same idea from the other side: "Generally, interest is paid when it is credited or set apart for a person without any substantial limitation or restriction as to the time, manner, or condition of payment. The interest must be made available so that it may be drawn on at any time and its receipt brought within the control and disposition of the person."

For an ordinary savings or money market deposit account that credits interest monthly, the result is simple. Interest credited in December 2026 is 2026 income. Interest credited in January 2027 is 2027 income.

## Treasury bills: the interest arrives at maturity

A Treasury bill doesn't pay interest along the way. TreasuryDirect describes it: "Bills are sold at a discount or at par (face value). When the bill matures, you are paid its face value." Its pricing page adds: "The difference between the face value and the discounted price you pay is 'interest.'"

Publication 550 sets the timing: "The difference between the discounted price you pay for the bills and the face value you receive at maturity is interest income. Generally, you report this interest income when the bill is paid at maturity."

There's no yearly accrual for a bill held by a typical saver. Publication 550 says "The OID accrual rules generally do not apply to short-term obligations (those with a fixed maturity date of 1 year or less from date of issue)." That is the difference from a 5-year CD, which can create taxable interest every year before you can touch the money, as we explained in [Why You Owe Tax on a 5-Year CD You Cannot Touch Yet](/2026/09/25/why-you-owe-tax-on-a-5-year-cd-you-cannot-touch-yet/).

Two qualifications come with that rule:

- **You can elect to report it as it accrues.** Publication 550 says that when you buy a short-term obligation, "you generally can choose to include any discount and interest payable on the obligation in income currently." That means reporting the discount as it accrues each year, not moving the whole maturity payment into the purchase year. The election is broad and sticky: "This choice applies to all short-term obligations you acquire during the year and in all later years. You cannot change this choice without the consent of the IRS." Without that election, the usual maturity-year rule generally applies.
- **Some holders must accrue it.** The publication lists holders who must include the discount as it accrues, including an "accrual-basis taxpayer," banks, certain pass-through entities, and obligations held for sale to customers in a trade or business or identified as part of a hedging transaction. An ordinary cash-method individual holding bills as an investment generally isn't in any of those groups.

The tax treatment of the interest itself doesn't change with timing. "Interest income from Treasury bills, notes, and bonds is subject to federal income tax but is exempt from all state and local income taxes," according to Publication 550. TreasuryDirect's summary line says the same thing: "No state or local taxes." That state exemption is why bills can come out ahead of CDs for savers in high-tax states, which we covered in [Treasury Bills vs CDs: The Tax Break That Changes the Comparison](/2026/09/23/treasury-bills-vs-cds-the-tax-break-that-changes-the-comparison/).

## Which recent bills cross into 2027

TreasuryDirect sells bills in terms of "4, 6, 8, 13, 17, 26, and 52 weeks," auctioned weekly except the 52-week bill, which is auctioned every four weeks. The table below uses actual results from recent auctions, taken from Treasury's auction data. The interest column is the face value minus the auction price for $10,000 of bills.

| Term | Auction date | Issue date | Maturity date | High discount rate | Price per $100 | Interest on $10,000 face | Tax year of the interest |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 6-week | Oct. 13, 2026 | Oct. 15, 2026 | Nov. 27, 2026 | 3.980% | 99.524611 | $47.54 | 2026 |
| 13-week | Sept. 28, 2026 | Oct. 1, 2026 | Dec. 31, 2026 | 4.110% | 98.961083 | $103.89 | 2026 |
| 13-week | Oct. 13, 2026 | Oct. 15, 2026 | Jan. 14, 2027 | 4.085% | 98.967403 | $103.26 | 2027 |
| 26-week | Oct. 13, 2026 | Oct. 15, 2026 | Apr. 15, 2027 | 4.200% | 97.876667 | $212.33 | 2027 |
| 52-week | Sept. 29, 2026 | Oct. 1, 2026 | Sept. 30, 2027 | 4.400% | 95.551111 | $444.89 | 2027 |

The math for the 13-week bill issued October 15: $10,000 × 98.967403 ÷ 100 = $9,896.74 paid, and $10,000 − $9,896.74 = $103.26 of interest on January 14, 2027. That interest goes on the 2027 return, even though most of the bill's 91-day term falls in 2026.

TreasuryDirect's price formula reproduces the auction price. "Price = Face value (1 – (discount rate x time)/360)." For that bill: 100 × (1 − 0.04085 × 91 ÷ 360) = 98.9674028, which rounds to the published price of 98.967403 per $100.

The discount rate is not the same as an annual yield on the money you put in, because it's figured on the face value and a 360-day year. Dividing the interest by the price paid and scaling to 365 days gives a different figure: $103.26 ÷ $9,896.74 × 365 ÷ 91 = about 4.185%. That matches the "investment rate" Treasury published for the same auction. Neither number is calculated the same way as a CD's APY, so comparing a bill's rate with a CD's APY is approximate.

These are results from auctions that have already happened. Future auctions will set their own rates, and the table isn't a forecast.

## The December 31 maturity case

TreasuryDirect has a specific reporting rule for bills maturing on December 31: "If your bill matures and is due to pay on December 31 and it is a holiday or weekend, we pay you on the first business day of the new year. However, we show the payment on your 1099-INT the day the bill matures, not the year it is paid."

December 31, 2026 is a Thursday, so the 13-week bill in the table that matures that day pays that day. Its $103.89 of interest on $10,000 is 2026 income. The bill auctioned two weeks later, maturing January 14, 2027, puts its interest in 2027. Two bills with nearly the same rate and the same term, bought two weeks apart, land in different tax years.

## Bank CDs of one year or less

For CDs, Publication 550 has a specific paragraph:

"If you buy a certificate of deposit or open a deferred interest account, interest may be paid at fixed intervals of 1 year or less during the term of the account. You generally must include this interest in your income when you actually receive it or are entitled to receive it without paying a substantial penalty. The same is true for accounts that mature in 1 year or less and pay interest in a single payment at maturity. If interest is deferred for more than 1 year, see Original Issue Discount (OID), later."

What that means depends on how the particular CD handles interest, which is in its account disclosure. Here are four versions of the same hypothetical CD: $10,000, a 6-month term of 182 days, a 4% APY, opened October 15, 2026, maturing April 15, 2027. If all the interest stays in the CD until maturity, a 4% APY over 182 days works out to about $197 ($10,000 × (1.04^(182/365) − 1) = $197.49). A version that pays interest out monthly would earn a somewhat different total, set by its own rate and payment terms.

| How the CD handles interest | Tax year of the interest |
|---|---|
| All interest paid in one payment at maturity in April 2027 | 2027 |
| Interest paid out monthly to a linked savings account | Each payment in the year it's paid: if payments come on the 15th, the November and December 2026 payments in 2026, the rest in 2027 |
| Interest credited monthly to the CD itself, withdrawable without penalty | Each credit in the year it's credited, because it's available without a substantial penalty |
| Interest credited to the CD, but withdrawing it would cost a penalty | Depends on whether the penalty makes the interest available on early withdrawal "substantially less" than at maturity |

The last row is the one the publication leaves to the facts. A CD agreement that lets you withdraw credited interest freely puts that interest in the year it's credited. Interest generally belongs to the maturity year only if it is neither paid nor available earlier without a substantial penalty. The bank's 1099-INT for 2026 will reflect how the bank treated the account, and the account disclosure explains when interest is paid and credited.

Breaking a CD has its own timing. Publication 550 says the 1099-INT "will show the total amount of interest in box 1 and will show the penalty separately in box 2," and that "You can deduct the penalty on Schedule 1 (Form 1040), line 18." The penalty shows up on the 1099-INT for the year of the withdrawal. The payer instructions tell banks to "Enter interest or principal forfeited because of an early withdrawal of time deposits ... that is deductible from gross income by the recipient." We covered how that deduction works in [CD Early Withdrawal Penalties: The Disclosure and the Deduction](/2026/09/22/cd-early-withdrawal-penalties-the-disclosure-and-the-deduction/).

## How the 1099 forms will show it

Bank interest generally goes in box 1 of Form 1099-INT. The payer instructions say box 1 includes "amounts of $10 or more" that "are paid or credited to the person's account" by banks and similar institutions, along with "interest on bank deposits."

Treasury interest goes in box 3, including a bill's discount. The box 3 instructions say: "Enter interest on U.S. Savings Bonds, Treasury bills, Treasury notes, and Treasury bonds. Do not include in box 1." Publication 550 says "You should receive Form 1099-INT showing the interest (in box 3) paid to you for the year," and that box 3 "shows the interest income you received from U.S. savings bonds, Treasury bills, Treasury notes, and Treasury bonds."

For bills held directly at TreasuryDirect, the 1099 comes from Treasury: "If your securities are in your TreasuryDirect account, your 1099 is available at the beginning of each year." TreasuryDirect also notes that the "Taxable Transaction Summary" in your account "is NOT your 1099." For bills held at a bank or brokerage, TreasuryDirect says to "Talk to them about tax forms and tax withholding."

Publication 550 also says "You must report all your interest income even if you do not receive a Form 1099-INT." The usual $10 reporting threshold decides whether a bank has to send the form, not whether the interest is taxable. (Banks also file the form below $10 in some cases, such as when backup withholding was taken.)

## Rolling a bill over

Many people buy a bill and set it to reinvest automatically at maturity. Each bill still has its own maturity date and its own interest. Publication 550 covers this: "If you reinvest your Treasury bill at its maturity in a new Treasury bill, note, or bond, you will receive payment for the difference between the proceeds of the maturing bill (par amount less any tax withheld) and the purchase price of the new Treasury security. However, you must report the full amount of the interest income on each of your Treasury bills by the time it reaches maturity."

So a 4-week bill rolled over three times between October and January creates interest in the year each bill matures. Rolling over doesn't carry the interest forward into the next bill.

## Selling a bill before it matures

TreasuryDirect says "You can hold a bill until it matures or sell it before it matures," but adds that "To sell a Treasury marketable security, you must work through a bank, broker, or dealer." For a bill bought in TreasuryDirect, "you must hold it in your TreasuryDirect account for 45 days before selling or transferring it," which it says means "you can't sell or transfer a 4-week bill from TreasuryDirect." A sale changes the timing. For short-term obligations, Publication 550 says that, without the election to report currently, "You must treat any gain when you sell, exchange, or redeem the obligation as ordinary income, up to the amount of the ratable share of the discount." A bill sold in December 2026 produces its income in 2026, even if it would have matured in 2027. The sale price depends on market rates at the time; gain above the ordinary-income amount, or a loss if rates have risen, falls under Publication 550's general rule that gain or loss on a discounted debt instrument is "capital gain or loss, except as explained" in its special rules. For bills held at a bank or broker, TreasuryDirect's guidance is to "Talk to them about tax forms and tax withholding."

## Money market funds follow dividend rules

A money market deposit account at a bank pays interest and follows the deposit rules above. A money market fund is different. Publication 550 says: "Money market funds are offered by non-bank financial institutions such as mutual funds and stock brokerage houses, and pay dividends. Generally, amounts you receive from money market funds should be reported as dividends, not as interest."

Dividends have their own year-end rule, and it runs the opposite way from a T-bill. Publication 550 says that if a mutual fund "declares a dividend ... in October, November, or December, payable to shareholders of record on a date in one of those months but actually pays the dividend during January of the next calendar year, the shareholder is considered to have received the dividend on December 31 and must report the dividend in the year it was declared."

So a December dividend from a money market fund that lands in your account in early January 2027 can still be 2026 income, while a T-bill that matures in early January 2027 is 2027 income. The fund's Form 1099-DIV reflects the year it treats the dividend as paid.

## Savings bonds have their own choice

Series EE and Series I savings bonds also grow without paying interest along the way, and they have their own reporting election with different rules from the one for short-term obligations. Publication 550 gives cash-method owners two ways to report:

- "Method 1. Postpone reporting the interest until the earlier of the year you cash or dispose of the bonds or the year in which they mature."
- "Method 2. Choose to report the increase in redemption value as interest each year."

The choice covers all your bonds: "You must use the same method for all Series EE and Series I bonds you own. If you do not choose method 2 by reporting the increase in redemption value as interest each year, you must use method 1." Switching from method 1 to method 2 needs no permission, but "In the year of change, you must report all interest accrued to date and not previously reported for all your bonds." Switching back requires a request to the IRS, which the publication says is automatically granted when a statement meeting its requirements is attached.

Other savings bond rules, including how long a bond must be held before it can be cashed, are covered in [I Bonds vs a High-Yield Savings Account: The 12-Month Lock](/2026/09/24/i-bonds-vs-a-high-yield-savings-account-the-12-month-lock/).

## Why the tax year can matter

For interest that is taxable in any case, timing decides only which year's return includes it. (Separate exclusions, such as the education savings bond exclusion, have their own rules.) A few situations where the year makes a difference, all covered in earlier articles:

- **Income thresholds.** The 3.8% net investment income tax applies only above fixed income lines, which we walked through in [The 3.8% Tax on Savings Interest: Who Owes It and How It Is Figured](/2026/10/15/the-38-tax-on-savings-interest-who-owes-it-and-how-it-is-figured/). Interest moved from one year to another moves between those calculations.
- **Social Security.** IRS [Publication 915](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p915) compares a base amount with "One-half of your benefits; plus All your other income, including tax-exempt interest," so interest counts in the test for taxing benefits, as shown in [Does Savings Interest Make Your Social Security Taxable?](/2026/10/11/does-savings-interest-make-your-social-security-taxable/).
- **Estimated tax.** Interest received in 2026 is part of the 2026 estimated tax picture. The 2026 [Form 1040-ES](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/f1040es.pdf) lists the last calendar-year installment as "Due Jan. 15, 2027," as explained in [Savings Interest and the Jan. 15, 2027 Estimated Tax: The $1,000 Test](/2026/10/07/savings-interest-and-the-jan-15-2027-estimated-tax-the-1000-test/).
- **A change in income.** A household expecting lower income in 2027, such as the first full year of retirement, might find the same interest taxed at a different rate in 2027. A household expecting higher income could find the opposite.

None of this changes the total interest earned. The 13-week bill issued October 15 pays $103.26 on $10,000 in either case. The bill's maturity date decides the year, and a different maturity date is a different investment with its own rate.

## A worked comparison for mid-October

Here is $10,000 of face value in each bill, compared with a $10,000 CD deposit, on October 15, 2026. The bills cost less than $10,000 up front ($9,952.46 for the 6-week bill and $9,896.74 for the 13-week bill), so this is a timing comparison, not a like-for-like return comparison.

| Choice | Interest | When it's received | Reported on |
|---|---|---|---|
| 6-week bill, then held in savings | $47.54 on the bill | Nov. 27, 2026 | 2026 return |
| 13-week bill | $103.26 | Jan. 14, 2027 | 2027 return |
| 6-month CD, all interest at maturity, 4% APY | about $197 | Apr. 15, 2027 | 2027 return |
| 6-month CD, interest paid monthly to savings | Depends on the CD's payment terms | Monthly | Split between 2026 and 2027 |

The 6-week bill earns less interest in total because it's a shorter investment, not because of tax. What happens to the money after November 27 decides how much more it earns in 2026. ## Common questions

**I bought a 13-week bill in October. Will it be on my 2026 1099?**

If it matures in January 2027, no. Publication 550 says you report bill interest "when the bill is paid at maturity," and Treasury interest appears in box 3 of Form 1099-INT. A bill maturing in January 2027 shows up on the 1099 for 2027.

**My CD's interest is credited every month but I didn't withdraw it. Is it taxable this year?**

If you could withdraw it without a substantial penalty, generally yes. Publication 550 says you constructively receive interest "when they are credited to your account and subject to your withdrawal," even if you leave it there.

**Does a 6-month CD create a Form 1099-OID?**

The payer instructions require Form 1099-OID for a CD that "has OID and a term of more than 1 year." For obligations of 1 year or less, they say to "report OID on obligations with a term of 1 year or less on Form 1099-INT."

**Can I choose to report T-bill interest in 2026 even though the bill matures in 2027?**

Publication 550 says that for a short-term obligation you "generally can choose to include any discount and interest payable on the obligation in income currently." That reports the discount as it accrues, so only the part that accrues in 2026 lands on the 2026 return and the rest still lands in 2027. The choice also applies to all short-term obligations acquired that year and later, can't be changed without IRS consent, and increases the obligation's basis by the amount included.

**Is the state tax treatment different depending on the year?**

Not for Treasury bills. Their interest is "exempt from all state and local income taxes" whichever year it's received. Bank CD and savings interest doesn't have that federal exemption from state tax, and how a state taxes it is set by that state's rules.

Two facts set the tax year of savings interest: when the money is paid or credited, and whether you could take it without a substantial penalty. For a cash-method investor holding a bill to maturity without the accrual election, interest generally belongs to the maturity year. For a CD, the payment and withdrawal terms in the account disclosure supply the facts, and the tax rules above decide the year.
