![The Premium Tax Credit Has No Repayment Cap for 2026: How Savings Interest Counts](/assets/pin-images/2026-10-19-the-premium-tax-credit-has-no-repayment-cap-for-2026-how-savings-interest-counts.png)

A 61-year-old who retired early and buys her health coverage through HealthCare.gov, a hypothetical case, estimated her 2026 household income at $52,000 when she signed up, and she has had the full advance premium tax credit paid to her insurer every month since. What she left out of that estimate was about $6,000 of interest from Treasury bills and CDs she bought during the year. On a 2025 return, a single filer in her income range would have repaid at most $1,625 of any overpaid credit. For 2026 there is no such ceiling. Whatever the advance payments exceed the credit she's actually allowed is added to her tax on the 2026 return, and that interest is part of the income the credit is measured against. In her case the shortfall turns out to be smaller than the old limit, but the same rule applies in full to larger gaps and to income that ends up just over 400% of the poverty line, as the examples below show.

The repayment caps were removed by the 2025 tax law, effective for tax years beginning after December 31, 2025. On October 14, 2026, Treasury and the IRS issued proposed regulations to write that change, and the law's other premium tax credit changes, into the rules under section 36B of the tax code. The 2026 figures are also different in a second way: the credit is again limited to households with income of no more than 400% of the federal poverty line, so a few hundred dollars of interest at the top of the range can decide whether any credit is allowed at all. Interest on savings accounts, CDs and Treasury bills counts toward household income, and so does tax-exempt interest.

The rules here come from the IRS news release [IR-2026-121](https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/treasury-and-irs-move-to-protect-health-care-benefits-for-eligible-americans), the [proposed regulations](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/10/15/2026-21056/premium-tax-credit) published in the Federal Register on October 15, 2026 at 91 FR 65494, the IRS fact sheet [FS-2025-10](https://www.irs.gov/pub/taxpros/fs-2025-10.pdf) of questions and answers on the premium tax credit, the [2025 Instructions for Form 8962](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/i8962.pdf), the 2025 [Schedule 1 (Form 1040)](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/f1040s1.pdf), [Rev. Proc. 2025-25](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-drop/rp-25-25.pdf), IRS [Publication 550](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/p550.pdf) and [Publication 969](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/p969.pdf), the IRS [Working Families Tax Cuts](https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/working-families-tax-cuts) page, and HealthCare.gov's pages on [what to include as income](https://www.healthcare.gov/income-and-household-information/income/), the [federal poverty level](https://www.healthcare.gov/glossary/federal-poverty-level-fpl/) and the [premium tax credit](https://www.healthcare.gov/lower-costs/save-on-monthly-premiums/). The example figures, including the plan premium, are made up to show the arithmetic. This is general information about how the rules work, not tax or health insurance advice for any person.

## What the October 14 proposal does

The IRS news release describes the proposal mostly in terms of eligibility. Its summary list includes: "Beginning in 2027, ensures taxpayer-funded health insurance subsidies go only to Americans who qualify under federal law" and "Restricts PTC eligibility to U.S. citizens, U.S. nationals, and certain lawfully present noncitizens."

The repayment change is in the proposal itself. The background section explains that section 71305 of the 2025 law, which the document calls the One, Big, Beautiful Bill Act (OBBBA), "removed the section 36B(f)(2)(B) repayment limitation for taxable years beginning after December 31, 2025," and continues: "Thus, for taxable years beginning after December 31, 2025, a taxpayer's tax liability for the taxable year of the coverage is increased by the full amount of the excess APTC." APTC is the advance premium tax credit, the part of the credit paid to the insurer during the year. Excess APTC is the amount by which those advance payments exceed the credit figured on the tax return.

The proposed regulations don't create that rule; the statute already did. The document notes that some of its provisions would apply from the date of publication, but adds that "the related statutory provisions are effective for tax years and plan years beginning on or after December 31, 2025, and supersede any conflicting provisions in the existing regulations as of that date." The regulations conform the existing rules to a law that already applies to 2026 coverage.

The proposal's economic analysis puts a number on it: "The Treasury Department and the IRS estimate that, in 2028, approximately 3.7 million returns will include a repayment of excess APTC."

The IRS had already described the change in its December 2025 update to the premium tax credit questions and answers. Question 31 there says: "There is no repayment cap for tax years after 2025. For tax years after 2025, you must repay the full amount by which your advance credit payments exceed your Premium Tax Credit." The same answer adds that this amount "is added to your total tax liability, which will reduce your refund or increase your balance due."

## The caps that applied through 2025

For 2025 returns, line 28 of Form 8962 generally limits the repayment for households under 400% of the poverty line, with exceptions in the instructions, such as for advance payments made for individuals who were not lawfully present. The 2025 instructions give these limits in Table 5:

| Household income as % of poverty line (Form 8962, line 5) | Single | Any other filing status |
|---|---|---|
| Less than 200 | $375 | $750 |
| At least 200 but less than 300 | $975 | $1,950 |
| At least 300 but less than 400 | $1,625 | $3,250 |
| 400 or more | Leave line 28 blank (no limit) | Leave line 28 blank (no limit) |

The instructions say: "If your entry on Form 8962, line 5, is 400 or more, there is no repayment limitation. You must repay the amount shown on line 27." For 2026 coverage, every row works like that last one. The full excess is repaid, whatever the household's income.

## The 400% line is back for 2026

The repayment change came from the 2025 law. A separate change for 2026 comes from the end of a temporary rule. The IRS's question 7 describes the general income limit: "In general, individuals and families may be eligible for the Premium Tax Credit if their household income for the year is at least 100 percent but no more than 400 percent of the federal poverty line for their family size." It then describes the exception that ended with 2025: "For tax years 2021 through 2025, Congress temporarily expanded eligibility for the Premium Tax Credit by eliminating the requirement that a taxpayer's household income may not be more than 400 percent of the federal poverty line." The 2025 Form 8962 instructions say the same thing from the other side: "For tax year 2025, taxpayers with household income that exceeds 400% of the federal poverty line for their family size may be allowed a PTC."

The applicable percentage table the IRS published for 2026 in Rev. Proc. 2025-25 shows the share of household income a family is expected to pay toward the benchmark plan, and its last row ends at 400%:

| Household income as % of poverty line | Initial percentage | Final percentage |
|---|---|---|
| Less than 133% | 2.10% | 2.10% |
| At least 133% but less than 150% | 3.14% | 4.19% |
| At least 150% but less than 200% | 4.19% | 6.60% |
| At least 200% but less than 250% | 6.60% | 8.44% |
| At least 250% but less than 300% | 8.44% | 9.96% |
| At least 300% but not more than 400% | 9.96% | 9.96% |

Which poverty guidelines apply is set by timing. Question 7 says: "For purposes of the Premium Tax Credit, eligibility for a certain year is based on the most recently published set of federal poverty guidelines on the first day of the annual open enrollment period. For example, the tax credit for 2025 is based on the 2024 FPL." By the same rule, 2026 coverage uses the 2025 guidelines. HealthCare.gov's poverty level table lists them, and HHS's [computation page for 2025](https://aspe.hhs.gov/topics/poverty-economic-mobility/poverty-guidelines/prior-hhs-poverty-guidelines-federal-register-references/2025-poverty-guidelines-computations) identifies these figures as the guidelines for the 48 contiguous states and the District of Columbia. The IRS fact sheet notes that HHS publishes separate guidelines for Alaska and Hawaii.

| Family size | 2025 poverty guideline | 300% | 400% |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | $15,650 | $46,950 | $62,600 |
| 2 | $21,150 | $63,450 | $84,600 |
| 3 | $26,650 | $79,950 | $106,600 |
| 4 | $32,150 | $96,450 | $128,600 |

On the form, the line is a hard one. Worksheet 2 in the Form 8962 instructions compares household income with four times the poverty guideline, and if it's higher, the instruction is: "The amount on line 1 above is more than 400% of the federal poverty line. Enter 401 here and on line 5 of Form 8962." Income exactly at 400% is within the limit, for a household that meets the other eligibility requirements; one dollar more is not.

## Where savings interest enters household income

The credit is measured against household income, which is built from modified adjusted gross income. Question 8 of the IRS fact sheet defines it: "Modified adjusted gross income is the adjusted gross income on your federal income tax return plus any excluded foreign income, nontaxable Social Security benefits (including tier 1 railroad retirement benefits), and tax-exempt interest received or accrued during the taxable year." Worksheet 1-1 of the Form 8962 instructions builds it line by line: AGI from Form 1040, line 11a, plus tax-exempt interest from line 2a, plus amounts from Form 2555, plus any Social Security benefits that weren't taxable.

HealthCare.gov gives the same instruction for the estimate on the application, under investment income: "Include expected interest and dividends earned on investments, including tax-exempt interest."

For savers, that has several practical effects:

- **Savings and money market account interest** is part of AGI for the year it's credited. Publication 550 says you receive interest on a bank deposit or account "when they are credited to your account and subject to your withdrawal."
- **Treasury bill interest** counts even though states don't tax it. Publication 550 says interest from Treasury bills, notes and bonds "is subject to federal income tax but is exempt from all state and local income taxes." For a bill, "Generally, you report this interest income when the bill is paid at maturity," so a bill that matures in January puts its interest into the new year.
- **CDs of one year or less** generally put their interest into the year you "actually receive it or are entitled to receive it without paying a substantial penalty," in Publication 550's words.
- **CDs longer than one year** put a share of interest into each year, paid out or not. Publication 550: "If you buy a CD with a maturity of more than 1 year, you must include in income each year a part of the total interest due and report it in the same manner as other OID." The details are in [Why You Owe Tax on a 5-Year CD You Cannot Touch Yet](/2026/09/25/why-you-owe-tax-on-a-5-year-cd-you-cannot-touch-yet/).
- **Series EE and I savings bonds** held by cash-method taxpayers, which is most individuals, are reported under Method 1 unless the owner chooses to report the interest every year. Method 1 is to "Postpone reporting the interest until the earlier of the year you cash or dispose of the bonds or the year in which they mature." Cashing bonds held for years under that method generally puts all of the deferred interest into one year's income, unless an exclusion such as the education savings bond exclusion described in Publication 550 applies.
- **Tax-exempt interest** isn't in AGI, but Worksheet 1-1 adds it back from Form 1040, line 2a. Publication 550 says: "Interest you receive on an obligation issued by a state or local government generally is not taxable." For the premium tax credit, that interest still counts.

A dependent's savings count only in some cases. Household income includes, besides your own and your spouse's modified AGI, "the modified AGI of each individual whom you claim as a dependent and who is required to file an income tax return because their income meets the income tax return filing threshold." A dependent who isn't required to file a return adds nothing, even if the dependent's account earns some interest. Parents who report a child's interest on their own return using Form 8814 have a separate rule in the instructions, which for 2025 applies when the amount on Form 8814, line 4, is more than $1,350.

The timing of short-term interest matters here as much as it does for the tax bill itself, since the credit for 2026 is measured only against 2026 income. How T-bill and CD timing works around the turn of the year is laid out in [T-Bills and Short CDs Bought Now: Which Tax Year Gets the Interest](/2026/10/16/t-bills-and-short-cds-bought-now-which-tax-year-gets-the-interest/).

## How the credit responds to more income

The IRS's question 24 gives the basic formula: "The amount of the Premium Tax Credit is generally equal to the premium for the second lowest cost silver plan available through the Marketplace that applies to the members of your coverage family, minus a certain percentage of your household income." The credit also can't be more than the premiums for the plan the family actually enrolls in.

That "certain percentage" is the applicable percentage from the table above. Between 300% and 400% of the poverty line it's a flat 9.96% for 2026, so for someone covered all year, each additional $1,000 of household income in that range raises the expected contribution by $99.60. The credit falls by the same amount as long as some credit remains and the credit is set by the benchmark premium minus that contribution, not by the lower cost of the plan actually chosen. Between 133% and 300%, the percentage itself rises as income rises, so each added dollar lowers the credit by somewhat more than the percentage alone suggests. Below 133% it stays at 2.10%.

When the advance payments were based on a lower estimate, that reduction shows up as excess APTC on the return, and for 2026 all of it is repaid.

## Worked example: $6,000 of interest left out

Back to the 61-year-old from the opening. She's single, lives in one of the 48 contiguous states, and estimated 2026 household income of $52,000. Suppose the benchmark silver plan for her costs $800 a month, or $9,600 a year, and the plan she picked costs at least that much. These are made-up round figures; the forms also use rounding steps not shown here.

**At enrollment.** $52,000 is about 332% of $15,650, in the flat 9.96% range. Her expected contribution is $52,000 × 9.96% = $5,179.20. Her estimated credit is $9,600 − $5,179.20 = $4,420.80 for the year, about $368.40 a month, and she took all of it in advance.

**On the return.** Her actual household income is $58,000, about 370% of the poverty line, because of the interest. Her expected contribution is $58,000 × 9.96% = $5,776.80, and her credit is $9,600 − $5,776.80 = $3,823.20.

**The repayment.** $4,420.80 − $3,823.20 = $597.60 of excess APTC, which is 9.96% of the $6,000. All of it is added to her 2026 tax. On the 2025 form, that repayment amount flows from Form 8962, line 29, to Schedule 2 (Form 1040), line 1a.

At this income level, the old cap wouldn't have made a difference: $597.60 is under the $1,625 limit that applied to single filers between 300% and 400%. The bigger effects come at the 400% line and when income moves up across brackets.

## Crossing 400%: when $400 of interest costs $3,365

Same person, same estimate, same $4,420.80 paid in advance. Now suppose the interest she left out was larger.

- **Actual income $62,600, exactly 400%.** She's still eligible. Expected contribution: $62,600 × 9.96% = $6,234.96. Credit: $9,600 − $6,234.96 = $3,365.04. Repayment: $4,420.80 − $3,365.04 = $1,055.76.
- **Actual income $63,000, $400 more.** Worksheet 2 puts 401 on line 5. No credit is allowed for the year, so all $4,420.80 of advance payments is repaid.

The extra $400 of interest raises her repayment from $1,055.76 to $4,420.80, an increase of $3,365.04. For 2025, the same household would have faced no eligibility cliff, since the credit could be allowed above 400%; the repayment cap, as Table 5 shows, already stopped at 400%. For 2026 the cliff is back, and nothing caps what is repaid.

For a family of four in the 48 contiguous states, the same line sits at $128,600. A household whose estimate is close to that line has little room for interest it didn't count.

## What the old cap would have changed

The caps mattered most when a household's income at tax time was much higher than its estimate at enrollment but still under 400%. Take the same benchmark premium and a different single person:

- **Estimate at enrollment: $39,125, exactly 250%.** Applicable percentage 8.44%. Expected contribution $39,125 × 8.44% = $3,302.15. Advance credit for the year: $9,600 − $3,302.15 = $6,297.85.
- **Actual income: $62,000, about 396%.** Applicable percentage 9.96%. Expected contribution $6,175.20. Credit: $3,424.80.
- **Excess APTC: $6,297.85 − $3,424.80 = $2,873.05.**

Under the 2025 Table 5, a single filer at 300% to 400% would have repaid at most $1,625. Applied to these 2026 figures, only to show the size of the old limit, the cap would have saved $1,248.05. For 2026 the full $2,873.05 is owed.

## What lowers household income on the return

Household income starts from AGI, and AGI is figured after the adjustments on Part II of Schedule 1 (Form 1040). Two of those adjustments relate directly to savers. On the 2025 Schedule 1, line 13 is the "Health savings account deduction" and line 18 is the "Penalty on early withdrawal of savings," where the penalty for breaking a CD or other deferred interest account early is deducted. Both reduce AGI and so reduce modified AGI for the credit. The rules for that deduction are covered in [CD Early Withdrawal Penalties: The Disclosure and the Deduction](/2026/09/22/cd-early-withdrawal-penalties-the-disclosure-and-the-deduction/).

The HSA route is newly open to some Marketplace enrollees. The IRS's Working Families Tax Cuts page says: "Starting Jan. 1, 2026, bronze and catastrophic health insurance plans are treated as HSA-compatible," and that "This applies whether the plans are bought through an insurance exchange or not." Publication 969's limits for 2026: "For 2026, if you have self-only HDHP coverage, you can contribute up to $4,400. If you have family HDHP coverage, you can contribute up to $8,750." Those are the base limits. Publication 969 adds that for an eligible individual "who is age 55 or older at the end of your tax year, your contribution limit is increased by $1,000." The other HSA eligibility rules in the publication still apply. Contributions for a year don't have to be made by December 31; the 2025 edition of Publication 969 says contributions for 2025 could be made through April 15, 2026.

A catastrophic plan, though, doesn't come with a premium tax credit. The proposed regulations' background restates that the term "qualified health plan" for the credit "does not include a catastrophic plan described in section 1302(e) of the Affordable Care Act."

On the application, HealthCare.gov lets enrollees subtract [a short list of expenses](https://www.healthcare.gov/reporting-deductions/) from their income estimate, including "IRA contributions (if you don't have a retirement account through a job)," student loan interest, and HSA deposits "(in limited situations)." It also lists expenses that don't count, among them charitable contributions, medical expenses and mortgage interest.

## Updating the estimate during the year

The advance credit is set from the income estimate, not last year's return. HealthCare.gov puts it this way: "Marketplace savings are based on your expected household income for the year you want coverage, not last year's income."

Its page on [reporting changes](https://www.healthcare.gov/reporting-changes/) describes what happens if income goes up and the estimate isn't changed: "you'll have to pay the difference back between the amount you used and the amount you actually qualify for when you file your federal tax return." The IRS's question 4 lists events that can raise household income significantly during the year, including "Realizing capital gains, including sales of stocks, bonds, or cryptocurrency" and "Lump sum taxable distributions from an individual retirement account or other retirement arrangement."

Enrollees also choose how much of the credit to take in advance. HealthCare.gov says: "If you're worried about having to pay back advance payments of the premium tax credit, you can decide to use less or none of the tax credit toward your premium each month." The change is made by updating the application through "Report a Life Change," where the eligibility results offer "use all, use some, or use none." Taking less in advance doesn't reduce the credit allowed; any credit not taken in advance is claimed on Form 8962 with the return.

For 2027 coverage, the estimate is made this fall. HealthCare.gov's [dates page](https://www.healthcare.gov/quick-guide/dates-and-deadlines/) says: "Starting November 1, you can apply for 2027 coverage or update your application with any changes you expect for the year ahead." For savers, under the reporting rules described earlier, that generally means the interest expected during 2027, including Treasury bills maturing in 2027, the yearly share of interest on CDs longer than a year, and any savings bonds expected to be cashed under Method 1.

## When the repayment comes due

Anyone who had advance payments must reconcile them. The IRS's question 28 says: "If you have APTC in any amount, you must file a Form 8962, and attach it to your federal income tax return for that year." It applies "whether or not you would otherwise be required to file a return," and skipping it has a cost: "If APTC is paid on behalf of you or an individual in your family, and you do not file a tax return, you will not be eligible for APTC to help pay for your Marketplace health insurance coverage in future years."

The numbers for the form come from Form 1095-A, which, according to question 29, the Marketplace sends "by early February." The repayment reduces the refund or adds to the balance due on the 2026 return. If the amount is more than a household can pay at once, question 32 says that "the IRS routinely works with taxpayers who owe amounts they cannot afford to pay," and that payment arrangements work the same way as for other tax balances.

Compared with the interest that caused it, the repayment can be small or large. In the first example, $6,000 of extra interest led to a $597.60 repayment. In the 400% example, $400 of extra interest led to $3,365.04 more in repayment, more than eight times the interest itself.

## Other changes in the proposal

The proposed regulations cover several other provisions of the 2025 law, with different start dates:

- **Income-based special enrollment.** For plan years beginning after December 31, 2025, a plan enrolled in during a special enrollment period offered because of where a person's expected household income falls relative to the poverty line, and not because of an event or change in circumstances specified by the Department of Health and Human Services, doesn't count as a qualified health plan for the credit.
- **Lawfully present immigrants below 100% of the poverty line.** The rule that let a household under 100% qualify when a lawfully present family member was ineligible for Medicaid because of immigration status was removed for tax years beginning after December 31, 2025.
- **Eligible aliens.** The news release says the proposal "Restricts PTC eligibility to U.S. citizens, U.S. nationals, and certain lawfully present noncitizens." The proposal ties the related statutory amendments to tax years beginning after December 31, 2026.
- **Pre-enrollment verification.** For tax years beginning after December 31, 2027, a plan doesn't qualify if the Exchange fails to provide a verification process through which an applicant may, "beginning not later than August 1 of the year prior to the enrollment year," verify household income and eligibility.

Comments on the proposal are due by November 30, 2026. A telephonic public hearing is scheduled for December 8, 2026, at 10 a.m. ET; requests to attend must be received by 5 p.m. ET on December 4, 2026, and the hearing will be canceled if no outlines of topics are received by November 30.

## Dates for 2026 and 2027 coverage

- **November 1, 2026:** HealthCare.gov opens applications for 2027 coverage and updates for the year ahead.
- **November 30, 2026:** deadline for comments on the proposed regulations.
- **December 8, 2026:** scheduled public hearing on the proposal.
- **Early February 2027:** Form 1095-A for 2026 coverage, used to complete Form 8962.
- **With the 2026 return:** reconciliation of 2026 advance payments, with no repayment cap.

## Common questions

**Does interest on a regular savings account count toward household income for the credit?**

Yes. It's part of AGI, and household income starts with AGI. HealthCare.gov's instruction is to "Include expected interest and dividends earned on investments, including tax-exempt interest."

**Does tax-exempt municipal bond interest count?**

Yes. It isn't in AGI, but the modified AGI worksheet adds tax-exempt interest from Form 1040, line 2a, back in.

**Do the balances in my savings or CDs count, or only the interest?**

Only income enters the formula. The credit is figured from household income and the benchmark premium; the account balances themselves aren't part of either.

**Does the repayment cap still apply to 2025 returns?**

Yes. The removal applies to tax years beginning after December 31, 2025. The 2025 Table 5 limits still apply to 2025 coverage reconciled on 2025 returns.

**Is there a cap if my income stays under 400%?**

Not for 2026. Question 31: "For tax years after 2025, you must repay the full amount by which your advance credit payments exceed your Premium Tax Credit."

**Does my teenager's savings account interest count?**

Only if your dependent is required to file a return because their income meets the filing threshold, or if you report the child's interest on your own return with Form 8814 and the instructions' threshold for that form is met.

For 2026 coverage, the credit is measured against household income that includes taxable interest from savings accounts, CDs and Treasury bills plus tax-exempt interest, it stops at 400% of the poverty line, and any amount paid in advance beyond the credit allowed is repaid in full. The proposal issued on October 14 restates that last rule in the regulations; the law already applies it to returns for 2026.
