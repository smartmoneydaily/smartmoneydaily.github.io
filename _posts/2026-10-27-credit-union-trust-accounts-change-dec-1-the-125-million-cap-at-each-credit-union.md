![Credit Union Trust Accounts Change Dec. 1: A $1.25 Million Cap Per Grantor](/assets/pin-images/2026-10-27-credit-union-trust-accounts-change-dec-1-the-125-million-cap-at-each-credit-union.png)

On Oct. 20, the [National Credit Union Administration](https://ncua.gov/consumers/share-insurance-coverage) announced three more proposals under its deregulation project, and on Oct. 21 they were published in the Federal Register. One would change how federally insured credit unions tell members about share insurance. As the NCUA reads the current rule, it requires each credit union to keep physical copies of the NCUA's insurance rules or its brochure, "Your Insured Funds," in its branches. The proposal would keep the duty to give notice but let each credit union choose how, including by linking to the NCUA's website. The NCUA says it "intends to discontinue" the brochure, which it describes as 64 pages long. This is still a proposal; comments are due Dec. 21, 2026.

The timing matters because the coverage those materials describe is about to change under a rule that is already final. On Dec. 1, 2026, the NCUA's rewrite of its trust account rules takes effect. Revocable trusts, including payable-on-death accounts and living trusts, and irrevocable trusts will be insured under one "trust accounts" category with one formula: $250,000 per eligible beneficiary, counting no more than five beneficiaries, for each grantor at each credit union. That caps trust coverage at $1,250,000 per grantor per credit union. The NCUA published the rule in September 2024 with a delayed start. The [FDIC](https://www.fdic.gov/resources/deposit-insurance) made the same change for banks on April 1, 2024.

For most members nothing changes. The NCUA said it expects that "for most accountholders, insurance coverage will not change under the final rule." Its own examples show coverage falling on Dec. 1 in two situations: grantors with both a revocable trust and an irrevocable trust at the same credit union, and trusts naming more than five beneficiaries. Trusts with ineligible beneficiaries or a grantor's retained interest can also come out differently, and some irrevocable trusts gain coverage. The NCUA didn't grandfather existing accounts. It called separate "legacy coverage" for them "unworkable" and said a delay of more than two years gave members time to adjust.

This article is based on the NCUA's [Oct. 20 press release](https://ncua.gov/newsroom/press-release/2026/ncua-announces-thirteenth-round-deregulation-proposals); the proposed rules on [notification to members](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/10/21/2026-21512/notification-to-membersshareholders) (91 FR 66537) and on [conversion to a state charter](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/10/21/2026-21508/conversion-to-state-chartered-credit-union) (91 FR 66534); the final rule on [simplification of share insurance](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2024/09/30/2024-21888/simplification-of-share-insurance-rules) (89 FR 79397) and the NCUA's [April 2025 request for comment](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/04/23/2025-06966/simplification-of-share-insurance-and-succession-planning-final-rules-solicitation-of-comments) on it (90 FR 16999); the current text of [12 CFR 745.13](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/12/745.13) and [12 CFR 740.4](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/12/740.4); the NCUA's [share insurance coverage page](https://ncua.gov/consumers/share-insurance-coverage); its [Sept. 30 release](https://ncua.gov/newsroom/press-release/2026/jackson-area-federal-credit-union-closes-five-star-credit-union-assumes-members-and-shares) on the Jackson Area Federal Credit Union; and the [FDIC](https://www.fdic.gov/resources/deposit-insurance)'s guide to [trust accounts](https://www.fdic.gov/financial-institution-employees-guide-deposit-insurance/trust-accounts). Coverage examples are hypothetical unless they're the NCUA's own, and they depend on the assumptions stated.

## What the NCUA proposed on Oct. 20

The Oct. 20 release lists three proposals, each described as removing rules that are obsolete, "unduly burdensome," or duplicative.

**Member notice of share insurance (12 CFR 745.13).** Today the rule reads, in part: "Each insured credit union shall provide notice to its members concerning NCUA insurance coverage of member accounts. This may be accomplished by placing either a copy of part 745 of these rules, the appendix, or one or more copies of the NCUA brochure 'Your Insured Funds' in each branch office and main office of the credit union. Copies of these materials shall also be made available to members upon request."

The proposal would cut that to one sentence: each credit union "shall provide notice to its members concerning NCUA insurance coverage of member accounts." The NCUA says that "while the regulation lists options and does not make them exclusive, it ultimately does require FICUs to provide physical copies of materials." (A FICU is a federally insured credit union.) Under the proposal, the rule "would not prescribe the timing for providing the required notice," and it "would defer to the FICU on the method used." The NCUA's stated expectation is that notice comes "at a logical point in time that is beneficial to members, which would include upon request by a member," such as at account opening, and that a credit union "will elect a reasonable method that provides members with authoritative information about share insurance coverage." Hard copies, a link to the NCUA website, or a link to the electronic Code of Federal Regulations would all qualify.

The brochure would go away. The NCUA "intends to discontinue" it as part of this rulemaking, without giving a date, and "will undertake an update of its share insurance coverage website to ensure its comprehensiveness, clarity, and ease of navigation." It gave cost as the reason: the agency pays to review, update, print and distribute a brochure "currently 64 pages long," and credit unions must throw out and reprint copies of the rules each time they change.

**Conversion to a state charter (12 CFR 741.7).** This proposal would delete a 1986-era rule saying a federal credit union "that petitions to convert to a state-chartered federally insured credit union is required to apply to the NCUA Regional Director for continued insurance of its accounts." The NCUA says the same process now sits in its Chartering and Field of Membership Manual, where approval moved to its Office of Credit Union Resources and Expansion. The proposal says a federal credit union "wishing to retain federal share insurance after converting to a state charter" must now "submit its request to CURE." For members, the point is that the approval step for keeping federal insurance remains; the proposal removes an outdated duplicate.

**Nonconforming investment reserve (12 CFR 741.3(a)).** The third proposal would drop a special reserve requirement for certain investments held by federally insured state-chartered credit unions. It doesn't touch members' coverage.

## What the notice change doesn't touch

The proposal leaves the official sign alone. A separate rule, 12 CFR 740.4, requires each insured credit union to "continuously display the official sign" at "each station or window where insured account funds or deposits are normally received" in its main office and branches, and to "display the official sign on its Internet page, if any, where it accepts deposits or open accounts." The proposal notes that the notice requirement in 745.13 "supplements" that sign rule. It doesn't change it.

Nor does the proposal change coverage; it is about how members are told, not how much is insured. The standard maximum share insurance amount "is currently set at $250,000," and the NCUA's share insurance page says the fund "is backed by the full faith and credit of the United States."

Members who want the brochure have it for now. The NCUA's share insurance page still links English and Spanish versions. The 2024 trust rule itself promised an updated brochure to reflect the new trust rules. Under the Oct. 21 proposal, the website would take its place instead.

## What changes on Dec. 1

The 2024 rule's effective date reads: "This rule is effective on December 1, 2026, except for the amendments to 12 CFR 745.2(c)(2) (instruction 5), 745.3 (instruction 7), and 745.14 (instruction 13), which are effective October 30, 2024." The early pieces dealt with mortgage servicing accounts and record requirements for pass-through coverage. The trust changes waited.

In April 2025, the NCUA asked for comments again on rules not yet in effect, following a January 2025 White House memorandum on a regulatory freeze. That notice described the trust rule as one "which takes full effect on December 1, 2026." The Oct. 21, 2026 notice proposal repeats the date in a footnote: the appendix to part 745 "will be removed. The final rule will take effect on December 1, 2026."

The rule does three things to trust coverage, in the NCUA's words:

1. "merge the revocable and irrevocable trust categories into one category"
2. "apply a simpler, common calculation method to determine insurance coverage for funds held by revocable and irrevocable trusts"
3. "eliminate certain requirements found in the current rules for revocable and irrevocable trusts"

The new trust category covers three kinds of accounts:

- **Informal revocable trusts.** Accounts where funds "pass directly to one or more beneficiaries upon the owner's death without a written trust agreement, commonly referred to as a payable-on-death account, in-trust-for account, or Totten trust account."
- **Formal revocable trusts.** A revocable trust "established by a written trust agreement," usually called a living trust or family trust.
- **Irrevocable trusts.** A trust "established by statute or a written trust agreement" that can't be revoked.

Each grantor's funds across all three types at the same credit union are added together. The new 12 CFR 745.4(b)(2) says trust funds that pass from the same grantor to beneficiaries "are aggregated for the purposes of determining coverage under this section, regardless of whether those funds are held in connection with an informal revocable trust, formal revocable trust, or irrevocable trust."

## The formula

The rule's core sentence: "Deposited trust funds are insured in an amount up to the SMSIA multiplied by the total number of beneficiaries identified by each grantor, up to a maximum of five beneficiaries." The SMSIA, the standard maximum share insurance amount, is $250,000.

So for one grantor at one credit union:

| Eligible beneficiaries | Maximum trust coverage |
|---|---|
| 1 | $250,000 |
| 2 | $500,000 |
| 3 | $750,000 |
| 4 | $1,000,000 |
| 5 or more | $1,250,000 |

Several details decide who counts.

- **Eligible beneficiaries** are "natural persons, as well as charitable organizations and other non-profit entities recognized as such under the Internal Revenue Code of 1986." The definition doesn't require a family relationship.
- **The grantor doesn't count**, and neither does a backup beneficiary, anyone who "would only obtain an interest in the deposited funds if one or more named beneficiaries are deceased." The NCUA says naming a chain of such beneficiaries "will not increase share insurance coverage." This is different from a current beneficiary whose share depends on a trustee's discretion; that person still counts.
- **Only beneficiaries of the deposits count.** The NCUA "will not consider beneficiaries who are expected to receive only non-deposit assets of the trust," such as someone who inherits only the house.
- **Allocations don't matter.** The NCUA "will presume that, for share insurance purposes, the trust provides for equal treatment of beneficiaries." A trust leaving 90% to one child and 10% to another counts as two beneficiaries, the same as an even split.
- **Two grantors, two limits.** Coverage is calculated for each grantor and applied to that grantor's own share, as long as the membership rules described below are met. In the NCUA's footnote example, "two co-grantors that designate five beneficiaries are insured for up to $2,500,000 (2 x 5 x $250,000)." Unless the credit union's records say otherwise, a trust with several grantors is "presumed to have been owned or funded by the grantors in equal shares."
- **Future trusts look through.** If a trust says the money passes into new trusts at death, the people or organizations that receive the money through those trusts are the beneficiaries.
- **Payable-on-death beneficiaries must be on file.** "The beneficiaries of an informal revocable trust must be specifically named in the account records of the federally insured credit union."
- **No eligible beneficiary at all** means the funds are treated as the grantor's single-ownership funds, added to the grantor's other individual accounts and insured up to $250,000.
- **Co-owners who are the only beneficiaries** of their own revocable trust are insured under the joint ownership rules instead.

Trust coverage is separate from the other categories. The rule says the trust coverage "is separate from coverage provided for other funds at the same federally insured credit union." A member's individual accounts, the member's share of joint accounts, and IRAs each have their own $250,000 limit on top of the trust limit.

## Who could lose coverage

The NCUA's own examples point to two groups.

**A revocable and an irrevocable trust for the same people.** In the NCUA's fourth example, a member has a living trust naming three beneficiaries and has also set up an irrevocable trust for the same three, with both accounts at one credit union. After Dec. 1, the two are added together and insured up to $250,000 × 1 grantor × 3 beneficiaries = $750,000. Under the current rules, the NCUA said the member "would be insured for up to $750,000 for revocable trust shares and separately insured for up to $750,000 for irrevocable trust shares (assuming non-contingent beneficial interests), resulting in $1,500,000 in total coverage." Even with contingencies in the irrevocable trust, current coverage would be $1,000,000. The NCUA called this "one of the few instances where the final rule may provide a reduced amount of coverage."

**More than five beneficiaries.** In the fifth example, a living trust names seven beneficiaries. After Dec. 1 the count stops at five, so coverage is $1,250,000. Under the current rule, a trust with more than five beneficiaries is insured for the greater of five times $250,000 or the sum of each beneficiary's interest, capped at $250,000 each, which the NCUA said produces "a range from $1,250,000 to $1,750,000, depending on the precise allocation of trust interests among the beneficiaries." If all seven shares were equal, current coverage would be 7 × $250,000 = $1,750,000.

For payable-on-death accounts and simple living trusts with five or fewer beneficiaries, the result doesn't change. The NCUA's first three examples, covering a POD account with three beneficiaries, a couple's POD account plus a living trust naming the same three people, and a mix of joint and individual POD accounts, all end with the same coverage as today.

## Worked examples

These are hypothetical households, each at a single federally insured credit union. The figures assume every beneficiary is eligible and entitled to the deposits, every grantor meets the membership rules, joint accounts are owned 50-50, and every account title and beneficiary record meets the NCUA's requirements.

**A widow with a living trust and an irrevocable trust.** She is the grantor of a living trust naming her two children, holding $400,000 in share certificates (the credit union version of CDs), and an irrevocable trust for the same two children holding $200,000. She also has $100,000 in a regular savings account with no beneficiaries.

- Trust funds after Dec. 1: $400,000 + $200,000 = $600,000
- Trust limit: $250,000 × 1 grantor × 2 beneficiaries = $500,000
- Uninsured trust funds: $100,000
- Savings account: $100,000, insured in the single ownership category

Today the living trust has its own $500,000 limit, and the irrevocable trust is insured separately; even at the $250,000 the NCUA says contingencies often produce, its $200,000 is covered. On Dec. 1, $100,000 of her $700,000 would be over the limit at this credit union.

**A trust with seven grandchildren.** A grandfather's living trust holds $1,600,000 at one credit union and names seven grandchildren in equal shares.

- Today: 7 × $250,000 = $1,750,000, so the full $1,600,000 is insured
- After Dec. 1: five-beneficiary cap, so $1,250,000
- Uninsured after Dec. 1: $1,600,000 − $1,250,000 = $350,000

**A couple with three children.** Spouses hold a joint payable-on-death account naming their three children, with $1,200,000 in it.

- Limit: $250,000 × 2 grantors × 3 beneficiaries = $1,500,000
- Fully insured, today and after Dec. 1

The 50-50 assumption matters. If the account records showed a 90-10 split instead, the larger owner's $1,080,000 share would be over that owner's $750,000 limit by $330,000, even though the other owner's limit had room to spare.

**The same couple, adding individual POD accounts.** Suppose each spouse also has an individual POD account naming the same three children, with $300,000 each. Coverage is figured for each grantor, using half of the joint account.

- Each spouse's trust funds: $600,000 (half of the joint account) + $300,000 = $900,000
- Each spouse's limit: $250,000 × 3 = $750,000
- Uninsured: $150,000 per spouse, $300,000 in all

That last result isn't new. The NCUA's third example combines a joint POD account with one spouse's individual POD account in the same way and says the method and coverage are "the same as that provided by the current share insurance rules." Naming different people on the individual accounts would change the count. In the NCUA's own illustration, a grantor with one account naming three beneficiaries and another naming "three separate and unique beneficiaries" has six unique beneficiaries, capped at five, for $1.25 million of coverage; if the same three people were named on both, the maximum would be $750,000.

## Some irrevocable trusts gain coverage

The change doesn't only cut. Under the current rules, an irrevocable trust's coverage depends on whether each beneficiary's interest is "contingent or non-contingent." Money held for non-contingent interests is insured "up to the SMSIA for each such beneficiary," meaning up to $250,000 each, but money held for contingent interests is "aggregated and insured up to the SMSIA in total." The NCUA's example of a contingency: "where a trustee has discretion to divert funds from one beneficiary to another to provide for the second beneficiary's medical needs, the first beneficiary's interest is contingent upon the trustee's discretion." A grantor's own retained interest in an irrevocable trust is added to the grantor's single-ownership accounts.

After Dec. 1, contingencies drop out of the calculation. The NCUA says coverage "will no longer be affected by the specific allocation of trust funds to each of the beneficiaries of the trust or by contingencies outlined in the trust agreement."

A hypothetical: a grandmother's irrevocable trust for her three grandchildren holds $600,000 at one credit union, and the trustee has discretion to shift money among them for medical needs. She has no other trust accounts there.

- Today, if the interests are contingent: insured up to $250,000 in total, leaving $350,000 uninsured
- After Dec. 1: $250,000 × 1 grantor × 3 beneficiaries = $750,000, so the full $600,000 is insured

What lifts this account's limit is the end of the contingency test. The same rule would also add any revocable trust she holds at that credit union to the total, so anyone with both kinds of trust should run the numbers on the combined balance.

## Membership, death and other rules that stay

Some rules carry over from today and matter as much as the formula.

**Membership.** Share insurance covers members and those otherwise entitled to keep insured accounts. The 2024 rule restates the requirement. For a revocable trust account, all grantors must be members or otherwise eligible; for an irrevocable trust account, "all grantors or all beneficiaries must be members of the FICU or otherwise eligible to maintain an insured account to receive share insurance coverage." Where a revocable trust becomes irrevocable at a grantor's death, the deceased grantor's membership "will continue to satisfy their membership requirement as long as the trust account continues to be maintained at the FICU."

**Death of an owner.** The NCUA insures a deceased owner's accounts "as if he/she/they were still alive for a period of 6 months after his/her/their death." Under 12 CFR 745.2(e), that holds "unless the member's share accounts are restructured in that time period"; after a restructuring, or after the six months, coverage follows actual ownership, and the grace period "will not result in a reduction of coverage." The new rule removes a current provision that let some revocable trusts that became irrevocable keep revocable-trust treatment, and the NCUA says that under the new rule "the death of a formal revocable trust owner will not result in a decrease in share insurance coverage for the trust." For a POD account with a co-owner who dies, coverage for the deceased owner's interest still ends after the six months, and the account is then insured "as if the deceased co-owner did not exist."

**Account titles.** A formal trust account's title must include terms "such as 'family trust' or 'living trust,'" or the trust must otherwise be identified in the credit union's records.

## What happens to trust accounts in a liquidation

The NCUA's description of the current process explains why it wanted a simpler formula. Beneficiaries of payable-on-death accounts must be named in the credit union's records. For formal revocable trusts, which have no such requirement under the current rules, "the NCUA generally obtains information on beneficiaries of such trusts from accountholders following a FICU's liquidation." The NCUA also wrote: "If a member's funds at a liquidated FICU held in trust accounts exceed the SMSIA, a hold will be placed on the portion of such funds in excess of the SMSIA until the NCUA can fully review the member's trust agreement and related documents to verify the beneficiary rules are satisfied." The NCUA said that process "can result in delays to some insured accountholders' insurance determinations and full insurance payments."

The new rule doesn't remove the need to identify beneficiaries. The NCUA says accountholders "will still need to identify the grantors and the eligible beneficiaries of the trust." What goes away is the need to value each beneficiary's share or test the trust's contingencies, which is the part that took time.

## Federally insured or not

All of this applies only at federally insured credit unions. The NCUA says its fund covers accounts "in all FCUs and the majority of state-chartered credit unions," which means some state-chartered credit unions aren't federally insured. The NCUA's share insurance page says: "There are several state-chartered credit unions that are insured by private insurers." That private coverage, it adds, isn't backed by the full faith and credit of the United States. The sign rule generally bars a federally insured credit union from taking deposits at a teller station where a non-federally insured one also does, but it has exceptions for shared branches and third-party facilities, which must post an added notice telling members to check their own credit union's status. The same NCUA page suggests confirming a credit union's status with the agency's Credit Union Locator tool.

## How this compares with banks

The NCUA said the rule "will also provide parity between the NCUA's regulations and those adopted by the FDIC in early 2022," and that its delay "mirrors the timeline the FDIC used." The FDIC's version took effect April 1, 2024. Its guide for bank employees states the same formula: an owner's trust deposits "are insured for up to $250,000 per eligible beneficiary, up to a maximum of $1,250,000 if five or more eligible beneficiaries are named."

The limits are per institution. The NCUA describes its cap as "$1,250,000 per grantor, per FICU," and the FDIC applies its cap per bank, so a grantor with trust accounts at a credit union and at a bank has a separate maximum at each.

## Checking an account before Dec. 1

The arithmetic is short enough to do by hand for each credit union where you keep trust accounts.

1. **List every trust-category account by grantor.** Include POD and in-trust-for accounts, living trust accounts, and irrevocable trust accounts. Share certificates count as much as savings and checking.
2. **Count unique eligible beneficiaries for each grantor** across all those accounts: people, charities and other IRS-recognized nonprofits who are entitled to the deposits. Leave out the grantor, backup beneficiaries who take only if a named beneficiary dies, and anyone who gets only non-deposit assets. Stop at five. Set aside accounts that fall under other rules: a revocable trust whose co-owners are its only beneficiaries is insured as a joint account, a trust with no eligible beneficiary as single-ownership funds, and a POD account payable to the owner's own formal trust is treated as if titled in that trust.
3. **Multiply by $250,000.** That's the grantor's trust limit at that credit union.
4. **Add the grantor's trust-category balances.** For joint POD accounts or trusts with two grantors, use each grantor's share, equal unless the records say otherwise.
5. **Compare.** Anything over the limit is uninsured at that institution after Dec. 1.
6. **Confirm the records.** Make sure POD beneficiaries are named in the credit union's account records and trust accounts are titled as trusts.

The NCUA's [Share Insurance Estimator](https://mycreditunion.gov/share-insurance-estimator-home) is another option. The 2024 rule said the agency "must update its share insurance estimator and share insurance coverage publications" for the change, so check whether a given version reflects the Dec. 1 rules before relying on it. The NCUA's consumer line is 1.800.755.1030, option 1, and its page lists email at DCAmail@ncua.gov.

Moving money around to fix an excess has its own costs. Share certificates can carry early withdrawal penalties, and changing a trust or its beneficiaries is a legal decision with consequences beyond insurance. Those choices belong with the member and, where a trust is involved, the person who drafted it.

## Why this comes up now

Credit unions do fail. On Sept. 30, the NCUA said Jackson Area Federal Credit Union in Jackson, Miss., which it had placed in conservatorship on May 6, was liquidated, and Five Star Credit Union of Dothan, Ala., assumed its share accounts. The release said all accounts "remain insured by the National Credit Union Share Insurance Fund" and repeated the fund's basic limits. A liquidation after Dec. 1 would be handled under the new trust rules, since the NCUA chose not to keep the old ones for existing accounts.

The NCUA also gave its reasons for the change in terms of failures. It said the simpler calculation "has proven beneficial in liquidations, as it leads to more prompt share insurance determinations and quicker access to insured funds for accountholders." Under the current rules, irrevocable trusts and trusts with more than five beneficiaries can require reading the trust agreement to value each beneficiary's interest. After Dec. 1, once the grantors, eligible beneficiaries, membership and records are established, coverage turns on the number of beneficiaries rather than the value of each one's interest.

## Common questions

**Is my credit union still required to tell me about share insurance?**

Yes. Both the current rule and the proposal require notice. The proposal changes the method, not the duty, and would let a credit union point members to the NCUA's website instead of the 64-page brochure, and the official sign at teller stations and on the credit union's deposit-taking web pages is required under a separate rule.

**Does the $1,250,000 cap apply to all my accounts at a credit union?**

No. It applies to a grantor's trust-category funds. Individual accounts, joint accounts and IRAs are insured in their own categories. In the NCUA's first example, a member with a POD account naming three beneficiaries and a share draft account (a credit union checking account) with no beneficiaries is insured for $750,000 + $250,000 = $1,000,000.

**My POD account names my two children and my grandchildren as backups. How many beneficiaries count?**

Two, assuming both children are living and the grandchildren receive money only if a child has died. Beneficiaries who "would only obtain an interest in the deposited funds if one or more named beneficiaries are deceased" are left out of the count.

**What if my trust names more than five people?**

Coverage stops at $1,250,000 per grantor at that credit union. The trust can name as many beneficiaries as its terms provide; the extra names just don't add insurance.

**Will the NCUA keep the old rules for accounts opened before Dec. 1?**

No. The NCUA considered "legacy coverage" for existing accounts and rejected it, saying that two sets of rules would create "significantly greater complexity" in liquidations.

For a member whose credit union trust accounts are payable-on-death accounts or simple living trusts naming five or fewer eligible beneficiaries, with no irrevocable trust at the same credit union, the NCUA's examples show no change on Dec. 1. Anyone with an irrevocable trust, more than five beneficiaries, an ineligible beneficiary or a retained interest should run the six steps before the date, with help from whoever drafted the trust where its terms aren't clear.
