If you have high-deductible coverage and are deciding what to put into a health savings account next year, the numbers are now set: for 2027 the IRS caps HSA contributions at $4,500 for self-only coverage and $9,000 for family coverage, up from $4,400 and $8,750 for 2026. The 2027 figures come from [Rev. Proc. 2026-24](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-drop/rp-26-24.pdf), which also sets the 2027 deductible floor and out-of-pocket ceiling a plan has to meet to count as a high deductible health plan. An eligible individual who is 55 or older at the end of the tax year can add $1,000 on top.

The limits are half of the picture. The other half is how the money is treated once it is inside the account. An HSA balance can sit as plain cash, and that cash is treated differently from an ordinary savings balance: its interest is tax free under IRS [Publication 969](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p969), and before 65 the part of a withdrawal not used for qualified medical expenses generally carries an additional 20% tax. And when the cash is on deposit at an insured bank, the [FDIC](https://www.fdic.gov/resources/deposit-insurance) insures it either as the owner's single account or, if beneficiaries are named, under the trust category, which can change how much of a household's money at that bank is covered.

The short version: plan on $4,500 or $9,000 for 2027, plus $1,000 at 55 or older; check that your plan qualifies, including under the newer rules for bronze and catastrophic Exchange plans; and look at the beneficiary line on the HSA, because whether beneficiaries are named there is what the FDIC's category for the cash turns on.

## What Rev. Proc. 2026-24 sets for 2027

Each year the IRS publishes the inflation-adjusted HSA figures in a revenue procedure, and for calendar year 2027 that document is Rev. Proc. 2026-24. The document says it "is effective for HSAs for calendar year 2027," and it gives the two numbers most savers look for in plain terms.

For self-only coverage: "For calendar year 2027, the annual limitation on deductions under section 223(b)(2)(A) for an individual with self-only coverage under a high deductible health plan is $4,500."

For family coverage: "For calendar year 2027, the annual limitation on deductions under § 223(b)(2)(B) for an individual with family coverage under a high deductible health plan is $9,000."

The limit is framed as a limitation on deductions, which says how the account works on the way in: an eligible individual's contributions are deductible up to the cap.

For comparison, Publication 969 gives the current-year limits: "For 2026, if you have self-only HDHP coverage, you can contribute up to $4,400. If you have family HDHP coverage, you can contribute up to $8,750." The step up for 2027 is therefore $100 on the self-only side and $250 on the family side.

The age-based addition sits in Publication 969 as well: "If you are an eligible individual who is age 55 or older at the end of your tax year, your contribution limit is increased by $1,000." The test is your age at the end of the tax year, not on the day you contribute. Under that wording, someone who turns 55 in December qualifies for the higher limit for that year even when the contributions go in during the spring.

| Figure | 2026 | 2027 | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Contribution limit, self-only coverage | $4,400 | $4,500 | Publication 969; Rev. Proc. 2026-24 |
| Contribution limit, family coverage | $8,750 | $9,000 | Publication 969; Rev. Proc. 2026-24 |
| Additional amount, age 55 or older at year end | $1,000 | $1,000 | Publication 969 |
| Self-only total at 55 or older | $5,400 | $5,500 | Sum of the rows above |
| Family total, one account holder 55 or older | $9,750 | $10,000 | Sum of the rows above |

The last two rows are simple addition. The 2027 column assumes the $1,000 increase described in Publication 969 applies to 2027 as it does to 2026; the IRS's own later publications are where to confirm it once they are issued.

## What counts as a high deductible health plan in 2027

The contribution limit only matters if you are eligible to contribute, and eligibility starts with the health plan. Rev. Proc. 2026-24 sets the 2027 definition in one sentence: "For calendar year 2027, a 'high deductible health plan' is defined under section 223(c)(2)(A) as a health plan with an annual deductible that is not less than $1,750 for self-only coverage or $3,500 for family coverage, and for which the annual out-of-pocket expenses (deductibles, co-payments, and other amounts, but not premiums) do not exceed $8,700 for self-only coverage or $17,400 for family coverage."

Two tests, then, and a plan has to pass both. The deductible has to be at least the floor. The out-of-pocket maximum has to be no higher than the ceiling. Premiums are expressly left out of the out-of-pocket figure, so a plan with high premiums and a modest out-of-pocket maximum can still qualify.

| 2027 HDHP test | Self-only coverage | Family coverage |
|---|---|---|
| Minimum annual deductible | $1,750 | $3,500 |
| Maximum annual out-of-pocket expenses (not premiums) | $8,700 | $17,400 |
| HSA contribution limit | $4,500 | $9,000 |

The plan summary lists a deductible and an out-of-pocket maximum; for a 2027 plan year, compare them with this table. A plan whose self-only deductible is below $1,750, or whose self-only out-of-pocket maximum is above $8,700, does not meet the general definition. An HSA-eligible label is a useful shortcut, but under the general definition those two numbers are what gets checked.

## Bronze, catastrophic, direct primary care and telehealth

The general definition is no longer the only route to eligibility. The law the IRS refers to as the One, Big, Beautiful Bill added several changes, and the IRS summarized them in [IR-2025-119](https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/treasury-irs-provide-guidance-on-new-tax-benefits-for-health-savings-account-participants-under-the-one-big-beautiful-bill) on Dec. 9, 2025.

The first matters most to people who buy their own coverage: "As of Jan. 1, 2026, bronze and catastrophic plans available through an Exchange are considered HSA-compatible, regardless of whether the plans satisfy the general definition of an HDHP." The last clause is the point: such a plan does not have to meet the deductible floor or out-of-pocket ceiling in the table above. The IRS phrase is "available through an Exchange," so that availability is the fact to confirm.

The second change concerns direct primary care. From the same release: "Beginning Jan. 1, 2026, an otherwise eligible individual enrolled in certain direct primary care (DPC) service arrangements may contribute to an HSA. In addition, they may use their HSA funds tax-free to pay periodic DPC fees." Rev. Proc. 2026-24 sets the 2027 fee threshold for these arrangements: "For calendar year 2027, a DPCSA is not treated as a health plan with respect to an otherwise eligible individual if the aggregate monthly fees for all DPCSAs with respect to the individual do not exceed $150 or, if the individual is covered by a DPCSA that covers more than one individual, $300."

The threshold is an aggregate: all of an individual's direct primary care arrangements count together, and the combined monthly fees have to stay at or under $150, or $300 where the arrangement covers more than one person. For an otherwise eligible individual, an arrangement within the threshold is not treated as a health plan, which keeps it from interfering with HSA eligibility. The fee cap is one condition; the IRS release speaks of "certain" arrangements, so the membership itself also has to be the kind the rules cover before the fees can be paid from the HSA tax-free.

The third change is telehealth. The release states that the law "made permanent the ability to receive telehealth and other remote care services before meeting the high-deductible health plan (HDHP) deductible while remaining eligible to contribute to an HSA, effective for plan years beginning on or after Jan. 1, 2025." A plan can cover remote care from the first dollar without costing the enrollee HSA eligibility.

The same revenue procedure also sets $2,250 as the maximum amount that may be made newly available for the plan year for an excepted benefit health reimbursement arrangement, for plan years beginning in 2027, a figure that matters only to people offered one.

## Room for 2027, worked through

The figures below are invented to show the arithmetic. Only the limits come from the IRS.

Priya is 38 and has self-only coverage under a plan that meets the 2027 definition for the whole year. Her 2027 limit is $4,500. If she puts $375 a month into the account, she reaches $4,500 in December. Her direct primary care membership costs $90 a month, which is under the $150 aggregate threshold for an arrangement covering one person. Assuming the membership is a qualifying arrangement, it does not affect her eligibility, and she can pay the $90 from the HSA without tax.

Marcus has family coverage and turns 56 in 2027. The family limit is $9,000, and, assuming the $1,000 figure carries into 2027 as noted above, Publication 969's increase applies to him as an eligible individual who is 55 or older at the end of the tax year. How the family limit is divided between spouses is set out in Publication 969, and a couple should read that section before splitting contributions.

Dana buys a bronze plan through the Exchange for 2027 with a deductible below the $1,750 floor. Under IR-2025-119 the plan still counts as HSA-compatible, so assuming she is otherwise eligible for the year, her self-only limit is $4,500.

The deadline side is simpler. Publication 969 states: "You can make contributions to your HSA for 2025 through April 15, 2026." Contributions for a year do not all have to be made during that calendar year. For 2027 contributions, the IRS will state the corresponding date in its guidance for that year.

## The HSA as a cash account

HSAs are usually discussed as investment accounts or as a way to pay the pharmacy. For a saver comparing savings accounts and CDs, a third view is more useful: an HSA is a tax-advantaged cash account with a narrow door.

Publication 969 states the part that matters most for cash: "The interest or other earnings on the assets in the account are tax free." Interest earned on the cash in an HSA is not taxed while it sits there. That is a different world from a regular savings account, where interest is taxable as it is earned, a cost that the [SmartMoneyDaily look at the tax bite on savings interest](https://smartmoneydaily.github.io/2026/08/26/the-tax-bite-on-savings-interest-what-your-real-yield-is/) walks through.

A hypothetical shows the shape of it. Assume an HSA holds $9,000 in cash at a round 4% yield, a number chosen only for the arithmetic. The account earns about $360 in a year. Inside the HSA, Publication 969 says those earnings are tax free. The same $360 earned in an ordinary savings account would go on the saver's tax return. The yield on the page is the same; what the saver keeps is not.

The trade-off is how money gets out. Money in a savings account can be spent on anything; money in an HSA carries a condition enforced with a tax. That condition makes an HSA an awkward fit for an emergency fund meant for rent or a car repair, and a natural fit for money headed to medical costs anyway.

There is a second cash question: what the trustee does with the cash. Some trustees keep the balance on deposit at a bank; others offer investment options alongside a cash portion. The rate paid on HSA cash depends on the trustee, and a saver who leaves a large balance in cash faces the same two questions as with any account: is it earning what it could, and is it protected the way the saver assumes. The sections below take up the second.

## The 20% line before 65

The condition on HSA money is stated in Publication 969: "There is an additional 20% tax on the part of your distributions not used for qualified medical expenses."

The tax applies to the part of a distribution not used for qualified medical expenses, not to the whole withdrawal. And the IRS calls it an additional tax: it is not the only tax consequence of a non-medical withdrawal in Publication 969, whose distributions section covers the rest.

Invented numbers again. Suppose a saver who is 45 takes $2,000 out of an HSA, spends $1,200 on qualified medical expenses and $800 on something else. The additional tax applies to the $800. At 20%, that is $160, before any other tax the $800 may carry.

Publication 969 then names when the additional tax does not apply: "There is no additional tax on distributions made after the date you are disabled, reach age 65, or die." At 65 the 20% layer comes off. That removes the additional tax only; it does not say a non-medical withdrawal after 65 carries no tax at all, so a saver planning that should read the full distribution rules.

For a cash saver the lesson is placement. Money that might be needed for non-medical costs before 65 may sit more comfortably in an ordinary savings account, where the interest is taxable but spending it carries no condition like the HSA's. Money earmarked for medical costs is where the HSA's tax-free interest earns its keep.

## Where HSA cash sits, and who insures it

The FDIC addresses HSAs directly in its [Financial Institution Employee's Guide to Deposit Insurance](https://www.fdic.gov/financial-institution-employees-guide-deposit-insurance/health-savings-accounts). It starts with a definition: "A Health Savings Account (“HSA”) is a tax-exempt trust or custodial account established with a qualified HSA trustee, such as an IDI, to pay or reimburse certain medical expenses." IDI is the FDIC's abbreviation for an insured depository institution, in plain terms an FDIC-insured bank.

Two facts follow. An HSA is a trust or custodial account, which is why the FDIC has to say which ownership category it belongs to. And a bank is one kind of HSA trustee, not the only kind. FDIC deposit insurance covers deposits at insured banks. When the balance has been moved into investments, or the trustee is not a bank, what protects it is a different question, answered in the provider's account agreement.

If the provider is a company rather than a bank, ask where the cash is actually deposited. The general problem of an app that says "FDIC insured" while the money sits at a partner bank is laid out in [When "FDIC Insured" Means a Partner Bank, Not Your App](https://smartmoneydaily.github.io/2026/09/08/when-fdic-insured-means-a-partner-bank-not-your-app/), and the same questions apply to HSA cash: which bank holds it, and in whose name.

Once the cash is at an insured bank, the FDIC applies one of two categories, and which one depends on a single fact.

## Beneficiaries change the FDIC category

The FDIC guide gives the rule in two sentences.

With beneficiaries: "If valid testamentary language exists with one or more beneficiaries named, then the FDIC will insure the deposit under the Trust Accounts category."

Without: "If an owner of an HSA has not designated beneficiaries, then the FDIC will insure the HSA as the single account of the owner."

The difference is larger than it looks. The single account category holds a person's ordinary accounts in their own name. An HSA with no named beneficiary is added to those accounts at the same bank, and the total is insured up to the standard $250,000, one cap shared with every savings account and CD the owner holds there in their own name.

The trust category is separate from the single account category, and its coverage is calculated per beneficiary. The FDIC's own worked example on the HSA page reads: "1 owner x 3 beneficiaries x $250,000 = $750,000." One owner naming three beneficiaries reaches a potential $750,000 of trust-category coverage, calculated separately from whatever that owner holds in single accounts. An HSA balance in the trust category no longer competes with the owner's personal savings for the same $250,000.

The trust category has rules of its own, and an HSA is not the only kind of account the FDIC places in it. How the category works after the FDIC's 2024 changes, including for payable-on-death and revocable trust accounts, is covered in [Trust Account Coverage After the 2024 FDIC Rule Change](https://smartmoneydaily.github.io/2026/09/06/trust-account-coverage-after-the-2024-fdic-rule-change/). A saver who already uses payable-on-death designations at the same bank should read that before assuming the HSA adds fresh coverage on top.

The key phrase in the FDIC's first sentence is "valid testamentary language." The FDIC guide does not spell out the paperwork, so the practical question for the HSA trustee is whether a beneficiary designation is on file for the account. A form that was never completed names no one, and without designated beneficiaries the FDIC insures the HSA as the owner's single account.

How the FDIC's ownership categories stack at a single bank, and why each category gets its own cap, is set out in [How FDIC Ownership Categories Stack Coverage at One Bank](https://smartmoneydaily.github.io/2026/08/21/how-fdic-ownership-categories-stack-coverage-at-one-bank/).

## Three savers at one bank

Every balance below is invented. Only the rules come from the FDIC guide. Assume each saver has no other accounts at the bank than the ones listed, and that each HSA's cash is on deposit at that same insured bank.

Ruth has $235,000 in savings and CDs in her own name at a bank, and her HSA trustee is the same bank, holding $30,000 of HSA cash. She has never named a beneficiary on the HSA. Under the FDIC guide, the HSA is insured "as the single account of the owner." Her single accounts total $265,000. With a $250,000 cap, $15,000 is uninsured.

Ben has the same balances at the same bank, but when he opened the HSA he named his two adult children as beneficiaries, and the designation is on file with valid testamentary language. The FDIC insures his HSA under the trust accounts category. His $235,000 of personal savings sits alone in the single account category and is fully insured. His $30,000 HSA is insured in the trust category, where two beneficiaries give a potential $500,000 of coverage by the FDIC's per-beneficiary arithmetic. Nothing is uninsured.

Carla has $60,000 in her own savings and $12,000 in HSA cash at one bank, with no beneficiary on the HSA. Her single accounts total $72,000, well under $250,000, so the beneficiary question does not change her insurance today.

| Saver | Personal single accounts | HSA cash | Beneficiary named on HSA | FDIC category for HSA | Uninsured |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ruth | $235,000 | $30,000 | No | Single account of the owner | $15,000 |
| Ben | $235,000 | $30,000 | Yes, two | Trust accounts | $0 |
| Carla | $60,000 | $12,000 | No | Single account of the owner | $0 |

The difference between Ruth and Ben is one form. The balances and the bank are identical, and $15,000 of exposure disappears because a beneficiary designation exists. For a saver whose combined balance at one bank stays well under $250,000, the category makes no difference today; the case where it matters is a saver who keeps most of their cash at one institution and chose that institution's HSA for convenience.

## What to check during open enrollment

Fall open enrollment is when many people choose the plan they will have for 2027. The HSA decisions that come with it fit on a short list.

Confirm the plan qualifies, using the deductible and out-of-pocket figures in the 2027 table above; a bronze or catastrophic plan available through an Exchange is HSA-compatible regardless of those figures.

Know the ceiling: $4,500 for self-only coverage or $9,000 for family coverage, plus $1,000 at 55 or older. Publication 969 explains how contributions from different sources count against the limit, which matters for anyone whose employer also contributes.

Check any direct primary care membership. For 2027, keep aggregate monthly fees at or under $150, or $300 where the arrangement covers more than one person, for the arrangement not to be treated as a health plan.

Find out where the cash is. Ask the HSA trustee whether the cash portion is on deposit at an FDIC-insured bank, and which bank. If the trustee is the same bank where you keep other savings, add the balances together.

Look at the beneficiary line. If there is no named beneficiary, the FDIC insures the HSA as your single account. If beneficiaries are named with valid testamentary language, it falls into the trust category. Either can be the right answer, but it should be a choice rather than a blank field.

The FDIC's [Electronic Deposit Insurance Estimator](https://edie.fdic.gov/) runs the category arithmetic for a specific bank once you know which accounts you hold there and how they are titled.

## Common questions

### Does the $1,000 extra for age 55 apply if I turn 55 late in the year?

Yes, under the test Publication 969 describes. The increase applies to "an eligible individual who is age 55 or older at the end of your tax year," so what counts is your age on the last day of the tax year. For 2027 that would take the $4,500 self-only limit to $5,500 and the $9,000 family limit to $10,000, assuming the $1,000 figure carries into 2027, which later IRS publications will state.

### Is the interest on HSA cash taxable like savings account interest?

No. Publication 969 states: "The interest or other earnings on the assets in the account are tax free." That is the main difference from an ordinary savings account or CD, where interest is taxable as it is earned. The condition comes on the way out: distributions not used for qualified medical expenses carry an additional 20% tax, with no additional tax on distributions after the owner is disabled, reaches 65, or dies.

### Is my HSA FDIC-insured, and for how much?

When the HSA's cash is on deposit at an FDIC-insured bank, yes. With no designated beneficiaries, the FDIC insures it "as the single account of the owner," added to the owner's other single accounts at that bank under one $250,000 cap. With named beneficiaries and valid testamentary language, it falls under the trust accounts category, where the FDIC's example of one owner with three beneficiaries reaches $750,000. Balances moved into investments are not bank deposits.

The 2027 figures are settled and modest in their change: $100 more for self-only coverage, $250 more for family coverage. What varies more from one household to the next is how the account is set up. A plan that qualifies, a contribution sized to the limit, cash that is actually earning something, and a beneficiary on file together decide what the HSA is worth as savings, and only the first two are settled at open enrollment unless someone checks the other two at the same time.
