Four ordinary places hold most household cash: a high-yield savings account, a money market account, a certificate of deposit, and interest checking. They differ on three things that matter more than the advertised rate — whether the rate can change, how fast you can get the money out, and what it costs to break the arrangement early.

This post explains how each one works mechanically, uses the FDIC's national average rates as a common baseline, and runs the arithmetic on one decision that is easy to get wrong: breaking a longer CD instead of picking a shorter one. All four are deposit accounts, so all four carry the same $250,000 of FDIC coverage per depositor, per insured bank, per ownership category.

One product with a similar name does not belong to that group. A money market *fund* is an investment held at a brokerage, not a money market *account* at a bank, and that distinction decides whether deposit insurance applies at all.

## The national averages, as of August 17, 2026

These are FDIC national average rates across insured institutions, as of August 17, 2026. Treat them as a baseline for comparison, not as a rate you are being offered.

| Product | National average APY |
|---|---|
| Savings | 0.38% |
| Interest checking | 0.07% |
| Money market | 0.63% |
| 1 month CD | 0.22% |
| 3 month CD | 1.14% |
| 6 month CD | 1.41% |
| 12 month CD | 1.71% |
| 24 month CD | 1.57% |
| 36 month CD | 1.34% |
| 48 month CD | 1.27% |
| 60 month CD | 1.36% |

Two patterns sit in that column. Every CD term except one month averages above all three of the demand-deposit averages, while the 1 month CD at 0.22% averages below both savings and money market. And the CD figures do not climb steadily with term: the 12 month average of 1.71% is the highest number in the table, above the 60 month average of 1.36%.

## High-yield savings accounts

A savings account pays a variable rate. The bank can change it at any time, in either direction, and your balance simply starts earning the new rate. Nothing matures and nothing is locked.

That is the trade. Deposit rates move with the Federal Reserve's policy rate and with competition among banks for deposits, and a variable-rate account passes those changes through in both directions without any action from you.

Regulation D's six-transfer-per-month limit on savings accounts was suspended in 2020, and many banks still set limits of their own. Your deposit agreement states whether yours does and what an excess transfer costs.

One comparison detail applies to every account in this post. APY already includes the effect of compounding; a stated interest rate does not. Two accounts quoting the same number are not paying the same amount if one figure is an APY and the other is a nominal rate, so line up APY against APY before you decide.

## Money market accounts

A money market deposit account is also variable-rate and also FDIC insured. Some money market accounts include check writing or a debit card. The product name does not settle that question: the account disclosure lists the withdrawal methods your account supports, and that list is what to compare between two offers.

As of August 17, 2026, the FDIC national average was 0.63% APY for money market accounts and 0.38% APY for savings. Both figures are averages of many institutions, so the number that matters to you is the specific APY a bank quotes on the balance you will actually hold.

### Money market account vs. money market fund

A money market fund is bought through a brokerage. It is an investment: no FDIC insurance, a share price that can move, and a return quoted as a yield rather than an APY. Deposits at a bank that fails are insured; investments are not. Check which one you are being sold before treating the two as interchangeable.

## Certificates of deposit

A CD fixes a rate for a stated term. You give up access, and in exchange the rate cannot be cut while the term runs. Withdraw early and the bank charges an early-withdrawal penalty, stated in the disclosure and commonly quoted as a number of months of interest.

Two mechanics deserve a look before you sign. Because the penalty is defined in months of interest rather than as a share of your gain, breaking a CD very early can cost more than the account has earned; the disclosure states whether the shortfall comes out of principal. And the disclosure also states what happens at maturity, including whether the CD renews automatically and how long the grace period lasts.

### The ladder structure

A ladder is not a separate product. It is several CDs of different terms opened at the same time, so that one matures at regular intervals. Split $12,000 evenly across 12, 24, and 36 month CDs and each rung is $12,000 ÷ 3 = $4,000; something comes due every twelve months after the first year, and you decide then whether to spend it or open a new CD at whatever rate exists at that moment. The structure buys you scheduled access without an early-withdrawal penalty, at the cost of not having every dollar in whichever single term pays most.

## Interest checking

Interest checking exists to move money, not to grow it. The national average was 0.07% APY as of August 17, 2026, the lowest figure in the table above. Judge a checking account on fees, access, and features, and keep only the balance you spend from.

## The penalty math

Say you put $10,000 into a 12 month CD at the 1.71% national average APY, then need the money after six months. The disclosure sets the penalty at three months of interest. To keep the steps readable I use simple monthly proration and ignore compounding within the year; a real disclosure states its own method.

- A full year at 1.71% on $10,000 is $171.00.
- One month is $171.00 ÷ 12 = $14.25.
- Six months earned: $14.25 × 6 = $85.50.
- Penalty of three months: $14.25 × 3 = $42.75.
- Net after breaking the CD: $85.50 − $42.75 = $42.75.

Now the alternatives over the same six months on the same $10,000. Savings at the 0.38% average earns $38.00 across a full year, so half a year is $19.00. A 6 month CD at the 1.41% average earns $141.00 across a full year, so six months is $70.50.

Ranked: the 6 month CD at $70.50, the broken 12 month CD at $42.75, savings at $19.00. Matching the term to the date you need the money earned $70.50 − $42.75 = $27.75 more than breaking the longer CD, and even the broken CD finished $42.75 − $19.00 = $23.75 ahead of savings. Penalties are real money: the $42.75 charge took exactly half of the $85.50 this CD had earned. The point is that the six-month CD reached $70.50 without paying any penalty at all, and every number in that comparison is printed before you commit.

## Matching the account to the money

- Money you might spend this month: interest checking, judged on access and fees.
- Money you might need at any time, including an emergency fund: a savings or money market account, where the rate can change but the balance never locks.
- Money with a known date, such as a tax payment or a closing: a CD whose term ends on or before that date.
- Money above one bank's insurance limit: split it by insured institution or by ownership category, not by product type, since all four products share the same $250,000 calculation.

## The short version

Variable-rate accounts trade rate certainty for access. CDs trade access for rate certainty. Interest checking trades both for convenience. The FDIC's national averages as of August 17, 2026 show term deposits averaging above demand deposits at every CD length except one month, with the 12 month CD the highest of the eleven figures. Before opening anything, read the two numbers the disclosure actually commits to: the APY and the early-withdrawal penalty.
