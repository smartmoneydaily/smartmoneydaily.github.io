An account you opened, funded, and then stopped thinking about does not sit at the bank forever. After a period of no owner contact set by state law, the bank is required to report the balance to the state as unclaimed property and hand the money over.

Nothing is lost permanently. It is recoverable, usually indefinitely. But it stops earning, and getting it back is a claims process rather than a login.

## What counts as dormant

Dormancy periods are set by state law and commonly run three to five years for deposit accounts. The clock starts at the last owner-initiated contact.

That phrase is where most of the confusion sits. Interest posting to the account is a bank action, not yours, and in most states it does not reset the clock. Neither does a monthly statement being generated, or an automatic transfer that was set up years earlier and continues running without your involvement.

What does count varies by state but typically includes logging into online banking, making a deposit or withdrawal you initiated, contacting the bank about the account, or responding to a notice about it. Some states have modernized their statutes to count electronic contact; others still describe written communication.

For a certificate of deposit, the dormancy clock generally starts at maturity rather than at opening. A five-year CD that auto-renews without contact can accumulate a long period of technical inactivity while looking perfectly healthy on paper.

## The process before the money leaves

Banks are required to attempt contact before reporting an account. That usually means a letter to the address on file, sometimes more than one, within a window before the reporting deadline.

The failure mode is obvious: the address on file is old. An account opened at a previous address, never updated because there was no reason to log in, receives a notice that goes nowhere. The bank has satisfied its obligation and the owner never knew.

After the dormancy period expires and contact attempts fail, the balance is remitted to the state's unclaimed property division, typically on an annual reporting cycle.

## What happens after the state has it

Most states hold unclaimed property indefinitely, and the original owner or their heirs can claim it at any time. A minority of states place time limits on certain property types, and a few pay interest on money held; most do not.

Claiming requires proving identity and connection to the account. That is straightforward for a living owner with matching identification and an old address that appears in the record. It is considerably more work for an heir, who is proving a chain of relationship and often producing a death certificate and estate documentation.

Searching is free. Every state runs an unclaimed property site, and a national search site operated by state administrators covers most of them. Any service charging a percentage to find and recover this money is charging for a search you can run yourself in a few minutes.

## The interest cost

Money at the state is not earning. An account that goes dormant with a meaningful balance and stays with the state for several years before anyone notices has given up whatever it would have earned in the interim.

For an emergency fund parked at a bank that stopped being the primary institution, that can be a real number. It is also the least dramatic way to lose yield: no bad decision, no fee, just an account that nobody looked at.

## Preventing it

Four habits handle it entirely:

1. **Log into every account at least once a year.** A login is owner contact in most states, and it takes a minute.
2. **Update your address at every institution when you move**, including ones you no longer actively use. This is the single most common cause.
3. **Keep a list of every account you hold**, including the small ones opened for a bonus and forgotten. An account nobody remembers cannot be maintained.
4. **Note CD maturity dates**, since a renewing certificate can be inactive for years without appearing to be.

The same list solves a related problem. Accounts that nobody knows about are the ones that go unclaimed after a death, and an executor working from statements that stopped arriving has no way to find them. Sharing the list with whoever would handle your affairs is worth more than any individual account on it.

## Checking for money already out there

If you have moved often or closed accounts over the years, running your name and former addresses through the unclaimed property site for each state you have lived in is worth the twenty minutes. Old deposit balances, uncashed final paychecks, refunds from closed utility accounts, and forgotten security deposits all end up in the same place.

Run your own numbers instead: your balance, the APY on offer, and the period you plan to hold.

## Safe deposit boxes follow the same path

The dormancy rules cover contents as well as balances. A safe deposit box whose rent goes unpaid and whose holder cannot be reached is drilled, inventoried, and its contents turned over to the state after the statutory period.

States handle the contents differently from cash. Documents are stored, and tangible property is frequently auctioned after a holding period, with the proceeds held for the owner in place of the items. That converts an heirloom into a dollar amount, permanently.

Two things prevent it, and both are administrative. Keep the rent on an account that stays funded, and make sure someone else knows the box exists. A box paid by automatic debit from an account that itself goes dormant is a compound version of the same problem.

## What states actually do with the money

Unclaimed property divisions hold cash and pay claims on request. Most states put the funds into the general fund in the meantime and pay claims out of current revenue, which is why the money remains claimable regardless of how long ago it was remitted.

A handful of states pay interest on cash held; most do not. A handful impose deadlines on certain property types; most do not. The practical assumption is that cash is recoverable indefinitely and non-cash property may not survive in its original form.

State administrators also run outreach, publishing searchable databases and occasionally mailing likely owners. The databases are the useful part, and they are free.

## What a claim requires

For a living owner, the standard package is identification, proof of the connection between you and the reported address or account, and a signed claim form. Straightforward claims are often processed in weeks.

For an heir, the requirements expand to a death certificate, proof of relationship, and documentation of authority to act for the estate, sometimes including letters testamentary. Claims involving multiple heirs or an estate that was never formally administered take considerably longer.

The asymmetry between those two processes is the argument for the record-keeping habit. An account list costs nothing to maintain and removes the hardest part of the problem for whoever handles your affairs later.
