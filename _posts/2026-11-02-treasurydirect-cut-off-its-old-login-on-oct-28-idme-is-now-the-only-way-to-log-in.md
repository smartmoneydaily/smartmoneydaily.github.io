On Oct. 28, 2026, TreasuryDirect retired its traditional login, the one tied to an account number, security questions and a personal security image. Since that day, [ID.me has been the only way to log in](https://www.treasurydirect.gov/savings-bonds/help-center/faqs/idme-faq/) to a TreasuryDirect account online. That's the account where savers hold electronic I bonds and EE bonds, along with Treasury bills, notes and bonds bought directly from the government. ID.me had been available as an option since Sept. 13. Anyone who hadn't linked it by Oct. 28 can't log in online until they do.

Nothing has happened to the money, though. TreasuryDirect's FAQ says your funds "are not permanently frozen or seized," and you can restore online access at any time by finishing the ID.me setup. The timing was awkward for I bond buyers. The switch came one day before a deadline posted on [TreasuryDirect's home page](https://www.treasurydirect.gov/): to get the 4.26% I bond rate, purchases had to be completed by 11:59 p.m. on Thursday, Oct. 29, for an October issue date.

## What keeps running without a login

The login change doesn't touch the account's automatic activity. Scheduled auto-reinvestments "will continue uninterrupted," TreasuryDirect says, and the switch has no effect on "the automatic processing of reinvestments, maturities, interest payments, etc."

So a 13-week bill set to roll over will still roll into the next auction, and a bill that isn't set to roll still matures on schedule. Savings bonds that are still earning interest keep earning it. None of that waits for you to log in.

What stops is anything you'd do yourself online. Until you verify through ID.me, you "will not be able to log in to view, edit, or cancel these pending transactions." That means no canceling a [bill reinvestment](/2026/10/20/how-a-t-bill-auction-sets-your-rate-noncompetitive-bids-and-the-oct-15-rule/) online, no buying a new bond and no cashing one in through the website.

Tax forms are the one offline route the FAQ spells out. ID.me is "the simplest way" to pull your 1099s online, but you can also ask for the current year's forms by mail. Call TreasuryDirect Customer Support at **844-284-2676** once the forms are available in January, which for 2026 forms means January 2027. If you're not sure which year a bill's interest belongs to, [an earlier post](/2026/10/16/t-bills-and-short-cds-bought-now-which-tax-year-gets-the-interest/) walks through how the maturity date decides it.

Mail can be slow. As of Oct. 30, TreasuryDirect's home page said a high volume of requests was delaying its responses to mailed requests. Its listed processing times may run two weeks or longer to reopen a locked account or update banking details, and ten months or longer for trust-related and other TreasuryDirect requests. It doesn't give a separate time for mailed tax forms.

## Setting up ID.me for TreasuryDirect

The setup starts on TreasuryDirect.gov, not on ID.me's site. Click "Log In," then choose "Secure Sign In." You're sent to ID.me, where you either create a wallet or sign in to one you already have.

To create one, have two government-issued forms of identity ready: your Social Security number and a valid photo ID, such as a driver's license, state ID, passport or passport card. You may be asked for photo verification with your phone's camera. No smartphone? You can verify on a live video call with an ID.me "Trusted Referee" from any computer with an internet connection, and the same video option is offered if automatic verification fails.

The typical setup takes 5 to 10 minutes, by TreasuryDirect's estimate. After that, a login takes your ID.me email, password and a security code. Physical security keys such as YubiKeys work as the second factor if you'd rather not get codes on your phone.

Once you link, your old login and security image are retired, and typing the old login just produces an error. You won't need your TreasuryDirect account number to link, but hold on to it and your security answers, since support may ask for them later. A wallet you already set up through another organization can work, though it may not meet TreasuryDirect's security level, in which case ID.me asks for more verification. Login.gov isn't affiliated with ID.me and can't be used at all.

If you were locked out after too many failed login attempts, a successful ID.me login lets you back in automatically, with no mailed request.

What TreasuryDirect can't do is fix or bypass a failed verification. Its agents "cannot bypass or help with this identity verification process," and because ID.me is a separate company, problems with text codes, ID uploads or logins go to ID.me's own help center. The mailed tax-form route still works either way.

## Updating your phone number takes at least a week

ID.me treats your phone number as its own piece of evidence that you are who you say you are, alongside your SSN, address and date of birth. For that reason it has no self-service way to change the number. To update it, you have to close your ID.me wallet, wait at least 7 days, then go back through TreasuryDirect to create a new wallet and repeat the identity proofing.

Name and address changes are easier. You can update your name in the wallet yourself, and after approval the new name carries over the next time you log in to TreasuryDirect. An address change goes through ID.me support, which may ask you to upload a proof-of-address document.

If I had bills rolling over at TreasuryDirect and a new phone number coming up, I'd check the maturity dates first. A week without a login won't stop a scheduled reinvestment, but it could keep you from canceling one in time.

## Spouses, kids, trusts and Americans abroad

Each person needs their own wallet. Spouses can't share one ID.me wallet or one email address, "even if you share a household or bank account," so a couple that shares an email will need a second one.

One wallet can reach several accounts, though. If you manage other accounts, you get a single login for all of them, and after you verify, TreasuryDirect lists every account associated with your identity.

For a child's account, the parent or guardian verifies their own identity and manages the account through that login. The child doesn't need a wallet. The catch comes at 18. When a child turns 18, the account is temporarily put on hold, and the parent has to deliver it to the new adult. The new adult then has to open their own TreasuryDirect account and verify through ID.me to take control of the money.

Trusts, estates and businesses work through a person. The trustee, manager or person holding power of attorney verifies their own identity with ID.me, and the entity's details can still be updated with help from TreasuryDirect Customer Support.

U.S. citizens living abroad can verify with ID.me, and a non-U.S. phone number is fine. TreasuryDirect accounts still require a U.S. address of record, though, so the address in your ID.me wallet has to be a U.S. address before you can log in.

## What ID.me shares with TreasuryDirect

ID.me passes six things to TreasuryDirect: your full name, Social Security number, date of birth, mailing address, email and phone number. In TreasuryDirect's words, ID.me "does not have access to your TreasuryDirect account balances, your transaction history, or your linked personal bank account information." Decline to share, and you're sent back to the homepage without logging in. You'll be asked again the next time you try.

Check your name before you link. If the name on your TreasuryDirect account doesn't exactly match the one in your ID.me wallet (a nickname, a middle initial, a suffix), TreasuryDirect changes the account name to match the wallet.

Setup is free. TreasuryDirect's notice says ID.me "will not charge you or ask for a credit card" to set up or link an account, so I'd treat any message asking for a setup fee as a scam. The transition email TreasuryDirect sent came from treasurydirect@lists.fiscal.treasury.gov, and its advice is to double-check any link in an email or type TreasuryDirect.gov into your browser yourself. A login change is an easy hook for impostors. In the FTC's latest report on older consumers, government imposters ranked second by reported dollars lost among people 60 and over, as I went through in [an earlier post](/2026/10/31/fraud-at-60-and-over-3-billion-lost-in-2025-over-1-billion-by-bank-transfer/).

## The November rate reset

TreasuryDirect [announces new I bond rates](https://www.treasurydirect.gov/savings-bonds/i-bonds/i-bonds-interest-rates/) every May 1 and Nov. 1. I bonds issued from May 1 through Oct. 31, 2026, started with a 4.26% combined rate for their first six months, including a 0.90% fixed rate that never changes. Existing bonds don't all switch on Nov. 1, though. Each one changes rate every six months from its own issue date. EE bonds issued in the May-to-October window earn 2.40%.

Bonds issued from November through April get the rates set for that period, and buying one online now takes an ID.me login. So does canceling a scheduled bill reinvestment online, or checking what your I bonds are worth before you cash any in once they're past the [12-month lock](/2026/09/24/i-bonds-vs-a-high-yield-savings-account-the-12-month-lock/). Before five years, cashing one costs the last three months of interest, and the values TreasuryDirect shows already leave those months out.

I wouldn't leave this until January. The account keeps running without you, but you can't touch it online until you link.

The rules above come from TreasuryDirect's ID.me FAQ and the notices on its home page, and the rates from its I bond page. The next date I'd mark is in January, when 2026 tax forms show up in accounts that open online only through ID.me.
