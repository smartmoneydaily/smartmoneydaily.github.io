![Diagram showing how a Federal Reserve policy rate change travels through banks to savings, money market, and CD yields](/assets/pin-images/2026-06-16-the-impact-of-federal-interest-rate-changes-on-your-financial-options.png)


A Federal Reserve decision does not change the APY on your savings account. It changes a target range for a rate that banks charge each other, and every deposit rate downstream of that is set by an individual institution under the terms of its own contracts. The gap between those two facts explains how a policy headline and your monthly statement can disagree.

So the honest answer to how fast deposit rates follow is that no schedule exists to quote — but the speed of your own account is measurable, and the measurement takes one division. This post traces the chain from a policy decision to the number on your statement, sets out that calculation, and works through what a fixed term costs if you exit it early. There is no forecast here about where rates go. There is a method for reacting to what your own accounts actually do.

The only rates quoted as current fact are the FDIC's published national averages. Figures used to demonstrate arithmetic are labeled where they are invented.

## The chain from policy rate to your statement

The Federal Open Market Committee sets a target range for the federal funds rate, the rate at which banks lend to each other overnight. That rate is not a consumer product, and no rule converts a change in it into a change in your APY.

What sits between the two is the contract. A variable-rate deposit account can be repriced under the terms of its agreement, which is why the disclosure language about whether and when the bank may change the rate matters more than any headline number. A CD's rate is fixed for its term by contract, so a policy change does not touch a CD you already hold. Same news, two entirely different consequences, decided by paperwork you signed.

Banks price deposits against their own funding needs and against competition for deposits. That is a mechanism, not a schedule. It explains why deposit rates move at all; it does not tell you when yours will.

FDIC national averages as of August 17, 2026:

|---|---|
| Savings | 0.38% |
| Money market | 0.63% |
| 3 month CD | 1.14% |
| 6 month CD | 1.41% |
| 12 month CD | 1.71% |
| 24 month CD | 1.57% |
| 36 month CD | 1.34% |
| 60 month CD | 1.36% |

Read the shape rather than any single row. Among the CD terms listed, the 12-month average is the highest, and the 24, 36, and 60 month averages all sit below it. Longer does not automatically mean more. These are averages across insured institutions, not quotes you can act on.

## Pass-through, measured on your own account

Pass-through is one division: the change in your APY divided by the change in the policy target range over the same window.

The numbers in this paragraph are invented to show the arithmetic; substitute your own. Suppose the target range moves down by 0.25 percentage points, and over the three months that follow, your savings APY goes from 3.60% to 3.45%. Your APY moved 0.15 percentage points. Pass-through is 0.15 ÷ 0.25 = 0.60, or 60%.

On a $20,000 balance, that is the difference between 20,000 × 0.0360 = $720.00 a year and 20,000 × 0.0345 = $690.00 a year. The change is $30.00 a year, or $2.50 a month using simple monthly proration. Whether that is worth opening a new account somewhere is your call, but it is now a number rather than an irritation.

Run the same division after the next few moves and you have a record of how quickly your bank responds — measured, not assumed.

## Locking versus waiting, worked out

The cost of locking a term is the early-withdrawal penalty, and the disclosure states it, commonly as a number of months of interest.

Take $10,000, using national averages as of August 17, 2026, and simple monthly proration for the partial periods:

- A 12 month CD at 1.71% earns 10,000 × 0.0171 = **$171.00** across the full term.
- Savings at 0.38% earns 10,000 × 0.0038 = **$38.00** over that same year.
- Now suppose you close the CD at six months, and the disclosure states a penalty of three months' interest. Accrued: 171.00 × 6/12 = $85.50. Penalty: 171.00 × 3/12 = $42.75. Net: 85.50 − 42.75 = **$42.75**.
- Savings across those same six months: 38.00 × 6/12 = **$19.00**.

Under those assumptions, breaking the CD at the halfway mark still left $42.75 against $19.00. That will not hold for every penalty schedule — a longer penalty, or a narrower gap between the two rates, flips the result. The point is that the penalty is a stated, computable number, so the decision can be arithmetic rather than nerves. Actual accrual and compounding differ slightly from simple proration; the convention is named so you can reproduce every figure above.

## Repricing by account type

One policy change reaches four common account types along four different paths:

- **Savings and money market accounts.** The rate is not fixed by contract. Your agreement states whether the bank may change it and whether it gives notice, so the answer for your account is in your paperwork, not in the news.
- **Interest checking.** Same paperwork question, much lower base. The national average is 0.07% as of August 17, 2026, against 0.38% for savings — a 0.31 percentage point gap, which on $10,000 held for a year is 10,000 × 0.0031 = $31.00.
- **A CD you already hold.** Fixed by contract until maturity. Nothing in a policy announcement changes it, which is the entire point of a term product.
- **A CD reaching maturity.** This is the moment that matters. The disclosure states what happens at maturity, including whether the CD renews automatically and how many days the grace period runs. A maturity you ignore is a decision you made by default.

Put maturity dates on a calendar with a reminder a week ahead. That one habit converts an automatic renewal into a choice.

## Nominal APY and real return

Every rate in this post is nominal. Real return is the nominal rate minus inflation, and it is what determines whether your balance buys more next year than it does now.

There is no shortcut worth trusting here, so use the source: the Bureau of Labor Statistics publishes the Consumer Price Index, and the current twelve-month change is the figure to subtract. Do the subtraction yourself rather than accepting anyone's characterization of it. Two accounts with the same APY have the same real return, so this changes what a rate means to you, not which account wins a comparison.

## Timing vs. structure

Timing asks when to lock. Structure asks how much to lock at once, and structure is answerable without a forecast.

A ladder splits the money into equal parts with staggered maturities. Using the averages above, four equal rungs at 3, 6, 12, and 24 months blend to (1.14 + 1.41 + 1.71 + 1.57) ÷ 4 = 5.83 ÷ 4 = 1.4575%, or 1.46% rounded, as a simple mean of the four term averages. Every few months one rung matures, so part of the money reprices at whatever is then available and the rest keeps its contracted rate.

That structure gives up the best single outcome in exchange for never needing the timing call to be right. Which trade suits you depends on when you actually need the cash, which is a question about your calendar rather than about policy.

## Three lines for your notes file

1. The APY on each deposit account you hold, with today's date, taken from your bank's own disclosure page.
2. The early-withdrawal penalty stated for any CD you hold or are considering, in months of interest.
3. The largest amount you are confident you will not need for twelve months.

Those three lines turn the next policy headline into a calculation you can finish in five minutes.
