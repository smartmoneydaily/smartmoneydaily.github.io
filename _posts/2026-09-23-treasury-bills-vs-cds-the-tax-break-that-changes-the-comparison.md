![Treasury Bills vs CDs: The Tax Break That Changes the Comparison](/assets/pin-images/2026-09-23-treasury-bills-vs-cds-the-tax-break-that-changes-the-comparison.png)

Two quotes sit on the same screen: a 52-week Treasury bill and a 12-month certificate of deposit, both showing the same number. Same maturity, same headline rate, the same money going in on the same day. Nothing on either quote separates them, and that is the trap. The figure that decides the comparison is printed somewhere else — in a two-line row on a government rate table and a single sentence in an IRS publication.

Treasury states it in five words. The "Bills at a Glance" table on [TreasuryDirect](https://www.treasurydirect.gov/marketable-securities/treasury-bills/) carries a row labeled Taxes, and the row holds two entries: "Federal tax due on interest earned" and "No state or local taxes." [Publication 550](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p550) puts it in a full sentence: "Interest income from Treasury bills, notes, and bonds is subject to federal income tax but is exempt from all state and local income taxes." CD interest has no equivalent shelter. It is taxable wherever the depositor's state taxes income, on top of the federal tax both instruments owe.

What follows from that is short arithmetic. At identical headline rates, the bill's advantage over the CD equals the state marginal rate multiplied by the interest — not approximately, exactly. On $100,000 held for a year at a hypothetical 4.5%, the gap is $598.50 for a filer at California's top state rate and nothing at all for a filer in Texas. Run the same relationship backwards and it produces a threshold: to draw level, a CD has to pay the bill's rate divided by one minus the state rate. At the top California bracket that means beating the bill by roughly 69 basis points before the CD is worth choosing.

The rest of the comparison has nothing to do with tax and pulls in other directions. A CD arrives with a rate disclosed before the account opens and $250,000 of federal insurance behind it. The bill's rate is set at auction, nothing insures it, and no coverage ceiling applies, because the obligation belongs to the government doing the insuring. Three ordinary situations cancel the tax argument outright.

## Where the exemption is written

Publication 550 sets up the exemption by first saying what these securities are: "Treasury bills, notes, and bonds are direct debts (obligations) of the U.S. government." The tax treatment follows immediately under the heading "Taxation of interest," and the sentence does two jobs at once: it confirms the federal tax and removes the state one. "Interest income from Treasury bills, notes, and bonds is subject to federal income tax but is exempt from all state and local income taxes. You should receive Form 1099-INT showing the interest (in box 3) paid to you for the year."

That box number is the mechanical tell. Publication 550 describes it directly: "Form 1099-INT, box 3 shows the interest income you received from U.S. savings bonds, Treasury bills, Treasury notes, and Treasury bonds." Bank interest (savings, money market, CD) lands in box 1 instead. A single 1099-INT can carry both, and a state return that starts from federal taxable income is where the two boxes stop being interchangeable.

Nothing in the exemption touches the federal side. The same publication lists, under taxable interest, that "Interest on U.S. obligations, such as [U.S. Treasury](https://www.treasurydirect.gov/) bills, notes, and bonds, and obligations issued by any agency or instrumentality of the United States is taxable for federal income tax purposes." A saver in a state with no income tax gets nothing from the exemption, because there was no state tax to exempt.

Two boundaries first. The exemption covers state and local income tax, not every state levy. And whether it survives being held indirectly, through a fund that owns bills rather than the bills themselves, is a matter of state law and fund reporting, not something the federal sources above settle. The case used throughout here is a bill held directly. The broader picture of what taxes do to a quoted yield is in [The Tax Bite on Savings Interest: What Your Real Yield Is](/2026/08/26/the-tax-bite-on-savings-interest-what-your-real-yield-is/).

## The after-tax gap and the break-even rate

Hold the rate constant to isolate the effect. Take $100,000 for one year, a bill and a CD both yielding a hypothetical 4.5%, and $4,500 of interest either way. Assume a federal marginal rate of 22% and a filer taking the standard deduction, so no federal offset for state tax enters the picture.

| State marginal rate | State tax on the CD interest | CD, after tax | Bill, after tax | Bill advantage |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0% | $0.00 | $3,510.00 | $3,510.00 | $0.00 |
| 5% | $225.00 | $3,285.00 | $3,510.00 | $225.00 |
| 9.3% | $418.50 | $3,091.50 | $3,510.00 | $418.50 |
| 10.9% | $490.50 | $3,019.50 | $3,510.00 | $490.50 |
| 13.3% | $598.50 | $2,911.50 | $3,510.00 | $598.50 |

The last column is the second column. That is the whole result: the advantage is the state tax the CD pays and the bill does not, dollar for dollar, with no interaction effects to model.

The bracket figures at the bottom of that table are real ceilings rather than typical rates. [Tax Foundation](https://taxfoundation.org/data/all/state/state-income-tax-rates-2026/) data as of February 11, 2026 puts California's top marginal individual rate at 13.30 percent, applying to single-filer taxable income above $1,000,000, and New York's at 10.90 percent on income above $25,000,000. Most savers sit well below both. They are in the table for scale: even at those brackets the exemption is worth about 13 cents and 11 cents on the dollar of interest, and for the great majority of filers considerably less.

The table leaves out one complication on purpose. A filer who itemizes and deducts state income tax federally recovers part of it, narrowing the bill's advantage by whatever that deduction is worth on that particular return — a question for a tax professional rather than a general table.

Inverted, the same relationship names a threshold. If the bill yields *t* and the state taxes interest at *s*, a CD paying *c* matches it after state tax when *c* × (1 − *s*) = *t*, so the required CD rate is *t* ÷ (1 − *s*). That quotient is the taxable-equivalent yield of the bill, and the difference between it and *t* is the premium a bank has to offer before its CD is competitive.

| State marginal rate | CD rate needed to match a 4.5% bill (percent) | Premium over the bill |
|---|---|---|
| 0% | 4.50 | none |
| 5% | 4.74 | about 24 basis points |
| 9.3% | 4.96 | about 46 basis points |
| 10.9% | 5.05 | about 55 basis points |
| 13.3% | 5.19 | about 69 basis points |

The premium grows faster than the state rate does, because the denominator shrinks as the rate climbs. At the top California bracket the flip is easy to locate: a CD at 5% keeps 5 × (1 − 0.133) = 4.335 after state tax, short of the bill, while a CD at 5.2% keeps 4.508 and clears it. Somewhere near 5.19 the two are indifferent, and every basis point above that belongs to the CD. In a state with no income tax the threshold is the bill's own rate, so a CD at 4.6% wins and a CD at 4.4% loses, with nothing else to calculate.

The inputs have to match. A bill's return is quoted both as a discount rate and as a coupon-equivalent yield, and only the second sits on the same footing as a CD's annual percentage yield. Comparing the wrong pair quietly hands one side a headline it did not earn. That discipline applies to two bank offers, covered in [Six Lines in a Bank Disclosure That Decide What You Earn](/2026/08/24/six-lines-in-a-bank-disclosure-that-decide-what-you-earn/).

## What the two curves actually pay

In practice the two instruments have not been quoted at the same number, and the gap has been too wide for any state exemption to decide it. Treasury's daily bill rates for [September 18, 2026](https://home.treasury.gov/resource-center/data-chart-center/interest-rates/TextView?type=daily_treasury_bill_rates), stated as coupon equivalents, against the [FDIC](https://www.fdic.gov/national-rates-and-rate-caps) national average CD rates published as of August 17, 2026:

| Bill term | Coupon equivalent, Sept 18, 2026 (percent) | FDIC national average CD (percent) | Gap in percentage points |
|---|---|---|---|
| 4 weeks | 3.89 | 1 month, 0.22 | 3.67 |
| 8 weeks | 4.00 | — | — |
| 13 weeks | 4.08 | 3 month, 1.14 | 2.94 |
| 17 weeks | 4.13 | — | — |
| 26 weeks | 4.27 | 6 month, 1.41 | 2.86 |
| 52 weeks | 4.41 | 12 month, 1.71 | 2.70 |

The two columns are dated about a month apart, so the gaps are indicative rather than a clean same-day snapshot. Even allowing for that, an exemption worth at most about two-thirds of a percentage point at the top California bracket does not close a gap of 2.7 points. The tax break is a tiebreaker at the competitive end of the CD market, not the main event against the average.

The averages sit that low because the [FDIC](https://www.fdic.gov/national-rates-and-rate-caps) national rate is "the average of rates paid by all insured depository institutions and credit unions for which data is available, with rates weighted by each institution's share of domestic deposits." Thousands of institutions paying nearly nothing drag the figure down. The FDIC's companion number tells the same story from the other side. The national rate cap is "the higher of: (1) the national rate plus 75 basis points; or (2) 120 percent of the current yield on similar maturity U.S. Department of the Treasury obligations plus 75 basis points." On August 17, 2026 the 12-month average was 1.71% and the matching cap 5.65 percent, a spread that opens only because the second leg of the formula is a Treasury calculation rather than a deposit one. Even the bank regulator benchmarks the top of the deposit market against Treasury yields.

Competitive CDs are a different population from the average, and the one worth comparing. Where the best terms tend to sit on the curve is the subject of [Building a CD Ladder When 12-Month CDs Pay the Most](/2026/08/19/building-a-cd-ladder-when-12-month-cds-pay-the-most/).

## Buying a bill: terms, minimum, auction

Treasury sells bills "for terms ranging from four weeks to 52 weeks," and the glance table lists the exact set: 4, 6, 8, 13, 17, 26, and 52 weeks. Cash management bills run at other terms but "are only available through a bank, broker, or dealer. We do not sell them in TreasuryDirect."

The purchase mechanics differ from opening a CD in ways that matter more than the paperwork suggests.

- **Minimum and increments.** "$100" minimum, "In increments of $100." Nothing about a bill requires a large balance, which is the opposite of the tiered-minimum structure many CD rate sheets use.
- **Maximum.** "$10 million (non-competitive bid)" per auction, or "35% of offering amount (competitive bid)."
- **Pricing.** "Bills are sold at a discount or at par (face value). When the bill matures, you are paid its face value." There is no periodic interest payment; the glance table's interest-paid row reads "When the bill matures."
- **Schedule.** "Weekly for 4, 6, 8, 13, 17, 26-week bills" and "Every four weeks for 52-week bills."

The largest difference is the one savers notice last. [TreasuryDirect](https://www.treasurydirect.gov/marketable-securities/buying-a-marketable-security/) is explicit: "When you schedule the purchase of a marketable security in TreasuryDirect, you don't know the interest rate. The interest rate is determined at auction." A non-competitive bid, the only kind TreasuryDirect accepts, trades certainty of rate for certainty of fill: "you are guaranteed to get the security you want in the amount you want" and "you agree to accept the discount (high) rate, (high) yield, or (high) discount margin determined at auction." A CD does the reverse: the APY is disclosed before the account is funded and fixed for the term.

Getting back out has a floor on it. New purchases through TreasuryDirect carry a lockup — "you must hold new Treasury marketable securities for at least 45 calendar days before transferring or selling them," though that holding period does not apply when a security is bought with proceeds from a reinvestment.

Rolling a bill is scheduled in advance rather than renewed automatically the way a CD is. [Reinvestments](https://www.treasurydirect.gov/marketable-securities/reinvesting-a-marketable-security/) can be set "when you buy the original security or at any time up to four business days before the original security matures," and a bill must roll into the same term: "you can reinvest a maturing 52-week bill only in another 52-week bill." The number of rolls is capped, because "bills may be scheduled for multiple reinvestments, up to two years":

| Bill term | Maximum scheduled reinvestments |
|---|---|
| 4-week | 25 |
| 6-week | 16 |
| 8-week | 10 |
| 13-week | 7 |
| 17-week | 6 |
| 26-week | 3 |
| 52-week | 1 |

Two failure modes come with that. The window closes early — "We close the option to reinvest four business days before the relevant auction." A roll can also cost more than the maturing bill returns, in which case "if funds are not available to pay the difference, we will cancel the reinvestment and deposit the proceeds of the maturing security into your bank account or C of I." A CD that auto-renews does none of this; it simply renews, which is a hazard of another kind.

## Insurance against credit

The second real difference is what stands behind the money, and the two answers are not the same kind of answer.

A CD is a deposit. [FDIC deposit insurance](https://www.fdic.gov/resources/deposit-insurance/understanding-deposit-insurance/) "covers $250,000 per depositor, per FDIC-insured bank, for each account ownership category," and the record behind it is why the coverage is taken for granted: "Since the FDIC was founded in 1933, no depositor has lost a penny of FDIC-insured funds." The insurance is a promise about a bank's failure, capped in dollars and counted per bank and per ownership category.

A bill is not insured and does not need to be. It is, in Publication 550's phrasing, a direct debt of the U.S. government — the same government that stands behind the insurance fund. There is no $250,000 line because there is no third party whose failure the coverage is meant to survive.

Where that distinction stops being academic is above the insurance limit. A $600,000 cash position in CDs has to be spread across banks, ownership categories, or a deposit network to stay fully covered; the same $600,000 in bills raises no coverage question at all. The mechanics of stretching coverage are in [Insuring More Than $250,000 Without Opening Ten Accounts](/2026/09/02/insuring-more-than-250000-without-opening-ten-accounts/) and [How FDIC Ownership Categories Stack Coverage at One Bank](/2026/08/21/how-fdic-ownership-categories-stack-coverage-at-one-bank/).

One trap sits between the two. The FDIC's list of what it does not insure includes "Stocks and Bonds," and that list applies inside an insured bank's own brokerage. A Treasury bill bought through a bank is not a deposit of that bank, and a brokered CD is a different instrument again, with its own secondary-market pricing. Those distinctions are worked through in [Brokered CDs vs Bank CDs: The Differences That Matter](/2026/08/27/brokered-cds-vs-bank-cds-the-differences-that-matter/) and [Where Brokerage Cash Actually Sits and What Insures It](/2026/08/30/where-brokerage-cash-actually-sits-and-what-insures-it/).

## One instrument, two tax years

The exemption changes how much tax is owed. The reporting rules change when it is owed, and a bill bought late in a calendar year can push an entire year of interest onto the following return.

Publication 550 sets the default for bills: "The difference between the discounted price you pay for the bills and the face value you receive at maturity is interest income. Generally, you report this interest income when the bill is paid at maturity." A 52-week bill issued in November therefore puts its entire year of income into the following tax year. A CD's timing depends on how the bank pays it. Publication 550 brings deferred-account interest into income "when you actually receive it or are entitled to receive it without paying a substantial penalty," and adds that "the same is true for accounts that mature in 1 year or less and pay interest in a single payment at maturity." A CD crediting interest monthly or quarterly therefore splits the income across both calendar years, while one paying a single sum at maturity does not. What gets reported is gross in either case: a depositor charged an early-withdrawal penalty "must report the total amount of interest paid or credited to your account during the year without subtracting the penalty."

The default is not mandatory. "When you buy a short-term obligation (one with a fixed maturity date of 1 year or less from the date of issue), other than a tax-exempt obligation, you generally can choose to include any discount and interest payable on the obligation in income currently." For a short-term government obligation, the amount brought into income is "the accrued acquisition discount, if any, plus any other accrued interest payable on the obligation," with the acquisition discount defined as "the stated redemption price at maturity minus your basis." The choice is not open to everyone: current accrual is required for a short-term obligation "held by an accrual-basis taxpayer," held by a bank or regulated investment company, held primarily for sale to customers, or identified as part of a hedging transaction, among the listed cases.

Rolling bills does not push the income forward either: "you must report the full amount of the interest income on each of your Treasury bills by the time it reaches maturity." Each bill settles its own tax at its own maturity, whether the chain runs one roll or twenty-five. The same passage describes reinvestment proceeds as "par amount less any tax withheld," a reminder that withholding can reach this interest as it reaches bank interest — what triggers it and how the money comes back is in [Backup Withholding on Savings Interest: Why the Bank Took 24%](/2026/09/21/backup-withholding-on-savings-interest-why-the-bank-took-24/).

## A disclosed penalty against a market price

Both instruments can be exited early, at costs of different shapes.

Breaking a CD has a price written down before the account is opened, and the tax code partly refunds it. Publication 550: "If you withdraw funds from a certificate of deposit or other deferred interest account before maturity, you may be charged a penalty. The Form 1099-INT or similar statement given to you by the financial institution will show the total amount of interest in box 1 and will show the penalty separately in box 2. You must include in income all interest shown in Form 1099-INT, box 1. You can deduct the penalty on Schedule 1 (Form 1040), line 18." The number is knowable in advance, and the deduction sits in the adjustments section, so it does not require itemizing. The disclosure rules behind that penalty are in [CD Early Withdrawal Penalties: The Disclosure and the Deduction](/2026/09/22/cd-early-withdrawal-penalties-the-disclosure-and-the-deduction/), and the arithmetic of whether to break one is in [When Breaking a CD Actually Pays: Run the Break-Even](/2026/08/25/when-breaking-a-cd-actually-pays-run-the-break-even/).

Selling a bill has no penalty and no fixed price. The bills page states the option in one line: "You can hold a bill until it matures or sell it before it matures." What the sale returns goes unmentioned, because that is a market question. Sold before maturity, a bill settles at whatever the market pays that day, which can be above or below what was paid for it. And for the first 45 calendar days after a new purchase in TreasuryDirect, no sale is possible at all.

That inverts the usual intuition about which instrument is easier to leave. A CD's worst case is a disclosed number, fixed when the account opens, that can reach into principal. A bill's worst case before maturity is bounded by nothing but the price on the screen — and at maturity there is no bad case, since face value is paid in full.

## Where the comparison flips back

The tax argument is conditional. Three situations switch it off.

**No state income tax to avoid.** Tax Foundation's 2026 data counts eight states that "levy no individual income tax at all": Alaska, Florida, Nevada, New Hampshire, South Dakota, Tennessee, Texas and Wyoming. New Hampshire counts because it "repealed its interest and dividends tax as of 2025." In those states the taxable-equivalent yield equals the bill's own rate, the second table above collapses to its first row, and the decision reverts to whichever headline number is larger.

**A CD premium above the break-even.** Competitive CDs are priced by institutions competing for deposits, not by the Treasury market, and the gap between the best CD and the bill curve is not fixed. Whenever a CD clears *t* ÷ (1 − *s*), the exemption has been out-bid, and the CD wins on after-tax yield while also carrying insurance and a rate that was disclosed rather than auctioned.

**A tax-deferred account.** Inside an IRA, interest is not being taxed currently, so exempting it from a state tax nobody is charging is worth nothing. A bill held in an IRA gives up the CD's disclosed rate and gains nothing in return on the tax side. The retirement-account wrinkles are in [IRA CDs: Insurance, Rollovers, and the RMD Deadline](/2026/09/12/ira-cds-insurance-rollovers-and-the-rmd-deadline/).

Two smaller cases push the same way. A saver whose state taxable income is low enough that the interest is effectively untaxed sits in the first situation without living in one of those states. Anyone who might need the money before maturity has reason to prefer a cost printed in a disclosure to a price set by a market.

## Common questions

**Is Treasury bill interest exempt from federal tax too?** No. Publication 550 lists interest on U.S. obligations under taxable interest and states that bill, note and bond interest "is subject to federal income tax but is exempt from all state and local income taxes." The exemption is state and local only.

**How does the tax difference show up on the forms?** By box. Publication 550: "Form 1099-INT, box 3 shows the interest income you received from U.S. savings bonds, Treasury bills, Treasury notes, and Treasury bonds." Bank interest, including CD interest, is reported in box 1 of the same form.

**What does it take to buy a bill?** The minimum is $100, in $100 increments, with a non-competitive maximum of $10 million per auction. Bills "are sold at a discount or at par (face value)," and no interest is paid until maturity. In TreasuryDirect the rate is unknown when the purchase is scheduled, because it is set at auction.

**Can a Treasury bill be cashed early like a CD?** Not in the same way. TreasuryDirect states that a bill can be held to maturity or sold before it matures, and that new purchases there must be held "for at least 45 calendar days before transferring or selling them." An early sale settles at a market price rather than a penalty formula, so the cost is not knowable in advance the way a CD's is.

**Is a Treasury bill FDIC insured?** No, and the question has the wrong shape. FDIC insurance covers "$250,000 per depositor, per FDIC-insured bank, for each account ownership category" and applies to deposits at banks; the FDIC's own list of uninsured products includes stocks and bonds. A bill is a direct obligation of the U.S. government rather than a deposit at an institution that could fail. Confirming an institution's insured status before a transfer is covered in [How to Verify a Bank Is Actually FDIC Insured Before You Transfer](/2026/09/09/how-to-verify-a-bank-is-actually-fdic-insured-before-you-transfer/).

The exemption is worth exactly one number: the state marginal rate applied to the interest. That number decides the comparison only where the two quotes already sit close together. Against the 12-month national average of 1.71% the headline gap settles it first, and in eight states the number is zero.
