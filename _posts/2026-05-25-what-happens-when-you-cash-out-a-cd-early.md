Breaking a CD before it matures is not a mystery charge. It is a formula, it is written into the disclosure you received when you opened the account, and you can run it in about a minute once you know which two numbers to pull out.

This post works that arithmetic on a $10,000 CD: what the penalty comes to, the point in the term where you stop coming out behind, the case where you get back less than you deposited, and the separate break-even calculation for the other version of the question — whether paying a penalty to move money somewhere higher-paying actually pays off.

One convention before the numbers. Everything below uses simple proration of the annual figure rather than day-by-day compounding. APY already includes the effect of compounding across a year, while a stated interest rate does not, so slicing an APY-based annual figure into months is an approximation. At these balances the gap is pennies. Your bank's actual accrual method is described in the same disclosure as the penalty.

## The penalty, as your disclosure states it

Three lines in that document decide the whole outcome.

**The penalty size**, quoted as a span of interest — 90 days, six months, and 12 months of interest are all formats you will see. The span is set per product, so one bank's 60-month CD and its 6-month CD can carry very different penalties. Compare them on the same rate sheet before you assume anything.

**The base it applies to.** The disclosure states whether the penalty is figured on the amount you withdraw or on the entire balance, and which rate it is figured at. Those two choices can double or halve the bill on the same withdrawal.

**Whether partial withdrawals are allowed at all.** Some time deposits permit them; others require you to close the account.

Under federal Truth in Savings disclosure rules, a bank has to give you the terms of a time deposit in writing, and the early-withdrawal penalty is one of the required items. If you cannot find your paperwork, ask the bank for the account disclosure for that specific CD — not the general rate sheet, which usually will not contain the penalty terms.

## A worked example at $10,000

Take $10,000 in a 12-month CD. For the rate, use the FDIC national average for 12-month CDs, 1.71% APY as of August 17, 2026 — that is an average across insured institutions rather than an offer, so substitute your own rate when you run this for real. Penalty: 90 days of interest.

- A full year of interest: $10,000 × 1.71% = **$171.00**
- The penalty: $171.00 × (90 ÷ 365) = **$42.16**

That $42.16 is fixed. It does not shrink because you waited, and it does not grow because you waited. The interest you have accrued, on the other hand, climbs every month — which is what creates a crossover point.

| Month you break | Interest accrued | Penalty | Net | You receive |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | $14.25 | $42.16 | −$27.91 | $9,972.09 |
| 3 | $42.75 | $42.16 | +$0.59 | $10,000.59 |
| 6 | $85.50 | $42.16 | +$43.34 | $10,043.34 |
| 9 | $128.25 | $42.16 | +$86.09 | $10,086.09 |
| 12 (maturity) | $171.00 | none | +$171.00 | $10,171.00 |

The crossover is exact: $42.16 ÷ $171.00 = 0.2465 of a year, which is 2.96 months. Past roughly three months into this CD, breaking it still leaves you ahead of where you started.

Compare that against having kept the cash liquid the whole time. At the 0.38% national average savings rate on the same date, $10,000 earns $38.00 across a full year and $19.00 across six months. Break at month six and you net $43.34 against that $19.00 — still $24.34 better off. Break at month two and you are at −$13.66 against $6.33, a $19.99 swing the wrong way.

## Penalties that reach into principal

Look for a sentence in your disclosure saying the penalty may reduce principal. If it is there, the month-one row above is real: you deposit $10,000 and walk away with $9,972.09. If it is not there — some accounts cap the penalty at interest actually earned — your floor is the deposit itself.

There is a second route to the same outcome. If your CD pays interest out monthly into a checking account instead of leaving it inside the CD, the accrued interest is not sitting there to absorb the penalty. The bank still charges the full amount, and it comes out of what remains.

On taxes: an early-withdrawal penalty is reported separately from your interest income on Form 1099-INT, and it is generally deductible as an adjustment to income, meaning you do not have to itemize to claim it. Check the current IRS instructions or your preparer for how it applies to your return.

## Break-even against a higher rate

The other reason people ask about penalties is that they are looking at a better rate somewhere else. That is a payback-period question, and it has a clean formula:

**Extra dollars per year** = balance × (new rate − old rate)
**Months to recover** = penalty ÷ (extra dollars per year ÷ 12)

Run it with the national averages as placeholders. Say $10,000 is locked in a 60-month CD at 1.36% APY while 12-month CDs are averaging 1.71% APY — both figures being FDIC national averages as of August 17, 2026. The penalty on the existing CD is 180 days of interest.

- Annual interest on the old CD: $10,000 × 1.36% = $136.00
- Penalty: $136.00 × (180 ÷ 365) = **$67.07**
- Rate difference: 1.71% − 1.36% = 0.35 percentage points
- Extra per year: $10,000 × 0.0035 = $35.00, which is $2.92 per month
- Payback: $67.07 ÷ $2.9167 = **about 23 months**

Two conditions have to hold for that to mean anything. The new account has to keep paying that difference for at least 23 months, and you have to be comparing two rates you can actually get rather than two averages. Change the penalty to 90 days and the payback halves; widen the rate gap and it collapses. The formula is the useful part, not the illustration.

## Waivers, exceptions, and partial withdrawals

Several conditions can change or remove the penalty, and all of them live in your specific account terms rather than in any general rule:

- Some disclosures waive the penalty on the death of an owner or on a court determination of legal incapacity
- Some CDs are sold as no-penalty or bump-up products, where the trade-off shows up in the rate itself
- Where partial withdrawals are permitted and the penalty applies only to the amount withdrawn, taking $3,000 out of a $10,000 CD costs far less than closing it
- CDs held inside an IRA raise separate tax questions on top of whatever the bank charges

Read your terms first, then call and ask the bank to quote the exact dollar figure for closing the account today. That number is the only one worth deciding on.

## Structure over timing

Most of these decisions trace back to money being locked up that should not have been. The fix is structural rather than tactical.

Keep the tier you might actually need in something you can withdraw from without a penalty. Split the rest across staggered maturities so a portion comes due at regular intervals and you are never forced to break the whole balance for a partial need. The FDIC national averages as of August 17, 2026 show what you are paid for accepting that lock: 0.38% APY on savings and 0.63% on money market accounts, against 1.41% for a 6-month CD and 1.71% for a 12-month CD. Deposit rates move with Federal Reserve policy and with how hard institutions are competing for deposits, and neither of those is something you can schedule around.

Your CD is insured to $250,000 per depositor, per insured bank, per ownership category, and credit unions carry the same standard limit through the NCUA. Worth remembering that insurance answers a different question. It covers the bank failing. A penalty is a contract term you agreed to, and no insurance touches it.

## The decision in three numbers

Before you break anything, write these down and compare them.

1. The penalty in dollars, quoted by your bank for today
2. The interest you have already accrued
3. What the money would earn elsewhere over the term you have left

If the emergency is real, the arithmetic is a footnote — take the money. If you are moving it because a better rate appeared, the payback period has to fit inside the time you plan to leave it there. Anything shorter than that and you paid to stand still.
