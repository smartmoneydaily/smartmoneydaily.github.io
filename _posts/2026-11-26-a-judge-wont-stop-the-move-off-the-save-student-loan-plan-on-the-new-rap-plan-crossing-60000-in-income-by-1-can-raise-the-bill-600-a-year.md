On Nov. 18, Judge Loren L. AliKhan of the federal district court in Washington, D.C., refused to stop the Education Department from moving borrowers off the SAVE repayment plan. The four borrowers who brought the case, Havens v. U.S. Department of Education, argued that when a Missouri court threw out the SAVE rule in March, the older REPAYE plan came back into force, so nobody should be pushed into something else. The judge didn't decide that question. She ruled that they hadn't shown the kind of harm that justifies a preliminary injunction, and the lawsuit goes on.

For the more than 7.5 million borrowers the department says were enrolled in SAVE, the move stays on its current track. People who haven't picked a new plan are sitting in a forbearance, getting a 90-day notice and then a 30-day "Final Notice." The final notices filed in the case say a borrower who doesn't pick a plan will be put on the Standard Repayment Plan. Anyone who wants a payment based on income will end up comparing the new Repayment Assistance Plan (RAP) with Income-Based Repayment (IBR), and the two treat income very differently. RAP takes a flat percentage of your entire adjusted gross income, the percentage steps up at each $10,000 line from $20,000 through $100,000, and taxable interest from savings counts toward that AGI.

## Why the court let the transfers continue

The [25-page opinion](https://storage.courtlistener.com/recap/gov.uscourts.dcd.290158/gov.uscourts.dcd.290158.36.0.pdf) lays out how SAVE ended. The department settled with Missouri and the other states that had sued, and on March 10, 2026 the Eastern District of Missouri vacated the 2023 rule that created SAVE, except for one provision about counting certain deferments and forbearances toward forgiveness. SAVE enrollees have stayed in the administrative forbearance that has held their loans since July 2024. The plaintiffs say REPAYE, the 2015 plan that SAVE had amended and renamed, came back with that vacatur and that refusing to run it violates the Administrative Procedure Act.

Judge AliKhan didn't rule on that claim. She found that two plaintiffs lacked standing on their tax argument, because restoring REPAYE wouldn't make their discharges tax-free; neither had applied to another income-driven plan by Dec. 31, 2025, the cutoff in a separate court order that let some SAVE borrowers' discharges be dated back. The other harms they described weren't irreparable, she wrote: monthly payments $14 and $49 higher for two of them, a lost interest subsidy, and months that wouldn't count toward forgiveness. The department told the court it could refund overpayments or apply them as credit if the borrowers eventually win. A separate ruling on the department's motion to dismiss is still to come.

## After the 90 days comes a final notice

The department laid out the process in a [March 27 announcement](https://www.ed.gov/about/news/press-release/us-department-of-education-announces-next-steps-borrowers-enrolled-unlawful-save-plan). Borrowers would get at least 90 days to enter a new plan, with servicers sending notices starting July 1, and anyone who didn't act would be enrolled in either the Standard Repayment Plan or the new Tiered Standard Plan. Each servicer's notice sets that borrower's own deadline.

The plaintiffs' Nov. 9 filing in the Havens case shows what came after the 90 days. Starting around Sept. 30, servicers sent a second letter titled "Final Notice," saying the borrower would be placed in a repayment plan in 30 days and, without a new application, on the Standard Repayment Plan. One plaintiff's 90-day period ended Oct. 12 and her final notice was sent Oct. 19, so her 30 days ran out on Nov. 18. That was the day of the ruling, and the filing says no plaintiff had been given an earlier date. None of the three plaintiffs still in SAVE had been moved when the filing went in. Asked by the plaintiffs' counsel when two of the three would be moved, the department's lawyer responded that he did "not know when borrowers whose 30-day period has expired will be moved to new plans." He also said borrowers who'd rather not make payments under a new plan could request a forbearance.

The standard plan's fixed payment comes from the balance and the interest rates, not from income. For loans made before July 1, 2026, the regulation at 34 C.F.R. 685.208 has a Direct Loan repaid within 10 years of entering repayment, not counting deferment and forbearance, while most consolidation loans get 10 to 30 years depending on the total balance. The Tiered Standard Plan, which applies to borrowers with a Direct Loan made on or after July 1, 2026, uses 10, 15, 20 or 25 years based on the balance. Landing on a standard plan isn't permanent: for those older loans, 34 C.F.R. 685.210 lets an eligible borrower change to an income-driven plan at any time, and the March announcement says a borrower can contact the servicer to enroll in another plan without waiting for a notice. Giving the department consent to pull federal tax information from the IRS speeds up an income-driven application.

## RAP charges a percentage of your whole income

RAP opened July 1, and the court noted it's available to qualifying borrowers no matter when they took out their loans. The formula is in [20 U.S.C. 1087e(q)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/20/1087e). It starts with the adjusted gross income from your most recent tax year. If you filed jointly, that's the joint AGI; if you're married and file separately, only yours counts. When both spouses on a joint return have eligible loans, the payment is split between them in proportion to their balances. At $10,000 or less, the base payment is $120 a year. Above that, it's 1% of AGI up to $20,000, 2% up to $30,000, and one more point for each $10,000 after that, reaching 9% above $90,000 through $100,000 and 10% above $100,000. Divide by 12, subtract $50 a month for each qualifying dependent claimed on that tax return, and that's the payment, with a $10 minimum.

The rate applies to all of the income, not just the part above a line. At an AGI of exactly $60,000, the base payment is 5%, or $3,000 a year ($250 a month). At $60,001 it's 6%, or $3,600.06, about $300 a month. IBR doesn't jump like that. It takes 10% or 15% of your income above 150% of the federal poverty guideline for your family size. As long as that works out to more than $10 a month and stays below IBR's cap, another dollar of income adds 10 or 15 cents a year.

RAP's interest rule is also broader. When you make the full required payment on time and it doesn't cover that month's interest, the unpaid interest isn't charged, and if the payment cuts principal by less than $50, the department reduces principal so the total drop is $50 or what you paid, whichever is less. IBR covers unpaid interest only on subsidized loans, and only for the first three years. RAP takes longer to reach forgiveness, though: 360 qualifying payments, the equivalent of 30 years, which can include qualifying payments made under earlier plans. IBR forgives after 25 years, or 20 for "new borrowers," who also pay 10% instead of 15%. Under the regulations, that generally means you had no Direct or FFEL loan balance when you borrowed on or after July 1, 2014. Under current federal law, a balance forgiven under an income-driven plan is generally taxable again. The court noted that the temporary exclusion for those discharges expired at the end of 2025.

Under the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, the 2025 law that created RAP, IBR no longer requires a "partial financial hardship" to sign up, but the payment can't go above what a 10-year standard plan would have been when you entered IBR. Someone on IBR can switch to RAP or the standard plan. Taking out a new Direct Loan on or after July 1, 2026, a consolidation loan included, narrows the choice to RAP or the Tiered Standard Plan, and some loans tied to Parent PLUS borrowing can't go into RAP at all. PAYE and the original ICR plan are still open to otherwise qualifying borrowers who haven't received a Direct Loan on or after July 1, 2026, until both close on June 30, 2028.

## RAP and IBR for three households

These are my numbers from the two statutes, using the 2026 HHS poverty guidelines for the 48 contiguous states and D.C.: $15,960 for one person and $21,640 for two (Alaska and Hawaii are higher). They leave out the IBR cap and any rounding by a servicer. The repayment calculator on StudentAid.gov works from your actual loans. The single-parent row assumes one qualifying dependent claimed for RAP and a family size of two for IBR.

| Household and AGI | RAP per month | IBR at 10% | IBR at 15% |
|---|---|---|---|
| Single, $45,000 | $150.00 | $175.50 | $263.25 |
| Single, $75,000 | $437.50 | $425.50 | $638.25 |
| Single parent, one child, $45,000 | $100.00 | $104.50 | $156.75 |

The 10% column is for new borrowers as defined above; everyone else on IBR is in the 15% column, where RAP comes out lower in all three rows. In both $45,000 rows, RAP is lower than either IBR figure. At $75,000, the 7% bracket puts RAP $12 above IBR at 10%. Over a long repayment, the bigger difference may be interest, since IBR lets uncovered interest keep adding up outside its three-year subsidy on subsidized loans and RAP drops it for each month the full payment arrives on time.

## Where savings interest meets RAP's $10,000 lines

AGI is the line on your federal return, and taxable interest is in it: bank interest, CD interest and Treasury bill interest, which is free of state income tax but not federal. For RAP, a savings account's interest counts the same way a paycheck does.

Say your AGI would be $59,700 without interest and you earn $400 on your savings over the year, which is what $10,000 at 4% pays. That makes AGI $60,100, in the 6% bracket. The RAP base payment becomes $3,606 instead of $2,985, which is $621 a year more, or about $52 a month, for $400 of interest. For a borrower below IBR's cap at 10%, the same $400 adds $40 a year.

An application filed in November 2026 generally uses your 2025 return, so interest earned in 2026 affects the payment once your 2026 return is the one used for an application or recertification. Interest on a Treasury bill held to maturity is generally taxed in the year it matures, which this [post on T-bill and short CD timing](/2026/10/16/t-bills-and-short-cds-bought-now-which-tax-year-gets-the-interest/) goes through. If the AGI on your last return doesn't reasonably reflect what you earn now, such as after a drop in pay, RAP's statute provides for other documentation of current income instead.

Deductions cut the other way. If you're eligible and under the limits, a deductible traditional IRA contribution or a personal HSA contribution lowers AGI, and contributions designated for 2026 can generally be made until April 15, 2027, with no extra time for a filing extension. In the example above, a $500 deductible IRA contribution for 2026 would bring AGI back to $59,600 and the base payment down to $2,980. The [2027 IRA and 401(k) limits post](/2026/11/16/the-ira-limit-stays-at-7500-for-2027-while-the-401k-limit-rises-1000-to-25500/) has the 2026 and 2027 income ranges for deducting a traditional IRA contribution.

## What the months in forbearance cost

Waiting in the SAVE forbearance doesn't stop what you owe from growing. The department restarted interest on SAVE loans on Aug. 1, 2025. When it announced that, Secretary Linda McMahon said borrowers in SAVE "cannot make progress toward loan discharge programs authorized by Congress." A $30,000 balance at 5.5% builds about $137.50 of interest a month, roughly $2,200 from August 2025 through November 2026 if nothing is paid. RAP's interest break only covers months with an on-time RAP payment, so it doesn't reach back over those. For someone whose RAP payment would be smaller than the monthly interest, every month in forbearance adds the full month's interest to what's owed, while a month on RAP with the full payment made on time adds none of it.

Leaving SAVE hasn't been smooth for everyone. One plaintiff who moved from SAVE to IBR got a schedule of $0 payments, then a bill for $330.01, then a statement showing $660.02 due with a warning about reporting to credit bureaus, according to the plaintiffs' Nov. 9 filing, which says neither the servicer nor the department has explained the mismatch.

## What I'd do before a final notice runs out

If I had a SAVE loan and a final notice in the drawer, I wouldn't wait for the servicer to put me on the standard plan. I'd run my last return's AGI through RAP and IBR to see what each would charge now, then look at where my 2026 income, savings interest included, sits against RAP's $10,000 lines while there's still time to make a deductible contribution for 2026. Then I'd keep at least the first month's new payment in an account I can reach the day the bill posts, and read each statement until the amount matches what the application said.
