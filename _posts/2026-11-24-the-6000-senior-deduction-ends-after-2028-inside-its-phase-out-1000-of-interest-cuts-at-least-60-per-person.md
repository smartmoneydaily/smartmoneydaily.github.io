Section 151(d)(5)(C) of the tax code gives people 65 and older an extra $6,000 deduction, and it also says when that stops. The deduction applies "in the case of a taxable year beginning before January 1, 2029," which covers the 2025, 2026, 2027 and 2028 returns. The 2028 return, filed in 2029, is the last one that can claim it unless Congress changes the law.

Until then, the deduction has an income test that most coverage mentions in one line and moves past. Above $75,000 of modified adjusted gross income, or $150,000 on a joint return, the $6,000 shrinks by 6% of the excess. Interest from savings accounts, CDs and Treasury bills is part of that income. When an extra $1,000 of it lands entirely inside the phase-out range, it takes $60 off the deduction for each qualifying person: $60 for a single filer, $120 for a married couple who both qualify. It can take more if the interest also makes more of your Social Security taxable, since that raises the same income figure.

## Who can claim it, and for which years

The statute gives $6,000 "for each qualified individual," meaning a taxpayer who has turned 65 before the end of the tax year and, on a joint return, a spouse who has too. The IRS treats a person as reaching 65 on the day before the 65th birthday, so the 2025 form asks whether you were born before January 2, 1961. The same rule moves that date to January 2, 1962 for 2026 returns.

A few conditions decide whether it's available at all:

- Married taxpayers have to file a joint return. Married filing separately rules it out.
- Each person claimed needs a valid Social Security number on the return. For 2025 returns, the IRS instructions define that as one valid for employment and issued before the return's due date, including extensions.
- You can claim it whether you itemize or take the standard deduction. If you take the standard deduction, you also keep the older age-based addition to it. Under Rev. Proc. 2025-32, that addition is $1,650 per qualifying person for 2026, or $2,050 for someone who is unmarried and not a surviving spouse.

The $6,000 and the $75,000 and $150,000 lines are fixed dollar amounts in the statute. Nothing in the provision adjusts them for inflation, so they'll be the same on the 2028 return as on the 2025 one.

## How the reduction is figured, line by line

The calculation is on Part V of [Schedule 1-A](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/f1040s1a.pdf); the line numbers here are from the 2025 form. Line 31 is your MAGI. Line 32 is $75,000, or $150,000 if married filing jointly. Line 33 is the difference, and if that's zero or less you simply enter $6,000 on line 35. Otherwise line 34 multiplies the difference by 6%, and line 35 subtracts that from $6,000, stopping at zero. Then lines 36a and 36b each take the line 35 amount, one for you and one for a qualifying spouse, and line 37 adds them.

That structure matters for couples. The IRS instructions say "the $6,000 per person amount is reduced," so on a joint return where both spouses are 65 or older, both $6,000 amounts shrink at the same time. The couple loses 12 cents of deduction for every dollar over $150,000, and the deduction is gone at $250,000. A single filer loses 6 cents per dollar over $75,000 and reaches zero at $175,000. A joint return with only one spouse 65 or older loses 6 cents per dollar and also hits zero at $250,000.

Interest doesn't reduce the deduction as long as MAGI stays at or below the threshold. Past the point where it's fully phased out, it doesn't either, because there's nothing left to lose. The squeeze only happens inside those bands, and a single year's interest can be what carries you across the starting line.

## The income that counts

Part I of Schedule 1-A builds MAGI from your adjusted gross income on Form 1040, line 11b, then adds back excluded income from Puerto Rico, the foreign earned income and housing exclusions from Form 2555, and the American Samoa exclusion from Form 4563. For most retirees living in the U.S., MAGI here is simply AGI.

So everything already in AGI counts. That includes interest from savings and money market accounts, CD interest, Treasury bill interest (exempt from state tax, but fully in federal AGI), I bond interest in the year you report it, taxable IRA withdrawals, capital gains and the taxable part of Social Security benefits.

Tax-exempt municipal bond interest is the notable exception. It isn't in AGI, and Schedule 1-A doesn't add it back. That's different from the test for taxing Social Security benefits, where [26 U.S.C. 86](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/86) increases income by "the amount of interest received or accrued by the taxpayer during the taxable year which is exempt from tax." The same muni interest can count for one test and not the other on the same return. It can still raise this MAGI indirectly, though: if it makes more of your Social Security taxable, that extra taxable benefit is in AGI.

## Two savers, worked through with 2026 figures

Both examples use the 2026 standard deduction and brackets from Rev. Proc. 2025-32. To keep them clean, I'm assuming MAGI equals AGI, the income is all taxed at ordinary rates, no other deductions or credits move with income, and 85% of each household's Social Security benefits, the most that can be taxed, is already included in income before the interest. If that last part isn't true for you, the interest can cost more because it makes more of the benefit taxable, and the [Social Security worksheet post](/2026/10/11/does-savings-interest-make-your-social-security-taxable/) shows how.

A single 67-year-old has $90,000 of MAGI, and $6,000 of it is interest, about what $150,000 earns at a hypothetical 4%. Without the interest, MAGI would be $84,000, the reduction would be 6% of $9,000, or $540, and the deduction would be $5,460. With it, the reduction is 6% of $15,000, or $900, leaving $5,100. Taxable income works out to $90,000 minus the $16,100 standard deduction, the $2,050 age add-on and the $5,100, which is $66,750, inside the 22% bracket that runs from $50,400 to $105,700. The interest adds $6,000 to taxable income directly and another $360 through the smaller deduction. At 22%, that's $1,399.20 of tax on $6,000 of interest, an effective 23.32%.

A married couple, both 66, has $190,000 of MAGI, including $8,000 of interest on $200,000 at the same hypothetical 4%. Without the interest, MAGI is $182,000 and each spouse's $6,000 drops by 6% of $32,000, or $1,920, so they'd claim $8,160 together. With the interest, each drops by 6% of $40,000, or $2,400, for $7,200 together. Taxable income is $190,000 minus $32,200, minus $3,300 for two age add-ons, minus $7,200, or $147,300, which is in the joint 22% bracket ($100,800 to $211,400). The interest raises taxable income by $8,960, and 22% of that is $1,971.20, an effective 24.64% on $8,000.

Neither household is anywhere near the 3.8% [net investment income tax](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/1411), which starts at $200,000 of MAGI for single filers and $250,000 for joint filers, so that tax doesn't enter these numbers.

## When interest lands in a tax year

Since the test runs one year at a time, timing decides which year's deduction a dollar of interest reduces. IRS Publication 550 treats interest on a bank deposit as received once it's "credited to your account and subject to your withdrawal," so savings and money market interest can't be pushed to a later year. For a CD that matures in a year or less and pays at maturity, you generally include the interest when you receive it or are entitled to receive it without a substantial penalty. For a CD that defers interest for more than a year, the original issue discount rules spread it across the years, which [the five-year CD tax post](/2026/09/25/why-you-owe-tax-on-a-5-year-cd-you-cannot-touch-yet/) covers. Treasury bill interest is generally reported "when the bill is paid at maturity."

That gives the end of 2028 an odd feature. A newly issued 26-week bill bought in the fall of 2028 matures in 2029, and a cash-method taxpayer who holds it to maturity generally reports its interest in 2029, a year with no senior deduction to shrink under current law. For someone inside the phase-out band in 2028, that keeps more of the 2028 deduction. It doesn't keep the deduction itself, which is gone for everyone in 2029, and the interest is still taxed at the bracket rate when it arrives. The [T-bill and short CD timing post](/2026/10/16/t-bills-and-short-cds-bought-now-which-tax-year-gets-the-interest/) has the year-end mechanics.

## 2027's numbers, and the ones that stay put

The 2027 inflation adjustments in Rev. Proc. 2026-35 raise the regular standard deduction to $16,600 for single filers and $33,200 for joint filers, and the older age-based addition to $1,700 per qualifying person, or $2,100 for someone unmarried and not a surviving spouse. The senior deduction itself stays at $6,000 with the same $75,000 and $150,000 lines, since the statute doesn't index them. If your interest income goes up along with CD rates, nothing in the provision moves the $75,000 line with it. The [2027 bracket post](/2026/11/11/2027-tax-brackets-and-savings-interest-12-up-to-104050-for-couples-and-a-2800-kiddie-tax-threshold/) has the rest of next year's numbers.

## A check before December 31

If your 2026 MAGI is likely to land between $75,000 and $175,000 single, or $150,000 and $250,000 married filing jointly, I'd add up all the interest that's taxable for 2026, including OID that accrues on longer CDs even if it hasn't been paid, and see which band you're in. Under the same assumptions as the examples, and as long as the whole $1,000 stays inside the phase-out range and the 22% bracket, each $1,000 of interest costs $13.20 more than the bracket alone suggests per qualifying person, or $26.40 for a couple who both qualify.

I don't think that's a reason to take on more risk with money meant to be safe, and it's small next to the interest itself. It is a reason to look at where the interest lands and in which year, especially from 2026 through 2028. After the 2028 return, the law as it reads today has no $6,000 left to claim.
