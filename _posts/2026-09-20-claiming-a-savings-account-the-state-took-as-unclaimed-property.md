The search form asks for a last name and a state. What can come back is your own name, an address you moved out of a decade ago, the name of a bank that has since been absorbed twice, and a dollar figure. A savings account opened for a car you ended up not buying, never closed, never touched, with the statements still going to an apartment you no longer rent.

The money is not gone. It is sitting with a state treasurer or comptroller, and it stays claimable.

No federal statute sets the dormancy clock on an ordinary bank account. State law does, and the period commonly runs three to five years with no owner-initiated contact. Before the bank hands the balance over, it generally has to write to the last address in its file. After the transfer the state holds the money, in some states indefinitely, and the claim itself is free. What the years cost is yield, plus whatever dormancy fee the account contract allowed on the way out. The tax answer splits by account type: interest the bank credited was taxable in the years it was credited, and an escheated IRA is treated as a distribution, with withholding and a Form 1099-R.

## Who sets the dormancy clock

Deposit insurance is federal. Interest disclosure is federal. Abandonment is not. The [FDIC](https://www.fdic.gov/)'s consumer article on [finding a long lost bank account](https://www.fdic.gov/consumer-resource-center/2020-12/how-find-long-lost-bank-account-or-safe-deposit-box) puts the division plainly: "Every state has laws requiring financial institutions to turn over abandoned property after a certain amount of time, usually between three to five years." The same article defines the transfer itself: "Escheatment is the transfer of unclaimed funds or property to a state government."

The Office of the Comptroller of the Currency's consumer site says the same thing from the regulator's side. Its page on [state unclaimed-property programs](https://www.helpwithmybank.gov/help-topics/bank-accounts/opening-closing-inactive-bank-accounts/inactive-accounts/inactive-unclaimed-programs.html) states that "Every state, plus the District of Columbia, has unclaimed-property programs," and that "Most state programs look at whether there has been any customer-initiated activity or contact within the last three to five years." Then the mechanics: "The bank turns over the account balance to the state if there is no contact from the account holder. This is known as escheatment."

The ranges are wide because the statutes differ. The [National Association of Unclaimed Property Administrators](https://unclaimed.org/), a network of the National Association of State Treasurers, [defines](https://unclaimed.org/what-is-unclaimed-property/) unclaimed property as "property or accounts within financial institutions or companies—in which there has been no activity generated (or contact with the owner) regarding the property for one year or a longer period." The [Missouri State Treasurer](https://treasurer.mo.gov/UCP/FAQ.aspx) describes abandoned property as "property for which there have been no documented transactions or contact with the owners for some statutorily defined period of time, generally five years."

One federal escheat rule does exist for deposits, and it applies only when the bank itself fails. That clock is shorter and its ending is different, and it is worth knowing separately.

## What counts as contact

The phrase the OCC uses is "customer-initiated activity or contact." That is the whole test, and the word doing the work is *customer-initiated*. Interest posting to the account is the bank's action. A statement being generated is the bank's action.

Whether an automatic transfer counts is a state-law question, and states have been changing the answer. Washington rewrote its statute effective January 1, 2023, and the Department of Revenue's [holder Q&A on the Revised Uniform Unclaimed Property Act](https://dor.wa.gov/sites/default/files/2022-09/RUUPAQA.pdf) records the change in one line: "The new law redefines positive owner contact to now include automatic deposits and withdrawals." Under the old Washington rule a standing monthly transfer did not stop the clock. Under the new one it does. Neither answer travels to another state.

That makes the safe version of the rule narrow. Something you actually did, recorded in the account, restarts the period: a sign-in the bank records as owner contact, a deposit or withdrawal you initiated, a written response to a notice, an address change. The rest is guesswork that depends on which statute governs. The broader picture of how an account slides into dormancy is in [How a Savings Account Goes Dormant and Ends Up With the State](/2026/09/04/how-a-savings-account-goes-dormant-and-ends-up-with-the-state/).

## The letter before the transfer

No state simply takes the balance on the anniversary. Holders owe a search first. NAUPA's [reporting overview](https://unclaimed.org/reporting-overview/) states the duty: "Organizations must make a good-faith effort to reach the owner of the property before turning it over to the state for safekeeping. Attempts may be made by letters, phone calls, electronic communications, or other methods."

The OCC describes what banks actually do: "Before sending the account balance to the state, the bank is usually required to try to notify the customer. Some banks publish the names of the account holders in a local newspaper. Some banks send a letter to the last known address of the account holder."

Washington's rules show how specific this gets. Due diligence letters "must be sent by first class mail to the apparent owners," and the timing is bracketed on both sides: "You must wait at least 60 days after you send the letters to the owners before you send in your report. You cannot wait any longer than 180 days from the time you send the letters to the owners." There is a floor on which accounts get one — "Due diligence letters need to be sent for any property $75 and over" — and an email rule that only supplements the mail: if an owner "has consented to receive email delivery from the holder, the holder shall send the due diligence letter both by first-class United States mail to the apparent owner's last known mailing address and by email, unless the holder believes that the apparent owner's email address is invalid." Washington reports are due by October 31.

The weak point is the same one every time: *last known mailing address*. A letter with a real legal obligation behind it goes to an apartment three moves ago, comes back or does not, and the transfer proceeds. Small balances in some states fall under the threshold and get no letter at all.

## Interest and fees while the account sits still

An account nobody touches still has to be paid on. The [CFPB](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/)'s [Truth in Savings examination procedures](https://files.consumerfinance.gov/f/documents/102012_cfpb_truth-savings-act-tisa_procedures.pdf) restate the Regulation DD staff commentary at 12 CFR 1030.7(a)(1)-6 under the heading "Dormant Accounts": "Institutions must pay interest on funds in an account, even if inactivity or the infrequency of transactions would permit the institution to consider the account to be 'inactive' or 'dormant' (or similar status) as defined by state, other laws, or the account contract."

Fees are the other direction, and state law lets them through with conditions. Washington's answer to whether holders may still charge dormancy fees: "Yes, there are certain requirements to do so. An enforceable contract must exist, the holder must regularly impose and not regularly waive or reverse the fees, and the charges must not be unconscionable." Three conditions, all of them about the bank's own consistency rather than the amount.

There is a quirk worth knowing before opening any account. The same CFPB procedures list the fees that make an advertisement's claim of "free" or "no cost" improper under 12 CFR 1030.8(a), and then list what does not count. On that second list: "Fees assessed against a dormant account." An account advertised as free can carry a dormancy fee, because a dormancy fee is not a maintenance or activity fee. Which lines of a disclosure actually bind the bank is covered in [Six Lines in a Bank Disclosure That Decide What You Earn](/2026/08/24/six-lines-in-a-bank-disclosure-that-decide-what-you-earn/).

The bank may also simply end the relationship. The CFPB's answer on [involuntary account closure](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/the-bankcredit-union-closed-my-checking-account-even-though-i-did-not-want-them-to-can-the-bankcredit-union-do-that-en-959/) says "Yes, a bank or credit union can close your account without your permission," and that institutions "may also close dormant accounts for which there has been no activity for a substantial period of time (generally years)." Notice before closing is again a state question: "Some states may require your bank or credit union to give you notice before it closes your account."

## Which state ends up with the money

Not necessarily the one you live in now. The address in the bank's file decides, which is why people find money in states they left a decade ago.

Where a file holds more than one address, Washington's revised act sorts them: "if there are multiple addresses for an owner, the state with the most recent address may take custody of the property. If the most recent address is temporary, the state with the next most non-temporary address may take custody." A college address or a short sublet can be treated as temporary and skipped.

Federal law uses the same logic in the one place it governs escheat directly. Under [12 U.S.C. 1822(e)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-2023-title12/html/USCODE-2023-title12-chap16-sec1822.htm), the "appropriate State" for an unclaimed deposit from a failed bank is the state the [FDIC](https://www.fdic.gov/resources/deposit-insurance)'s notice was mailed to, and if the notice did not go to an address within a state, it is the state where the failed institution had its main office.

The practical consequence for searching: check every state you have lived in, not just the current one, and check the state where the bank was headquartered. [NAUPA](https://unclaimed.org/what-is-unclaimed-property/) links out to each state program and says of the search itself that "It's free to search for yours."

## After the state has it

The transfer is custody, not forfeiture. Missouri's treasurer states the holding period bluntly: "Unclaimed Property holds cash property in trust forever," and proceeds, "as well as any other Unclaimed Property, are held forever until claimed by the original owner or heir." Not every state words it that way, but the reason a decade-old account still shows up in a search is this.

Claims cost nothing. Missouri: "Claims processing is a public service provided by the Missouri State Treasurer at no cost to the owner." The [Illinois Treasurer](https://www.illinoistreasurer.gov/Individuals/Unclaimed_Property) says its office is there "to help you search for your money and claim it at no cost," and describes the contents of its file the way most states do — "forgotten bank accounts, uncashed checks, insurance payments, utility refunds, safe-deposit box contents, and other funds turned over to the state." No finder, locator, or service is required to file.

What a claim asks for is proof that you are the person named. Missouri's process is typical: many owners can file online, and for the rest "we will send you a claim form with instructions regarding the necessary documentation to establish rightful ownership in order to claim the property." Identity documents and something tying you to the old address are the usual core of that.

Some states add interest, and the terms are narrow. Missouri again: "If the property was reported to the Treasurer as an interest-bearing account, the Treasurer even pays the owner up to seven years interest on the cash property." That depends on the holder having coded it as interest-bearing, and on the state having such a rule at all.

Safe deposit boxes are the exception to "nothing is lost." Washington requires banks to "hold contents for 180 days after the report is filed before sending the contents." After that the contents go to the state, and Missouri describes the ending: the treasurer "is required by law to auction abandoned non-monetary assets of value (jewelry, coins, etc.) from safe deposit boxes, and records the proceeds from their sale in the owner's names so they or their heirs can claim the proceeds." The jewelry is sold. The proceeds wait.

## Failed banks run on a federal clock

When the bank itself fails, state dormancy law is not what moves the money, and the ending is genuinely final. [12 U.S.C. 1822(e)](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-2023-title12/html/USCODE-2023-title12-chap16-sec1822.htm) sets a short sequence.

Two notices come first. A written notice goes out "within 30 days after the initiation of the payment of insured deposits," telling insured depositors to claim from the FDIC or from the institution that took over the deposits, and "A second notice containing this information shall be mailed by the Corporation to all insured depositors who have not responded to the first notice, 15 months after the Corporation initiates such payment of insured depositors." Both go to "the last known address of the depositor appearing on the records of the insured depository institution in default."

At 18 months the deposit leaves: "the Corporation shall deliver the deposit to the custody of the appropriate State as unclaimed property, unless the appropriate State declines to accept custody." If the state declines, "the deposit shall not be delivered to any State," and the claim has to be made to the FDIC before the receivership ends.

Then comes the part with no counterpart in state law. "If a depositor does not claim the deposit delivered to the custody of the appropriate State pursuant to paragraph (2)(B) within 10 years of the date of delivery, the deposit shall be immediately refunded to the Corporation and become its property." Rights against the state are barred as of that refund. Ten years of state custody, and then the money is the FDIC's. Deposits at a failed bank sit under the FDIC's [unclaimed funds search](https://closedbanks.fdic.gov/funds/), which covers "Unclaimed insured deposits up to the insurance limit" along with dividends and creditor claims. Confirming an institution is insured in the first place is covered in [How to Verify a Bank Is Actually FDIC Insured Before You Transfer](/2026/09/09/how-to-verify-a-bank-is-actually-fdic-insured-before-you-transfer/).

## The tax side of getting it back

Interest the bank credited along the way was taxable in the years it was credited. [IRS Publication 550](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p550) states the rule as "any interest that you receive or that is credited to your account and can be withdrawn is taxable income," and ties the timing to availability rather than to withdrawal: you are treated as receiving interest on a bank deposit when it is "credited to your account and subject to your withdrawal." For the years no form arrives, Pub 550 adds the backstop: "You must report all your interest income even if you do not receive a Form 1099-INT."

A retirement account works differently, and the guidance is explicit. In [Rev. Rul. 2018-17](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-drop/rr-18-17.pdf) the IRS worked through a traditional IRA worth $1,000 that state law required the trustee to pay to the state's unclaimed property fund. Because a payment from a non-Roth IRA "is treated as includible in gross income," it is a designated distribution, and for a nonperiodic distribution section 3405(b)(1) "provides that the payor shall withhold from such distribution an amount equal to 10 percent of the distribution." The trustee sends $900 to the state and $100 to the IRS and reports the full $1,000 on a Form 1099-R. The holdings: "the payment by Trustee Y of Individual C's interest in IRA O to the State J unclaimed property fund, as required by State J law, is subject to federal income tax withholding under § 3405," and "is subject to reporting under § 408(i)."

[Rev. Rul. 2020-24](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-drop/rr-20-24.pdf) applied the same result to employer plans: the payment of an accrued benefit to a state unclaimed property fund "is subject to federal income tax withholding under § 3405" and "is subject to reporting under § 6047." The [Instructions for Forms 1099-R and 5498](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i1099r) carry both rules and the dates: "Payments made from IRAs to state unclaimed property funds must be reported on Form 1099-R," and "Payments made from qualified plans on or after January 1, 2022, to state unclaimed property funds must be reported on Form 1099-R."

There is a repair route, and it is the reason to check for escheated retirement money early. [Rev. Proc. 2020-46](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-drop/rp-20-46.pdf) added a reason to the list a taxpayer may self-certify for a waiver of the 60-day rollover deadline. Item (l) on that list: "the distribution was made to a state unclaimed property fund." The contribution then has to go into the plan or IRA "as soon as practicable after the reason or reasons listed in the preceding paragraph no longer prevent the taxpayer from rolling over the amount distributed," with a safe harbor: the requirement "is deemed to be satisfied if the contribution is made within 30 days after the reason or reasons no longer prevent the taxpayer from making the contribution." Model language sits in the revenue procedure's appendix. How IRA CDs interact with rollover deadlines is covered in [IRA CDs: Insurance, Rollovers, and the RMD Deadline](/2026/09/12/ira-cds-insurance-rollovers-and-the-rmd-deadline/).

| Stage | What governs it | What it means for you |
|---|---|---|
| Account goes quiet | State dormancy period, commonly three to five years | Only owner-initiated contact is reliable |
| Due diligence letter | State statute, e.g. 60 to 180 days before the report in Washington | Sent to the last known address in the bank's file |
| Interest during dormancy | 12 CFR 1030.7(a)(1)-6 | Interest keeps being credited |
| Dormancy fees | State law plus the account contract | Allowed where the contract supports it |
| Transfer to the state | State unclaimed property act | Custody, and free to claim |
| Failed bank instead | 12 U.S.C. 1822(e) | 18 months out, 10 years of state custody, then the FDIC's |
| Interest credited | IRS Pub 550 | Reportable in the year credited, form or no form |
| Retirement account | Rev. Rul. 2018-17 and 2020-24 | Withholding and a Form 1099-R |

## Common questions

**Does interest keep accruing after the state takes the money?**
Not automatically. The bank's duty under Regulation DD applies while the funds are in the account. After the transfer it depends on the state: Missouri pays "up to seven years interest on the cash property" when the holder reported it as an interest-bearing account, and other states take other approaches. Ask the program handling the claim rather than assuming.

**Can a claim expire?**
For an ordinary escheated deposit, generally no. Missouri holds cash property "in trust forever," and states with that posture are why decades-old accounts still turn up in searches. The exception is a failed bank, where 12 U.S.C. 1822(e) refunds an unclaimed deposit to the FDIC after 10 years of state custody and bars the depositor's rights against the state.

**Is it worth paying a company that offers to recover the money?**
The search and the claim are already free. NAUPA's own framing is that it is free to search, Illinois says it will help "you search for your money and claim it at no cost," and Missouri calls claims processing "a public service provided by the Missouri State Treasurer at no cost to the owner." Start at the state program named in the search result.

**What if the account owner has died?**
State programs pay heirs as well as owners. Missouri records auction proceeds "in the owner's names so they or their heirs can claim the proceeds," and the claim then turns on documenting the relationship and the estate. What happens to a deposit account at the moment of death is a separate question, covered in [What Happens to a Savings Account or CD When the Owner Dies](/2026/09/11/what-happens-to-a-savings-account-or-cd-when-the-owner-dies/).
