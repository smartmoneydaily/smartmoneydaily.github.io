An extension filer who leaves the direct deposit lines blank on a 2025 return, or copies an account number with one digit wrong, is no longer choosing a paper check by default; that filer is likely setting up a hold. The IRS [generally stopped issuing paper refund checks for individual taxpayers after Sept. 30, 2025](https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/questions-and-answers-about-executive-order-14247-modernizing-payments-to-and-from-americas-bank-account), and a return that shows a refund but carries missing, invalid or rejected bank information now produces a CP53E notice instead of a check. The notice gives the filer 30 days to add a bank account or select an exception condition, and if nothing comes back and there are no other issues with the return, the IRS says the refund will go out as a paper check after 6 weeks. With the extension deadline on Thursday, Oct. 15, 2026, the practical answer is short: put correct, verified account details on the return before it goes in, and if part of the refund belongs in savings, split it with Form 8888 on the same filing so no second step is needed.

The guidance appears on four IRS pages, and each one answers a different question. The [questions and answers on Executive Order 14247](https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/questions-and-answers-about-executive-order-14247-modernizing-payments-to-and-from-americas-bank-account) explain why the paper check went away and what replaces it. The [Understanding your CP53E notice](https://www.irs.gov/individuals/understanding-your-cp53e-notice) page explains the notice itself and how to answer it. [IRS Tax Tip 2026-63, The top 5 tips for extension filers](https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/the-top-5-tips-for-extension-filers), dated Aug. 18, 2026, covers the October deadline. [About Form 8888, Allocation of Refund](https://www.irs.gov/forms-pubs/about-form-8888) covers sending a refund to more than one account. They describe general rules, not the outcome on any particular return.

## What changed after Sept. 30, 2025

The change traces to Executive Order 14247, titled "Modernizing Payments To and From America's Bank Account." The IRS summarizes its effect on individual refunds in a single sentence: the agency "generally stopped issuing paper refund checks for individual taxpayers after Sept. 30, 2025." The updated version of those questions and answers was released to the public in FS-2026-02 on Jan. 27, 2026. The page itself says these FAQs give general information, may be updated or modified, and that later updates will be dated, so the version on IRS.gov is the one to check before filing.

Two words in that sentence carry weight. "Generally" signals that the paper check did not disappear completely. The same Q&A says that "Limited exceptions to the paper check phase-out will also be established." And "individual taxpayers" tells you whose refunds the sentence is about: individual filers, a group that includes everyone sitting on an individual extension right now.

For someone who files a 2025 return between now and Oct. 15, 2026, the filing comes after Sept. 30, 2025, so a filer who skips the direct deposit section out of habit ends up in the new process on this return.

The Q&A also describes what is supposed to replace a check for people who do not have a traditional bank account: "Alternative electronic payment methods, including payments via certain mobile apps and prepaid debit cards, will be available to serve these individuals." The word "certain" is doing work there too. The page does not promise that every app or every prepaid card will qualify, and it does not list which ones do. That gap matters for anyone planning to use one.

## What sends a return into a CP53E

The CP53E page names two situations that produce the notice. The first is the one most filers will recognize: "Your tax return shows a refund, but the bank information is invalid, missing, or rejected." The second catches people off guard: "A return with a balance due is corrected by the IRS and now results in a refund."

The first trigger contains three separate failures, and the IRS page does not define each one. Read plainly:

- **Missing** is the simplest. The refund section of the return asks for a routing number, an account number and whether the account is checking or savings. If those lines are blank, there is nowhere to send the money.
- **Invalid** reads as details that are on the return but cannot be used as entered, such as a transposed digit or a routing number copied from the wrong document.
- **Rejected** reads as details that looked usable on the return but did not work when the deposit was attempted at the receiving end, for example because the account had been closed.

Those readings are interpretations of ordinary words, not IRS definitions. What matters for a filer is that all three lead to the same notice, and careful checking before filing can catch many of the errors behind them, though not every rejection is in the filer's control.

The second trigger is the one few filers plan for. A filer who expects to owe tax has no reason to fill in bank details for a refund. If the IRS then corrects that return and the correction turns the balance due into an overpayment, the return now "results in a refund," and the CP53E page lists that as a reason for the notice. The CP53E page gives two examples of how that happens: unreported estimated payments with the return, and math errors such as addition or subtraction mistakes. An extension filer who made estimated payments during the year and left one off the return fits the first example. The IRS pages quoted here do not say what a balance-due filer should do in advance. They do say that if the notice arrives, the response path is the same as for everyone else.

## The 30-day window and the six-week fallback

Both IRS pages describe the same deadline from slightly different angles. The CP53E page puts it as an instruction: "When you receive the CP53E notice you have 30 days to update or add a new bank account or select an exception condition to allow a paper check." The Executive Order Q&A describes the same letter as a request: "The taxpayer will then receive a CP53E notice in the mail requesting a response within 30 days, either to provide banking information or to explain why such information cannot be provided."

So the notice offers two routes, not one. A filer can supply an account, or a filer can say why no account is possible and select one of the exception conditions. Neither page lists the exception conditions; the CP53E page says only that "There are limited exception conditions to request a paper check using your online account." The notice itself and the online account are where a filer finds out which ones exist and whether one fits.

The fallback is the part people misread as a safety net. The CP53E page says: "If you don't respond to the notice, we will issue a paper check after 6 weeks." The Q&A adds a condition: "If there is no response to the notice and there are no other issues with the tax return, the refund will be released as a paper check after six weeks." Answering early is what shortens the wait. The Q&A says: "Once the taxpayer provides the direct deposit information or exception, the refund will be immediately released via direct deposit or paper check." Without an answer, a check is still possible, but only after a wait, and only if nothing else on the return needs attention. Neither page states the exact day the six weeks start counting from, so the dates printed on the notice are the ones to rely on.

Here is the sequence laid out in one place, using only what the two pages state.

| Stage | What the IRS pages say | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Return shows a refund with missing, invalid or rejected bank information | Triggers a CP53E notice | Understanding your CP53E notice |
| Balance-due return corrected by the IRS into a refund | Also triggers a CP53E notice | Understanding your CP53E notice |
| Notice arrives by mail | Response requested within 30 days; the CP53E page counts them from the date of the notice | Understanding your CP53E notice; EO 14247 Q&A |
| Filer adds a bank account online | Allow around 2-5 business days for refund status to update | Understanding your CP53E notice |
| Filer selects an exception condition | Allows a paper check | Understanding your CP53E notice |
| No response, no other issues on the return | Paper check after 6 weeks | Understanding your CP53E notice; EO 14247 Q&A |

## Answering the notice through an online account

The CP53E page gives a short set of steps for adding an account, and they are worth following in order rather than hunting around the site:

1. "Sign in or create an online account."
2. "Check your Notifications in your Account Home."
3. "Select the Add bank account notification."
4. "Follow the prompts to add your account details." The page adds that "Guidance is provided if you don't have a bank account."
5. "Allow around 2-5 business days for your refund status to update in your online account."

The CP53E page notes that the Add bank account option "will only be present when a CP53E notice has been issued," which is why the steps start in Notifications rather than in account settings.

Two limits on that page matter more than the steps. First, the phone is not a way to add an account. The page says "you can only update your bank account by accessing your online account. IRS employees cannot update bank account information," and the Q&A adds that "For security reasons, IRS employees cannot take direct deposit information over the phone or in person." The toll-free number shown on the notice is for questions the website does not answer, not for giving account details. Second, there is one attempt: "you will only have one opportunity to add or update your bank account using your online account. If your direct deposit is not accepted by the bank and is rejected, we will issue a paper check." For anyone who cannot get into an online account, the page points to IRS.help.id.me for help creating or accessing one.

A few habits make this step go smoothly. Copy the routing and account numbers from the bank's own records, such as its app, a statement or the account details screen, rather than from memory or from an old check that may belong to a closed account. Confirm whether the account is checking or savings before selecting it, since the two are separate choices. Use an account that is yours: the CP53E page says "the IRS requires you to use a direct deposit account under your own name or a joint account you share," so a relative's or friend's account will not work. And be wary of any message asking for bank details. The Q&A says that for this purpose "the IRS will only contact taxpayers by sending a letter through the U.S. mail," not by phone or text. If you use the link or QR code on the notice, the CP53E page says to look for a lock icon or "https://" at the start of the address and to make sure ".gov" is in the URL.

A filer who opens a new account specifically to answer the notice should make sure the account is fully open and able to receive deposits before entering it. An account that is still pending, or that the bank later declines, is the kind of detail that could turn an "added" account into a rejected deposit. With only one opportunity to add an account online, the CP53E page says a rejected deposit ends in a paper check, which means waiting on a mailed check instead of a deposit.

## What to put on the return before Oct. 15

Everything above is about recovering from a notice. The better outcome is never getting one, and the extension-filer tip sheet is direct about how. Tax Tip 2026-63 says: "Taxpayers should file electronically and choose direct deposit for their tax refund." The same page gives the deadline, "The deadline for extension filers is Oct. 15, 2026," and a reminder that is easy to forget in October: "The extension to file is not an extension to pay."

That last line matters for refunds too. An extension moved the filing date; it did not change how the IRS pays a refund, and it did not move any payment due date. A filer who is owed money and a filer who owes money both face the same Oct. 15, 2026 filing deadline (the tip sheet notes that taxpayers in disaster areas may have more time), and only the first group needs the direct deposit lines, unless the second trigger described above turns a balance due into a refund later.

For cost-conscious filers, the tip sheet also points to IRS Free File: "The program is available to taxpayers who had an adjusted gross income of $89,000 or less in 2025." Free File is one route to filing electronically, which is the other half of the tip sheet's recommendation.

A pre-filing check for the refund section takes a few minutes:

| Item on the return | What to check | Why it matters under the new rules |
|---|---|---|
| Routing number | Taken from the bank's own records for this exact account | A wrong number is the simplest path to "invalid" |
| Account number | Copied, then compared digit by digit against the source | A transposed digit can make the details unusable |
| Checking or savings | Matches the account type the bank shows | The return asks for this separately from the numbers |
| Account status | Open and able to receive deposits today, not planned to close | A closed account reads as "rejected" |
| Account owner | In your own name, or a joint account you share | The CP53E page says the IRS requires this |
| More than one account | Form 8888 attached if the refund is being split | Splitting is done on the return, not after |

None of those checks changes the refund amount. Every one of them affects whether the money arrives by deposit or gets routed into the notice process.

## Sending part of the refund to savings with Form 8888

Form 8888 is the IRS's tool for splitting a refund. The About page describes it this way: "Use Form 8888 to directly deposit your refund (or part of it) to one or more accounts at a bank or other financial institution (such as a mutual fund, brokerage firm, or credit union) in the United States."

That sentence answers three questions at once. The deposit can be the whole refund "or part of it." It can go to "one or more accounts." And the receiving institution does not have to be a bank; the page names mutual funds, brokerage firms and credit unions as examples, as long as the institution is in the United States.

For a saver, the practical use is obvious. A refund that lands in a checking account can easily get absorbed into ordinary spending. A refund that is split on the return, with a set amount going straight to a savings account, never passes through checking at all. For a household building an emergency cushion, the split does the saving automatically, which is the approach described in [A Beginner's Guide to Creating an Emergency Fund with Different Financial Products](https://smartmoneydaily.github.io/2026/08/17/a-beginners-guide-to-creating-an-emergency-fund-with-different-financial-products/).

Here is how a split might look, using assumed numbers chosen only to show the arithmetic. Suppose an extension filer's 2025 return shows a $2,400 federal refund. The filer wants $400 available for October bills and the rest set aside.

| Destination | Account type | Amount (assumed) | Share of refund |
|---|---|---|---|
| Everyday account at Bank A | Checking | $400 | 1/6 |
| Online savings account at Bank B | Savings | $2,000 | 5/6 |
| Total | | $2,400 | all of it |

Each leg of the split carries its own routing number, account number and account type, so each one is a separate chance to get a digit wrong. The CP53E page lists "rejected" bank information as a trigger, but neither the About page nor the CP53E page describes what happens when one account in a split works and another does not. The safe assumption is that every line on Form 8888 deserves the same verification as a single deposit on the return itself.

Two further points about the form. First, the About page does not state a maximum number of accounts, so anyone planning a split across several accounts should read the current form and its instructions for the limit rather than guessing. Second, the page shows "None at this time" under Recent developments, and it was last reviewed or updated April 23, 2026. Nothing on it suggests the form was changed for the paper check phase-out.

## If there is no bank account to give

Some filers do not have a bank account, and some cannot use the one they have. The Executive Order Q&A addresses them directly: "Alternative electronic payment methods, including payments via certain mobile apps and prepaid debit cards, will be available to serve these individuals. Limited exceptions to the paper check phase-out will also be established."

That sentence gives two paths. One is an electronic alternative: an app account or a prepaid debit card that can receive a deposit. The other is an exception that allows a paper check. The CP53E notice reflects both, since it asks for either banking information or a reason why banking information cannot be provided, and the online account offers the option to "select an exception condition to allow a paper check."

What the Q&A does not do is name which apps or which cards qualify. "Certain mobile apps and prepaid debit cards" is a category, not a list. Before entering an app or card on a return, a filer should confirm with the provider that it can accept a direct deposit from the IRS and should get the routing and account numbers the provider assigns for that purpose. Some app-based accounts sit on top of a partner bank, and how that arrangement affects deposit insurance is covered in [When 'FDIC Insured' Means a Partner Bank, Not Your App](https://smartmoneydaily.github.io/2026/09/08/when-fdic-insured-means-a-partner-bank-not-your-app/).

For someone who qualifies for an exception, the process runs through the notice rather than around it. Nothing in the IRS pages quoted here describes a way to request an exception in advance on the return. The route they describe is to receive the CP53E and respond within 30 days by selecting an exception condition.

## What the delay costs

The six-week fallback is easy to wave off as a mild inconvenience. It is worth putting a number on it, with the caveat that the figures below are assumptions for arithmetic, not a description of any real account or rate.

Take the same assumed $2,400 refund. Suppose the filer would otherwise have moved it into a savings account paying an assumed 4% APY, compounded over the period. Six weeks is 42 days.

| Item | Calculation | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Assumed refund | given | $2,400 |
| Assumed savings yield | given | 4% APY |
| Interest over 42 days at that APY | $2,400 × (1.04^(42/365) − 1) | about $10.86 |

The roughly eleven dollars of interest is the smaller part of the cost. The larger part is that $2,400 the filer was counting on stays out of reach for weeks, at a time of year when some households are budgeting around it. And that figure covers only the six-week fallback. A filer who first waits for the notice, then misses the 30-day window, is further from the money than one whose deposit went through on the first try.

The comparison that matters is between two filers with the same refund. One enters verified details on an electronic return and starts on the direct deposit path. The other leaves the lines blank and enters the sequence in the earlier table. The IRS pages promise neither filer a date; the difference is how many extra steps sit between the return and the money.

## Mistakes that lead to the notice

The IRS pages do not break down how often each trigger occurs, but several of the paths into a CP53E are small, ordinary errors. A few patterns are worth checking against before filing.

**Reusing last year's account without checking it.** An extension filer preparing a return in October may be working from details entered on a prior-year return. If that account has since been closed or changed, the details on the new return are out of date. Pull the numbers fresh.

**Assuming a blank section means a check.** Before the phase-out, leaving the refund section empty was a way of choosing a paper check. The Q&A's statement that the IRS "generally stopped issuing paper refund checks for individual taxpayers after Sept. 30, 2025" ends that default. The Q&A says a return with missing banking information "will still be accepted and processed," but a blank section now reads as missing bank information, which is one of the listed triggers.

**Splitting after the fact.** A filer who decides after filing to put part of the refund into savings has missed the window for Form 8888, which goes in with the return. The time to split is before filing.

**Ignoring the envelope.** The notice comes "in the mail," according to the Q&A, which says the IRS sends these letters to the taxpayer's "last-known address." The CP53E page says you must access your online account "within 30 days from the date of the CP53E notice." A filer who has moved since the last return, or who does not open IRS mail promptly, can lose most of that window without realizing it.

## What to do, in order

Start with the return. File electronically, as Tax Tip 2026-63 recommends, and fill in the refund section with details copied from the bank's records for an open account that can receive deposits. Check the account type. Keep the Oct. 15, 2026 deadline in view, and remember that the extension was never an extension to pay.

Decide on a split before filing. If part of the refund belongs in savings, attach Form 8888 and send that portion straight to the savings account. Verify every account on the form with the same care as a single deposit, and check the current form and instructions for how many accounts it allows.

If there is no bank account, sort out an electronic alternative first. Confirm with the app or prepaid card provider that it can receive an IRS direct deposit, and get the deposit details it assigns.

If a CP53E arrives anyway, answer it quickly. Sign in or create an online account, open Notifications in Account Home, select the Add bank account notification and follow the prompts. Allow around 2-5 business days for the refund status to update. If you cannot access or create an online account, the CP53E page points to IRS.help.id.me; IRS employees cannot take the account details by phone. Doing nothing leads to a paper check after 6 weeks, and only when there are no other issues with the return.

## Common questions

### I received a CP53E but I do not have a bank account. What are my options?

The notice asks for one of two things within 30 days: banking information, or an explanation of why it cannot be provided. The CP53E page says you can "update or add a new bank account or select an exception condition to allow a paper check." The Executive Order Q&A adds that "certain mobile apps and prepaid debit cards" are meant to serve people without traditional accounts, and that "Limited exceptions to the paper check phase-out will also be established." Neither page lists the qualifying apps, cards or exception conditions, so the notice and your online account are where to see the options that apply to you. If you do nothing and there are no other issues with the return, the IRS says the refund will be released as a paper check after six weeks.

### Does filing on extension change how my refund is paid?

No. The extension changed the filing deadline to Oct. 15, 2026; it did not change the refund rules, and Tax Tip 2026-63 is explicit that "The extension to file is not an extension to pay." Because the IRS "generally stopped issuing paper refund checks for individual taxpayers after Sept. 30, 2025," a 2025 return filed in October 2026 falls under the new rules. The same tip sheet recommends that taxpayers "file electronically and choose direct deposit for their tax refund."

### Can I send part of my refund to a savings account at a different bank?

Yes, using Form 8888. The IRS describes it as a way "to directly deposit your refund (or part of it) to one or more accounts at a bank or other financial institution (such as a mutual fund, brokerage firm, or credit union) in the United States." The accounts do not have to be at the same institution. The About page does not state a maximum number of accounts, so check the current form and instructions before planning a split across several of them, and verify each account's details, since each one is a separate place for an error to occur.

The paper refund check did not vanish overnight, but after Sept. 30, 2025 it stopped being the automatic result of a blank line. For an extension filer, avoiding the notice process often comes down to a few minutes spent copying the right numbers onto the return, plus a Form 8888 if part of the money should go straight into savings.
