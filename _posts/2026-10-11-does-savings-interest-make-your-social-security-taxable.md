![Does Savings Interest Make Your Social Security Taxable?](/assets/pin-images/2026-10-11-does-savings-interest-make-your-social-security-taxable.png)

Yes, it can. Interest from a savings account, a CD, a Treasury bill and even a tax-exempt municipal bond goes into the test the IRS uses to decide whether any of your Social Security benefits are taxable. That test adds half of your benefits to your other income and compares the total with a base amount: $25,000 for a single filer and $32,000 for a married couple filing jointly. Above that line, each extra dollar of taxable interest can pull 50 cents of your benefits into taxable income with it, and past a second line, 85 cents, until the benefits reach their limit. Whether any of that becomes a real tax bill depends on your deductions, and from 2025 through 2028 many taxpayers 65 and older can claim an extra $6,000. In the two examples below, estimated with the 2026 tax brackets, a single retiree owes nothing on the first $2,000 of interest, while a married couple whose top bracket is 12% pays $1,263 more on $6,000 of interest, about 21% of it.

The rules here come from IRS [Publication 915](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p915), the 2025 edition used for returns filed in 2026, and [Tax Topic 423](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc423). The dollar thresholds are written into [26 U.S.C. § 86](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-2023-title26/html/USCODE-2023-title26-subtitleA-chap1-subchapB-partII-sec86.htm). The 2026 standard deduction and tax brackets come from [Rev. Proc. 2025-32](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-drop/rp-25-32.pdf), and the $6,000 deduction for people 65 and older is described in IRS fact sheet [FS-2025-03](https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/working-families-tax-cuts-tax-deductions-for-working-americans-and-seniors). The timing rules for interest are in [Publication 550](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p550). The interest discussed here is earned in accounts held outside IRAs and workplace retirement plans, since Publication 550 says its rules "do not apply to investments held in individual retirement arrangements (IRAs), section 401(k) plans, and other qualified retirement plans." This is general information about how the federal rules work, not tax advice for any household, and state income taxes are left out.

## The test that decides whether benefits are taxed

Publication 915 sets it up as a comparison. To find out whether your benefits may be taxable, it says to "compare the base amount (explained later) for your filing status with the total of: One-half of your benefits; plus All your other income, including tax-exempt interest."

Tax Topic 423 says the same thing the other way around: "Your benefits won't be taxable unless the sum of your modified adjusted gross income (MAGI) plus one half of your benefits received in the tax year is more than the base amount for your filing status."

That total is often called provisional income, and this article uses the term as shorthand. The base amounts in Publication 915 are:

- $25,000 if you are single, head of household or a qualifying surviving spouse
- $25,000 if you are married filing separately and lived apart from your spouse for the whole year
- $32,000 if you are married filing jointly
- $0 if you are married filing separately and lived with your spouse at any time during the year

On a joint return, both spouses' income counts even when only one of them gets benefits. Publication 915 is direct about it: "Even if your spouse didn't receive any benefits, you must add your spouse's income to yours to figure whether any of your benefits are taxable." A working spouse's wages and the interest on a [joint savings account](/2026/09/30/joint-savings-account-one-1099-int-who-reports-the-interest-and-how/) land in the same total.

The benefit figure you halve is the net amount in box 5 of Form SSA-1099, which Tax Topic 423 says should arrive "by early February for the benefits paid in the prior calendar year." Box 5 is not the deposit that reaches your bank. Publication 915 says it is box 3, gross benefits, minus box 4, repayments, and adds: "Don't reduce the amount of net social security benefits (box 5) by any of the items listed below." The first item on that list is Medicare premiums deducted from your benefits. Supplemental Security Income stays out of the test entirely, because Publication 915 says Social Security benefits "don't include Supplemental Security Income (SSI) payments, which aren't taxable."

## Where savings interest enters the worksheet

Worksheet 1 in Publication 915 is the full calculation. Its line 3 tells you to "Combine the amounts from Form 1040 or 1040-SR, lines 1z, 2b, 3b, 4b, 5b, 7a, and 8." Line 2b of Form 1040 is taxable interest. Line 4 of the worksheet then adds line 2a, which is tax-exempt interest. In practice that means:

- **Bank interest.** Interest from savings accounts, money market deposit accounts and CDs, reported to you on Form 1099-INT, goes in through line 2b.
- **Treasury interest.** Publication 550 says interest from Treasury bills, notes and bonds "is subject to federal income tax but is exempt from all state and local income taxes." The state break doesn't help here. The federal worksheet counts every dollar.
- **Municipal bond interest.** It stays out of your taxable income but still enters the test. The quick-check version of the worksheet, Worksheet A, has a separate line for it: "Enter any tax-exempt interest income, such as interest on municipal bonds."
- **Savings bonds used for education.** If you exclude savings bond interest because you paid qualified education costs, the exclusion is ignored for this purpose. Publication 915 says not to reduce your other income by exclusions for "Interest from qualified U.S. savings bonds."

Line 7 of the worksheet subtracts certain adjustments: "the amounts from Schedule 1 (Form 1040), lines 11 through 20, and 23 and 25." One of those is line 18, the penalty on early withdrawal of savings. The Form 1040 instructions say "The Form 1099-INT or Form 1099-OID you received will show the amount of any penalty you were charged." So if you broke a CD early in 2026, the penalty reduces the figure that's compared with the base amount, not only your adjusted gross income. The deduction itself is covered in [CD Early Withdrawal Penalties: The Disclosure and the Deduction](/2026/09/22/cd-early-withdrawal-penalties-the-disclosure-and-the-deduction/).

## The 50% and 85% tiers

Crossing the base amount doesn't make all of your benefits taxable. Publication 915 puts the ceiling this way: "Generally, up to 50% of your benefits will be taxable. However, up to 85% of your benefits can be taxable if either of the following situations applies to you." The first situation is when "The total of one-half of your benefits and all your other income is more than $34,000 ($44,000 if you are married filing jointly)." The second is filing separately while living with your spouse.

Worksheet 1 builds the taxable amount in layers. Here is the same arithmetic in plain terms, for a single filer:

1. Take the amount by which provisional income exceeds $25,000. The first $9,000 of that excess is counted at 50%, so this layer tops out at $4,500. It also can't exceed half of your benefits.
2. Any excess beyond that first $9,000, which means provisional income above $34,000, is counted at 85%.
3. Add the two layers. The result can't be more than 85% of your benefits.

For a joint return, the numbers are $32,000, a $12,000 first layer worth up to $6,000, and $44,000. Line 11 of the worksheet says it directly: "Enter $12,000 if married filing jointly; or $9,000 if single, head of household, qualifying surviving spouse, or married filing separately and you lived apart from your spouse for all of 2025."

This is where the leverage comes from. While the taxable part of your benefits is still growing, one more dollar of taxable interest in the first layer adds that dollar plus 50 cents of benefits to your adjusted gross income, $1.50 in all. In the second layer it adds $1.85. The multiplier stops when a limit binds: the first layer can't count more than half of your benefits, and the total can't pass 85% of them. Tax-exempt interest works a little differently. The interest itself stays out of adjusted gross income, so only the extra benefits it pulls in are added.

The thresholds also stay put. Section 86 of the tax code sets the base amounts at $25,000 and $32,000 and the "adjusted base amount" at $34,000 and $44,000, with no inflation adjustment in the text. They are not in Rev. Proc. 2025-32, the IRS list of amounts adjusted for 2026, while the standard deduction and the tax brackets are. Your benefits and your savings balance can grow from year to year, but the lines they're measured against stay where they are.

## Example 1: a single retiree whose deductions absorb most of it

Take a hypothetical single filer who is 67 in 2026. She receives $26,000 a year in Social Security benefits (box 5), takes $18,000 of taxable withdrawals from a traditional IRA and claims the standard deduction. She meets the eligibility rules for both age-based deductions, has no other income and claims no credits. Her 2026 deductions:

- $16,100 standard deduction for a single filer, from Rev. Proc. 2025-32
- $2,050 additional standard deduction, the amount Rev. Proc. 2025-32 gives an aged individual who "is also unmarried and not a surviving spouse"
- $6,000 enhanced deduction for seniors, in full because her income is far below the $75,000 phase-out

That's $24,150 in total. The tax figures in both examples apply the 2026 rate schedules in Rev. Proc. 2025-32 directly. The Form 1040 instructions say that "If your taxable income is less than $100,000, you must use the Tax Table," and the Tax Table can come out a few dollars different. A $100,000 balance at a hypothetical 4% APY earns $4,000 in a year.

| Savings interest | Provisional income | Taxable benefits | Adjusted gross income | Taxable income | Estimated federal income tax |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $0 | $31,000 | $3,000 | $21,000 | $0 | $0 |
| $2,000 | $33,000 | $4,000 | $24,000 | $0 | $0 |
| $4,000 | $35,000 | $5,350 | $27,350 | $3,200 | $320 |
| $8,000 | $39,000 | $8,750 | $34,750 | $10,600 | $1,060 |

The $4,000 row through the worksheet: half of her benefits is $13,000, and her other income is $22,000 ($18,000 plus $4,000), for provisional income of $35,000. That's $10,000 over the $25,000 base. The first $9,000 of the excess counts at 50%, giving $4,500. The remaining $1,000 counts at 85%, giving $850. Taxable benefits come to $5,350, well under the 85% cap of $22,100.

With no interest at all, $3,000 of her benefits is already taxable, because the IRA withdrawals and half her benefits put her above $25,000. The first $2,000 of interest raises her taxable benefits by another $1,000, but it costs her nothing, since her adjusted gross income stays below her $24,150 in deductions.

At $4,000 of interest she owes $320, or 8% of the interest. If the interest hadn't pulled more benefits into income, her adjusted gross income would have been $25,000, her taxable income $850 and her tax $85. The benefits that came along with the interest account for $235 of the $320.

At $8,000 of interest the tax is $1,060, about 13% of the interest, even though every dollar of her taxable income falls in the 10% bracket. The 2026 single brackets in Rev. Proc. 2025-32 start with 10% on taxable income "Not over $12,400."

## Example 2: a married couple in the 85% zone

Now a hypothetical married couple, both 67, filing jointly and meeting the eligibility rules for the age-based deductions. They receive $40,000 a year in combined Social Security benefits and $45,000 from a pension and IRA withdrawals. Their 2026 deductions:

- $32,200 standard deduction for a joint return
- $3,300 in additional standard deductions, $1,650 each for two aged spouses
- $12,000 in enhanced senior deductions, $6,000 each, in full because their income is below $150,000

That totals $47,500. They hold $150,000 in savings.

| Savings interest | Provisional income | Taxable benefits | Adjusted gross income | Taxable income | Estimated federal income tax |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $0 | $65,000 | $23,850 | $68,850 | $21,350 | $2,135 |
| $3,000 | $68,000 | $26,400 | $74,400 | $26,900 | $2,732 |
| $6,000 | $71,000 | $28,950 | $79,950 | $32,450 | $3,398 |

Before any interest, their provisional income of $65,000 is already above $44,000, so they are in the second layer. Every dollar of interest now adds $1.85 of taxable income. At $6,000 of interest, which is $150,000 at a hypothetical 4% APY, taxable benefits rise by $5,100 (85% of $6,000), and their tax goes up by $1,263.

How that $1,263 builds up depends on the brackets. The 2026 joint schedule taxes income "Not over $24,800" at 10% and the next $76,000 at 12%. Their taxable income starts at $21,350, so roughly the first $1,865 of interest is taxed at 10%, which works out to about 18.5 cents per dollar of interest. After that it's taxed at 12%, about 22.2 cents per dollar. Averaged over the $6,000, it's about 21%.

Without the benefits effect, the same $6,000 would have raised their tax by $651. The difference, $612, is the cost of the extra benefits the interest drew into income.

Cutting the interest to cut the tax doesn't pay here. Suppose the couple moved the $150,000 to an account paying a hypothetical 1% to keep their income down. They'd earn $1,500, their tax would rise by $277.50 over the no-interest baseline, and they'd keep $1,222.50 after federal tax. At 4%, they keep $4,737 after federal tax. An average rate of about 21% is a real cost, but it's far below the rate at which a lower yield would come out ahead.

## Where the effect stops

The multiplier only operates in a band of income.

For single and joint filers, interest doesn't reach your benefits until provisional income crosses $25,000 or $32,000. Below that, interest is taxed on its own, if your deductions leave any of it taxable. Married people who file separately and lived together at any point in the year have a base amount of zero, so the worksheet counts their benefits from the first dollar.

At the top, the cap takes over. Once 85% of your benefits is taxable, more interest can't pull in more benefits, and each dollar of interest is just one dollar of income. For the single retiree in Example 1, that happens when provisional income reaches about $54,700, or other income of about $41,700 alongside her $13,000 half-benefit figure. For the couple in Example 2, it's provisional income of about $76,900, or other income of about $56,900.

So the multiplier matters most for households in between: past the base amount, but with less than 85% of their benefits taxable.

## The $6,000 senior deduction doesn't change the worksheet

The July 2025 tax law, Public Law 119-21, added a deduction that is easy to misread as a change in how benefits are taxed. The IRS describes it in FS-2025-03: "Effective for 2025 through 2028, individuals who are age 65 and older may claim an additional deduction of $6,000. This new deduction is in addition to the current additional standard deduction for seniors under existing law." It is "per eligible individual (i.e., $12,000 total for a married couple where both spouses qualify)," and it phases out above modified adjusted gross income of $75,000, or $150,000 for joint filers. Married taxpayers must file jointly to claim it, and the Form 1040 instructions add a valid Social Security number requirement.

The deduction is claimed on Schedule 1-A and, in the words of the Form 1040 instructions, flows to "line 13b the amount, if any, from Schedule 1-A, line 38." That line isn't part of the Publication 915 worksheet. The worksheet starts from your income lines and subtracts only the Schedule 1 adjustments listed earlier. So the senior deduction does its work after the benefits calculation. It lowers the taxable income your tax is figured on, which is why the single retiree in Example 1 owes nothing on her first $2,000 of interest. It doesn't reduce the share of benefits that's counted.

One more connection for savers close to the phase-out line: the Schedule 1-A instructions say most filers enter the amount from Form 1040 line 11b, their adjusted gross income, as the MAGI figure. Taxable benefits are part of that adjusted gross income. In the 85% layer, $1,000 of taxable interest raises that figure by $1,850, so a household near $75,000 or $150,000 can see interest shrink this deduction faster than the interest alone would suggest.

## Timing the interest: what moves and what doesn't

Some interest lands in the year it's earned no matter what you do. Some lands in the year an account matures. The difference matters when one year's numbers are unusual, for example a year with a large one-time IRA withdrawal.

- **Savings and money market deposit accounts.** No timing choice. Publication 550's constructive receipt rule says you're treated as receiving interest on a bank deposit "when they are credited to your account and subject to your withdrawal." Monthly credited interest is that year's income whether or not you touch it.
- **CDs of one year or less.** Publication 550 says interest paid at intervals of a year or less is generally income "when you actually receive it or are entitled to receive it without paying a substantial penalty," and adds: "The same is true for accounts that mature in 1 year or less and pay interest in a single payment at maturity." A six-month CD opened in November 2026 that pays all its interest at maturity in May 2027 would generally be 2027 income, as long as the interest wasn't paid to you or available to withdraw without a substantial penalty in 2026. The CD's terms on crediting and early access to interest decide that.
- **CDs longer than one year.** These don't defer. Publication 550 says that for a CD "with a maturity of more than 1 year, you must include in income each year a part of the total interest due." The mechanics are in [Why You Owe Tax on a 5-Year CD You Cannot Touch Yet](/2026/09/25/why-you-owe-tax-on-a-5-year-cd-you-cannot-touch-yet/).
- **Treasury bills.** Publication 550: "Generally, you report this interest income when the bill is paid at maturity." A newly issued 26-week bill bought at auction in October 2026 and held until it matures in April 2027 would generally produce 2027 interest. The rule assumes you hold the bill to maturity. Treasury notes and bonds pay every six months, and you report that interest "for the year paid." For the state tax side of the comparison, see [Treasury Bills vs CDs: The Tax Break That Changes the Comparison](/2026/09/23/treasury-bills-vs-cds-the-tax-break-that-changes-the-comparison/).
- **Series EE and I savings bonds.** Under what Publication 550 calls Method 1, you can "Postpone reporting the interest until the earlier of the year you cash or dispose of the bonds or the year in which they mature." That deferral means years of interest can arrive in one tax year. Cashing a large stack of bonds in the same year as other income can push provisional income through both layers at once.

Shifting interest into a later year moves the income rather than removing it. Whether that helps depends on the later year's other income, deductions, benefits and tax rates.

## A second look-back: Medicare's income surcharge

Savings interest also feeds a separate threshold, usually with a two-year delay. Medicare's [2026 Medicare costs fact sheet](https://www.medicare.gov/publications/11579-medicare-costs.pdf) says: "If your modified adjusted gross income as reported on your IRS tax return from 2 years ago is above a certain amount, you'll pay the standard Part B premium and an income-related monthly adjustment amount."

The income figure Medicare uses is defined in SSA's operating manual, [POMS HI 01101.010](https://secure.ssa.gov/apps10/poms.nsf/lnx/0601101010), as adjusted gross income plus "tax-exempt interest income (line 2a of IRS Form 1040)." The same section says "Generally, the information is from two years prior to the year for which the premium is being determined, but not more than three years prior," and gives the example that "2026 premiums are generally based on 2024 tax return information." Because the figure starts from adjusted gross income, the standard deduction and the $6,000 senior deduction, which come after adjusted gross income on Form 1040, don't lower it. Municipal bond interest is added in.

The [CMS 2026 premium fact sheet](https://www.cms.gov/newsroom/fact-sheets/2026-medicare-parts-b-premiums-deductibles) sets the standard Part B premium at $202.90 a month. For individual tax returns, the first income tier starts above $109,000, and for joint returns above $218,000. That tier adds $81.20 a month, for a total of $284.10. Married people who lived together but file separately are on a different schedule. CMS says these adjustments "affect roughly 8% of people with Medicare Part B." The tiers are thresholds, not phase-ins: for a single filer, one dollar above $109,000 brings the full $81.20 a month, which is $974.40 over a full year.

Under the usual two-year look-back, interest reported on your 2026 return, along with the taxable benefits it pulls in, would generally feed the income figure for 2028 premiums. The 2028 thresholds haven't been published. Both households in this article's examples had incomes well below the 2026 thresholds. For a household with large balances and income already close to a tier, the timing rules above can matter more for premiums than for income tax.

## Withholding and estimated tax on the extra amount

If interest makes part of your benefits taxable and nothing is withheld to cover it, you may owe when you file. Publication 915 says: "You can choose to have federal income tax withheld from your social security benefits and/or the SSEB portion of your tier 1 railroad retirement benefits. If you choose to do this, you must complete a Form W-4V."

[Form W-4V](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/fw4v.pdf) offers fixed rates only. You "may choose to have the payer withhold federal income tax of 7%, 10%, 12%, or 22% from each payment, but no other percentage or amount." It also says to "Ask your payer exactly when income tax withholding will begin," so a form filed late in the year mostly affects the following year's payments. The form points to the other route as well: "if you have other income that isn't subject to withholding, consider making estimated tax payments."

For the 2026 tax year, [Publication 505](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p505) lists four estimated tax due dates: April 15, June 15 and Sept. 15, 2026, and Jan. 15, 2027. It also says "The penalty is figured separately for each payment period," so a January payment doesn't undo the penalty for the time an earlier installment was short. Whether any earlier period was short depends on your withholding and the safe-harbor tests, which are covered in [Savings Interest and the Jan. 15, 2027 Estimated Tax: The $1,000 Test](/2026/10/07/savings-interest-and-the-jan-15-2027-estimated-tax-the-1000-test/).

## A fourth-quarter check before Dec. 31

The estimate needs only a few numbers, and most of them are on statements you already get:

1. **Benefits.** Estimate your 2026 box 5 amount: benefits before Medicare premiums and any tax withholding, minus any benefits you repaid. Twelve times the gross monthly benefit is a starting point; a partial year or a lump-sum payment needs adjusting. Then take half.
2. **Interest so far.** Add the year-to-date interest on each savings, money market and CD statement, then estimate the remaining months at the current rate.
3. **Maturities.** List CDs and Treasury bills maturing between now and early 2027, and note which pay in December and which in January.
4. **Other income and adjustments.** Add the other income lines the worksheet uses, such as taxable pension and IRA distributions, wages, dividends, capital gains and tax-exempt interest, plus any exclusions it adds back. Then subtract only the Schedule 1 adjustments it allows, such as a CD early-withdrawal penalty. Lines 1 through 8 of Worksheet 1 in Publication 915 list each item.
5. **Compare.** Put the total against $25,000 or $32,000 for the first layer, and $34,000 or $44,000 for the second. Married people filing separately who lived together use the worksheet's zero base.
6. **Payments.** If the result adds tax that withholding doesn't cover, check it against the payment tests in Publication 505 before deciding whether a payment is needed.

The maturity list helps forecast which year interest will be reported in. How much room you have to move it depends on each account's terms. Interest already credited to a savings account and available to withdraw belongs to 2026.

## Questions readers ask

**Did the $6,000 senior deduction make Social Security tax-free?**

No. FS-2025-03 describes it as an additional deduction "in addition to the current additional standard deduction for seniors." It lowers taxable income, but the worksheet that decides how much of your benefits is taxable works the same way it did before.

**Does interest earned inside an IRA count?**

Not as interest. Publication 550's rules on interest don't apply to investments held in IRAs, 401(k) plans and other qualified retirement plans. What enters the worksheet is the taxable amount you withdraw, which goes on Form 1040 line 4b for IRA distributions and line 5b for pensions and annuities.

**My child receives benefits on my record. Whose income does the child's savings interest go with?**

The child's. Tax Topic 423 says "the person with the legal right to receive the benefits must include them in gross income," and that "you must use only your own portion of the benefits in figuring if any part is taxable to you, even if the check for your child was made out in your name." For the child, "half of the portion that belongs to your child must be added to your child's other income," and interest on the child's own savings account is part of that other income. How a child's interest is taxed on its own is covered in [Savings Accounts for Kids: Custodial Rules and the Kiddie Tax](/2026/09/17/savings-accounts-for-kids-custodial-rules-and-the-kiddie-tax/).

**If Social Security is my only income, do I have to file?**

Publication 915 says that if your only income was Social Security, "your benefits generally aren't taxable and you probably don't have to file a return." Once you add other income, including interest, the filing rules for your status apply, and you may have to file even if none of your benefits end up taxable.

There's no separate tax on savings interest for people who collect Social Security. The interest counts in the provisional income test, and from there it can bring part of the benefits into taxable income. In the examples, that meant nothing on the first $2,000 for one household and about 21% for another. The provisional income figure is what tells a household which of those situations it's in.
