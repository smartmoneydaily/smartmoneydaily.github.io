![Dependent Care FSA at $7,500: Election Math vs Paying From Savings](/assets/pin-images/2026-10-09-dependent-care-fsa-at-7500-election-math-vs-paying-from-savings.png)

A parent with two kids in daycare is clicking through the 2027 open enrollment screens at work, sees a dependent care FSA box that will accept up to $7,500, and wonders whether to fill it or keep paying the daycare bill from savings and claim the child care credit at tax time. The short answer: under current federal law the dependent care exclusion is $7,500 ($3,750 if married filing separately) for tax years that begin after Dec. 31, 2025, which covers 2027 elections. But every excluded dollar also shrinks the $3,000 or $6,000 of expenses that can count toward the child and dependent care credit, whose top rate rose to 50% for 2026. For a family with care costs above $7,500 whose tax rates stay about the same either way, the choice usually comes down to two endpoints, all of the FSA or none of it, and the deciding number is the share of each excluded dollar that would otherwise go to tax. Unspent FSA money is generally lost, so the election should match care you are confident you will buy.

The main rules here come from six official sources. [Public Law 119-21](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/PLAW-119publ21/html/PLAW-119publ21.htm) contains the statutory changes in Sec. 70404 and Sec. 70405. [Publication 15-B (2026), Employer's Tax Guide to Fringe Benefits](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p15b) explains how employers apply the new limit. The IRS page [Working Families Tax Cuts](https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/working-families-tax-cuts), which lists the law's tax provisions, summarizes the credit change. [Publication 503 (2025), Child and Dependent Care Expenses](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p503) sets out how FSA benefits interact with the credit. [Publication 969 (2025)](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p969) describes the use-it-or-lose-it rule, and [Revenue Procedure 2025-32](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-drop/rp-25-32.pdf) gives the 2026 health FSA figures. The [Instructions for Form 2441 (2025)](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i2441) and [Form W-4 (2026)](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/fw4.pdf) fill in details on credit limits, forfeitures and withholding. This is general education, not advice for any household; a tax professional or your benefits administrator can check your own situation.

## What changed: $5,000 became $7,500

Sec. 70404 of Public Law 119-21 is short. It amends Section 129(a)(2)(A) of the Internal Revenue Code "by striking ``$5,000 ($2,500'' and inserting ``$7,500 ($3,750''." Its effective date reads: "The amendment made by this section shall apply to taxable years beginning after December 31, 2025."

That is the entire provision: no inflation adjustment and no end date. So under current law, the $7,500 figure that applies to 2026 also applies to 2027 benefits, the ones being elected now. Only a new act of Congress would change it; nothing in the section schedules a change.

Publication 15-B confirms it: "For the 2026 tax year, the annual dependent care FSA limit was raised from $5,000 to $7,500 ($2,500 to $3,750 for married filing separately)." From the employee's side: "An employee can generally exclude from gross income up to $7,500 ($3,750 if married filing separately) of benefits received under a DCAP each year." DCAP means dependent care assistance program; a dependent care FSA funded by salary reduction is one kind.

The exclusion also reaches beyond income tax. Publication 15-B's table of how fringe benefits are treated under employment taxes lists dependent care assistance as "Exempt" up to certain limits, "$7,500 ($3,750 for married employee filing separate return)." A footnote adds that the exemption "doesn't apply to certain highly compensated employees under a program that favors those employees." Higher earners can ask the plan administrator whether that applies.

One caution: the Publication 503 edition cited here is for 2025, so its exclusion figure is the old $5,000. For 2026 and 2027 benefits, the statute and Publication 15-B (2026) carry $7,500.

## Who the money can pay for and the earned income cap

A dependent care FSA covers the same kind of care that qualifies for the credit. Publication 15-B says the services "must be for a qualifying person's care and must be provided to allow the employee to work," requirements it calls "basically the same" as the credit's tests. Publication 503 defines the people covered: you may be able to claim the credit "if you pay someone to care for your dependent who is under age 13 or for your spouse or dependent who isn't able to care for themselves."

That age line drives election sizing. Publication 503's test is a child "who was under age 13 when the care was provided," so care after a 13th birthday does not count unless the child isn't able to care for themselves. Count the months of eligible care before you pick a dollar figure.

Two caps sit alongside the $7,500:

**Earned income.** Publication 15-B says "the exclusion can't be more than the smaller of the earned income of either the employee or employee's spouse." In a two-earner household where one spouse works part time and earns less than $7,500 in the year, the lower earnings set the ceiling. Special rules for a spouse who is a student or unable to self-care are in Publication 503.

**Your actual expenses and your plan's maximum.** Publication 503 lists the amount you can exclude as the smallest of five figures: the dependent care benefits you received, the qualified expenses you incurred, your earned income, your spouse's earned income, and the maximum your plan allows. Because the plan's own maximum is one of the five, the figure on your enrollment screen may be lower than $7,500.

Employer money counts too. Publication 503 says dependent care benefits include amounts your employer paid directly to you or your provider, the fair market value of employer-provided or sponsored daycare, and "Pre-tax contributions you made under a dependent care flexible spending arrangement." If your employer adds a contribution to the account, your own election plus the employer amount is what runs into the $7,500.

Couples where both spouses are offered a dependent care FSA should coordinate before electing more than $7,500 between them; the Common questions section below covers why.

## The credit you trade away: the reduced dollar limit

The credit and the FSA exclusion cover the same expenses, and the same dollar can't count twice. Publication 503 says excluded benefits are not included in your work-related expenses and they reduce the dollar limit: "If you received dependent care benefits that you exclude or deduct from your income, you must subtract that amount from the dollar limit that applies to you. Your reduced dollar limit is figured on Form 2441, Part III."

The dollar limits themselves did not change. The IRS Working Families Tax Cuts page says that "the maximum qualifying expenses remain $3,000 for one qualifying individual and $6,000 for two or more qualifying individuals." Publication 503 frames the exclusion test this way: "the total amount you exclude or deduct must be less than the dollar limit for qualifying expenses (generally, $3,000 if you had one qualifying person or $6,000 if you had two or more qualifying persons) in order for you to claim a credit on the remaining amount."

The 2025 publication adds that with two or more qualifying persons, the excluded amount "will always be less than the dollar limit because the total amount you can exclude or deduct is limited to $5,000." With the cap at $7,500, that no longer holds. A full $7,500 exclusion is larger than both the $3,000 and $6,000 limits, leaving no credit on the same expenses. Sec. 70405 did not change this; it rewrote only paragraph (2) of section 21(a), the "applicable percentage."

| Election (excluded benefits) | One qualifying person: credit limit left | Two or more: credit limit left |
|---|---|---|
| $0 | $3,000 | $6,000 |
| $2,500 | $500 | $3,500 |
| $3,000 | $0 | $3,000 |
| $5,000 | $0 | $1,000 |
| $6,000 | $0 | $0 |
| $7,500 | $0 | $0 |

Source: dollar limits from the IRS Working Families Tax Cuts page; subtraction rule from Publication 503 (2025). The credit limit cannot fall below zero.

So a household that elects the full $7,500, spends it on qualifying care and excludes it from income should expect no credit for the same year's care. One that excludes less keeps a credit only on the gap between the excluded amount and the dollar limit.

## How the 50% credit rate phases down

The credit is a percentage of the expenses that survive the limits above. Sec. 70405 replaced the old percentage with a new formula for tax years beginning after Dec. 31, 2025. The statute reads: "the term `applicable percentage' means 50 percent-- ``(A) reduced (but not below 35 percent) by 1 percentage point for each $2,000 or fraction thereof by which the taxpayer's adjusted gross income for the taxable year exceeds $15,000, and ``(B) further reduced (but not below 20 percent) by 1 percentage point for each $2,000 ($4,000 in the case of a joint return) or fraction thereof by which the taxpayer's adjusted gross income for the taxable year exceeds $75,000 ($150,000 in the case of a joint return)."

The IRS Working Families Tax Cuts page summarizes it: "Beginning with tax year 2026, the maximum credit rate increases from 35% to 50% of qualifying expenses."

Read the formula in two stages:

- **Stage A applies to every filer.** The rate starts at 50% and loses 1 point for each $2,000, or fraction of $2,000, of AGI above $15,000, but it stops at 35%. The 15 points between 50% and 35% are used up once AGI is more than $43,000.
- **Stage B starts higher and depends on filing status.** Above $75,000 of AGI ($150,000 on a joint return), the rate loses another point for each $2,000 ($4,000 joint), or fraction, until it reaches 20%.

"Or fraction thereof" means a partial step counts as a full point. AGI of $16,000 is $1,000 over $15,000, which costs a full point: 49%, not 49.5%.

| AGI | Rate, joint return | Rate, other filers |
|---|---|---|
| $15,000 | 50% | 50% |
| $25,000 | 45% | 45% |
| $40,000 | 37% | 37% |
| $45,000 to $75,000 | 35% | 35% |
| $90,000 | 35% | 27% |
| $103,000 | 35% | 21% |
| $120,000 | 35% | 20% |
| $150,000 | 35% | 20% |
| $160,000 | 32% | 20% |
| $180,000 | 27% | 20% |
| $206,000 | 21% | 20% |
| Above $206,000 | 20% | 20% |

Figures calculated from the Sec. 70405 formula. Rows are sample AGI points, not brackets.

Check one row. On a joint return with AGI of $180,000, stage A has already brought the rate to 35%. Stage B: $180,000 − $150,000 = $30,000 over the line, and $30,000 ÷ $4,000 = 7.5 steps, which rounds up to 8 points. 35% − 8 = 27%.

The full 50% rate reaches only AGI of $15,000 or less. For a two-earner couple paying enough for care to consider a $7,500 FSA, the working rate is more often 35% or lower. Because the exclusion lowers AGI, the two options can land in different rows; the examples below stay in one row to keep the math clean.

## Worked example: two children, full FSA or the credit

All numbers in this example are hypothetical, chosen to make the arithmetic easy to follow.

A married couple filing jointly has two children, ages 3 and 6. Daycare and after-school care will cost $14,000 in 2027. Both spouses work full time and each earns well above $7,500. Without the FSA, their AGI is $120,000. With a $7,500 exclusion it would be $112,500. Both figures fall in the 35% row for joint returns, so the credit rate is 35% either way.

The other input is what each excluded dollar would otherwise cost in tax, which depends on federal bracket, state tax and payroll taxes. For illustration, call it a combined rate of 30%: each $1 excluded keeps $0.30 that would otherwise have gone to tax.

**Option 1: no FSA, pay from savings, claim the credit.**

- Expenses: $14,000. Dollar limit for two children: $6,000.
- Credit: $6,000 × 35% = $2,100.

**Option 2: elect the full $7,500.**

- Tax saved by the exclusion: $7,500 × 30% = $2,250.
- Credit limit: $6,000 − $7,500 is below zero, so $0. Credit: $0.
- Total: $2,250.

**Option 3: elect the old $5,000 figure.**

- Tax saved by the exclusion: $5,000 × 30% = $1,500.
- Credit limit: $6,000 − $5,000 = $1,000. Credit: $1,000 × 35% = $350.
- Total: $1,500 + $350 = $1,850.

At a 30% combined rate, the full $7,500 comes out $2,250 − $2,100 = $150 ahead of skipping the FSA, and the middle choice is the worst of the three.

The middle choice loses for a structural reason. Each election dollar up to $6,000 swaps 35 cents of credit for 30 cents of tax savings; only the dollars from $6,000 to $7,500 add value without costing credit.

That makes the comparison a two-way test between no FSA and the full $7,500. The full election wins when $7,500 × combined rate is more than $2,100. Solving: $2,100 ÷ $7,500 = 0.28. A household with a combined rate above 28% comes out ahead with the full FSA; one below 28% comes out ahead with the credit alone. That test assumes the couple owes enough income tax to use the whole $2,100. The Credit Limit Worksheet in the Instructions for Form 2441 caps the credit at your tax from Form 1040 line 18, less certain other credits, and says that if that figure is "zero or less, stop; you can't take the credit." A household with little income tax gets less from the credit than the table shows.

| Illustrative combined rate | No FSA (credit only) | $5,000 election | $7,500 election |
|---|---|---|---|
| 20% | $2,100 | $1,350 | $1,500 |
| 25% | $2,100 | $1,600 | $1,875 |
| 28% | $2,100 | $1,750 | $2,100 |
| 30% | $2,100 | $1,850 | $2,250 |
| 35% | $2,100 | $2,100 | $2,625 |

Same household: $14,000 of care, two children, 35% credit rate. Each cell is tax saved plus credit on any dollar limit left.

If the couple's AGI put them at a lower credit rate, such as 27% at $180,000, the credit option would be worth $6,000 × 27% = $1,620 and the break-even combined rate would fall to $1,620 ÷ $7,500 = 0.216, or about 22%.

## Worked example: one child and a lower combined rate

Also hypothetical. An unmarried parent has one child, age 4. Care will cost $9,000 in 2027. The parent's AGI without the FSA is $60,000, and $52,500 with it. Both are in the 35% band for filers other than joint, which runs from just over $43,000 through $75,000. Assume an illustrative combined rate of 20%.

**Option 1: no FSA.** The dollar limit for one child is $3,000. Credit: $3,000 × 35% = $1,050. The other $6,000 of care earns no credit.

**Option 2: full $7,500 FSA.**

- Tax saved: $7,500 × 20% = $1,500.
- Credit limit: $3,000 − $7,500 is below zero, so $0.
- Remaining care: $9,000 − $7,500 = $1,500, paid from take-home pay or savings with no credit.
- Total: $1,500.

**Option 3: elect $3,000, matching the credit limit.** Tax saved: $3,000 × 20% = $600. Credit limit: $3,000 − $3,000 = $0. Total: $600.

The full FSA wins by $1,500 − $1,050 = $450. The break-even combined rate is $1,050 ÷ $7,500 = 0.14, or 14%. With one child the credit applies to at most $3,000 of spending, while the exclusion can shelter $7,500, so once care costs reach $7,500 the larger base usually wins.

The comparison changes if care costs less. If this parent expected only $4,000 of care, the most the FSA could shelter is $4,000, worth $4,000 × 20% = $800, against a $1,050 credit. At that spending level, the credit wins.

## Use-it-or-lose-it: sizing the election to care you will buy

The tax math assumes every dollar you elect gets spent on qualifying care. Publication 969 describes the default for FSAs: "FSAs are generally 'use-it-or-lose-it' plans. This means that amounts in the account at the end of the plan year can't generally be carried over to the next year. However, the plan can provide for either a grace period or a carryover."

The rest of that passage is written for health FSAs. Publication 969 says a plan "can provide for a grace period of up to 2 1/2 months after the end of the plan year" for "qualified medical expenses," and "Your employer isn't permitted to refund any part of the balance to you." The carryover option in Publication 15-B sits under the heading "'Use-or-lose' rule for health FSAs." Neither says a dependent care FSA can carry money into the next year. The Instructions for Form 2441 (2025) do address dependent care plans: "your employer may have permitted you to carry forward any unused amount from 2024 to use during a grace period in 2025," and Form 2441 asks for "Any amount you forfeited. You forfeited an amount if you didn't receive it because you didn't incur the expense." Whether your dependent care account has a grace period is set by your plan document, so check before you elect.

The same caution applies to timing. Publication 969's rule that you must be able to receive your full elected amount at any time during the coverage period appears in its health FSA section; it does not tell you how a dependent care account pays claims, so check your plan's reimbursement rules.

Here is the risk side, using the single parent above and the same 20% rate. The parent elects $7,500, then a grandparent takes over part of the year's care for free, and only $6,000 of qualifying care is bought.

- Used: $6,000 × 20% = $1,200 of tax saved.
- Unused: $7,500 − $6,000 = $1,500 left in the account at year-end.

If the plan has no grace period that rescues it, the $1,500 is gone. Without that extra $1,500 election, the parent would have received it as pay: $1,500 × (1 − 0.20) = $1,200 of take-home pay, now lost. Had the care happened, the same $1,500 would have saved $1,500 × 20% = $300 in tax.

At a 20% combined rate, each over-elected dollar risks four times what it can gain: $1,200 against $300. The ratio shrinks as the rate rises, but at any combined rate below 50% the possible loss is larger than the possible gain. That imbalance is why the election should follow care you are confident of, not care you might buy.

Common reasons care spending falls short:

- **A child turns 13.** Care after the 13th birthday does not qualify unless the child isn't able to care for themselves.
- **Free care replaces paid care.** A relative, a school program with no fee, or a schedule change can remove a bill.
- **Care is not work-related.** Publication 503 says a babysitter while you go out to eat isn't normally work-related, and "The cost of sending your child to an overnight camp isn't considered a work-related expense."
- **A forfeited deposit.** Publication 503 says "a forfeited deposit isn't for the care of a qualifying person if care isn't provided."
- **Payments to the wrong relative.** Publication 503 says not to count amounts paid to someone you can claim as a dependent, to your child who was under age 19 at the end of the year, to a person who was your spouse at any time during the year, or to the parent of your qualifying person if that person is your child under 13.

## Paying from savings instead: cash flow and timing

A family that skips the FSA pays the daycare bill from checking or savings and claims the credit on Form 2441 with its 2027 return, filed in 2028. That route has one clear advantage: no forfeiture risk. If care changes midyear, the cash simply stays in savings.

The trade-off is timing. With an FSA, the election comes out of each paycheck before tax, so the tax effect shows up in pay during the year; Publication 503 notes that "Your salary may have been reduced to pay for these benefits." With the credit, the benefit for care paid in 2027 usually arrives when the 2027 return is filed and processed. One exception: Form W-4 (2026), Step 3, lets you include "other tax credits for which you are eligible" with your dependent credits, and says "Including these credits will increase your paycheck and reduce the amount of any refund you may receive when you file your tax return."

The per-paycheck cost of a full election depends on the pay schedule:

| Pay schedule | Paychecks per year | Per-paycheck reduction for $7,500 |
|---|---|---|
| Weekly | 52 | $144.23 |
| Every two weeks | 26 | $288.46 |
| Twice a month | 24 | $312.50 |
| Monthly | 12 | $625.00 |

Calculated as $7,500 divided by paychecks. Your plan may spread the election differently if you enroll midyear.

Cash still has to be on hand in both cases. If your plan reimburses after you pay the provider and file a claim, you need enough in checking or savings to cover the gap. Under the credit route, unless you adjust withholding, you carry the full cost until the return is processed.

The interest effect is small next to the tax effect. Suppose, purely for illustration, a savings account pays 4%. In the two-child example, the credit route delivers $2,100 at filing instead of a $2,250 benefit spread across the year's paychecks. If withholding is not adjusted and the credit arrives about a year after the average dollar of care is paid, the forgone interest is $2,100 × 0.04 = $84. The combined-rate test and the forfeiture risk usually matter more.

Where savings matter most is as a buffer. For the $14,000 family, one month of care held in savings is $14,000 ÷ 12 = about $1,167, which can cover the wait between paying the provider and getting reimbursed.

## Health FSA figures for 2026, and what is not out yet for 2027

If your enrollment screen also offers a health FSA, it is a separate account with separate limits.

Revenue Procedure 2025-32 states: "For taxable years beginning in 2026, the dollar limitation under § 125(i) on voluntary employee salary reductions for contributions to health flexible spending arrangements is $3,400. If the cafeteria plan permits the carryover of unused amounts, the maximum carryover amount is $680." Publication 15-B repeats it: "For plan years beginning in 2026, a cafeteria plan may not allow an employee to request salary reduction contributions for a health FSA in excess of $3,400."

| Account | 2026 figure | 2027 figure | Grace period or carryover |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dependent care FSA (DCAP) | $7,500 ($3,750 MFS) | $7,500 ($3,750 MFS) under current law | Not described as allowing a carryover; check plan for any grace period |
| Health FSA contribution | $3,400 | Not announced as of Oct. 4, 2026 | Grace period up to 2 1/2 months or carryover, if the plan allows |
| Health FSA carryover | $680 maximum | Not announced as of Oct. 4, 2026 | A plan with a carryover can't also offer a grace period |

Sources: Public Law 119-21; Publication 15-B (2026); Revenue Procedure 2025-32; Publication 969 (2025).

The 2027 health FSA numbers were not out when this post was checked: the IRS [news releases page](https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/news-releases-for-current-month), through IR-2026-119 on Oct. 2, 2026, includes no 2027 inflation adjustments. Publication 969 notes that a plan "may specify a lower dollar amount as the maximum carryover amount," so your plan's own figure is the one that applies. If enrollment closes before the IRS figures appear, use the number in your employer's materials.

Publication 969 adds that under a carryover plan, "any unused amounts in excess of the carryover amount are forfeited." The dependent care $7,500, by contrast, is written into the statute with no indexing, so it needs no annual IRS announcement.

## An open enrollment checklist

Work through these before the enrollment window closes:

1. **Estimate 2027 care costs month by month.** Note 13th birthdays, overnight camps, school schedule changes and free relative care.
2. **Find the smaller of each earner's expected earned income.** The exclusion can't exceed it. A spouse with low or no earnings may cap the account or rule it out.
3. **Check your plan's maximum and any employer contribution.** Your election plus the employer amount is what counts toward $7,500.
4. **Coordinate with a spouse's plan.** If both of you are offered an account, confirm with both administrators before electing more than $7,500 between you.
5. **Estimate your credit rate.** Use the Sec. 70405 formula and expected 2027 AGI. A joint return with AGI between $45,000 and $150,000 is at 35%.
6. **Run both endpoints.** No FSA (credit on up to $3,000 or $6,000) against the full election (exclusion on up to $7,500, credit likely zero). When your rates stay about the same either way, care costs at least $7,500 and every dollar gets spent, a partial election lands between the two endpoints rather than above both.
7. **Size down for uncertainty.** If you are unsure about part of the year's care, leave that part out of the election.
8. **Read your plan's grace period and claim rules,** including when claims are paid and the deadline to submit them.
9. **Keep a cash buffer in savings,** sized to your plan's claim cycle.
10. **Save provider details.** Publication 503 requires you to identify every care provider on Form 2441, Part I, and your plan will have its own documentation rules for claims.

## Common questions

**Is the $7,500 dependent care FSA limit also the limit for 2027?**

Under current law, yes. Sec. 70404 of Public Law 119-21 changed the figure from $5,000 to $7,500 ($3,750 for married filing separately) for tax years beginning after Dec. 31, 2025. The section contains no inflation indexing and no end date, so the same amount applies to 2027. Your employer's plan can set a lower maximum, so check the enrollment screen.

**Can I use the dependent care FSA and still claim the child care credit?**

Sometimes, but the two overlap. Publication 503 says excluded benefits reduce the $3,000 or $6,000 dollar limit for the credit, and a credit is possible only on whatever limit remains. A full $7,500 exclusion is larger than both limits, so it leaves no credit on the same expenses. A smaller election leaves a credit on the gap; under the assumptions in the examples above, that mix does not beat the better of the two full routes.

**Does the 50% credit rate apply to most families?**

Only at low incomes. Under Sec. 70405, the 50% rate falls 1 point for each $2,000 or fraction of AGI above $15,000, stopping at 35%, and then falls further above $75,000 of AGI ($150,000 on a joint return) until it reaches 20%. A married couple filing jointly with AGI between $45,000 and $150,000 lands at 35%. Above those levels the rate keeps dropping, which makes the FSA relatively more attractive.

**What happens to money left in my dependent care FSA at the end of the year?**

Publication 969 describes FSAs as generally use-it-or-lose-it, with grace periods and carryovers allowed only if the plan provides them. The detailed grace period and carryover rules in Publication 969 and Publication 15-B are written for health FSAs. Check your dependent care plan's own terms for any grace period and the claim deadline, and size your election to care you are confident will happen.

**My spouse and I both have access to a dependent care FSA. Can we each elect $7,500?**

The sources reviewed for this post do not describe $7,500 as a separate allowance for each working spouse. Benefits are totaled on Form 2441, Part III, and Publication 15-B says amounts an employer can't exclude are added to wages in boxes 1, 3 and 5 of Form W-2. Before electing more than $7,500 combined, check with both plan administrators or a tax professional.

**What is the health FSA limit for 2027?**

It had not been announced as of Oct. 4, 2026. For 2026, Revenue Procedure 2025-32 sets the health FSA contribution limit at $3,400 and the maximum carryover at $680. Your plan may also set a lower carryover than the IRS maximum. Until the IRS releases 2027 figures, rely on the numbers in your employer's enrollment materials.
