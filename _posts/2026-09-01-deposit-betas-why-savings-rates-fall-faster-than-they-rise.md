Anyone who watched their savings rate during a full rate cycle has noticed the asymmetry. When the [Federal Reserve](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy.htm) raised rates, the APY crept up over months. When cuts came, the APY dropped within days.

This is not imagination, and it is not a conspiracy. It is a measured feature of deposit pricing with a name: deposit beta.

## What beta measures

Deposit beta is the share of a change in market rates that a bank passes through to depositors. If market rates rise by two percentage points and a bank raises its savings rate by half a point, the beta on that increase is 25%.

Betas differ by product, by institution, and by direction. Broadly:

| Product | Typical upward beta | Behavior on cuts |
|---|---|---|
| Checking | Very low | Little to change |
| Traditional savings at a large bank | Low | Falls, from an already low level |
| High-yield online savings | High | Falls quickly |
| CDs | High at issue | Existing CDs unaffected until maturity |

The pattern is consistent: accounts that pay close to market rates on the way up also track them down. Accounts that never rose have nothing to give back.

## Why the asymmetry exists

Three forces, none of them mysterious.

**Deposits are sticky.** Most balances do not move when a better rate appears elsewhere. Moving money takes attention and paperwork, and the difference on an average balance is small enough that many people never act. A bank that knows its depositors are unlikely to leave has little reason to raise rates quickly.

**Repricing on the way down is free.** A savings account rate is variable by contract. The bank can change it at any time with notice that is often minimal. On the way up, raising rates costs the bank money on every existing dollar, not just new ones. On the way down, cutting saves money on every existing dollar. The incentive runs one direction on speed.

**Competition is concentrated at the margin.** Online banks compete for money that moves. They run high betas because their depositors are rate-sensitive by selection. Large branch banks hold balances that are there for convenience, and they price accordingly. The national average savings rate stays low while online accounts pay multiples of it, and both are rational for the institutions involved.

## What it means in practice

Several things follow.

**The rate you opened with is not the rate you have.** A high-yield account is a variable rate product. Checking the current APY on your own account, rather than the one advertised for new accounts, is the single most useful five-minute exercise in personal deposit management. Banks sometimes run different rates for new and existing money on similarly named products.

**CD timing is a beta decision.** A CD converts a variable rate into a fixed one. Locking in before a cutting cycle preserves a rate the savings account will lose. Locking in before a rising cycle does the opposite. Nobody knows the path in advance, which is the argument for a ladder rather than a single bet on timing.

**The national average is not your benchmark.** Averages published by the [FDIC](https://www.fdic.gov/resources/deposit-insurance) include the enormous pool of near-zero balances at large branch banks. A high-yield account paying many times the national average is normal, not remarkable, and the comparison that matters is against other high-yield accounts and short-term Treasury yields.

## Watching your own account

Three habits cover most of the value:

1. Note the APY on your statement each month. The change tells you what your bank's beta actually is, which is better information than any forecast.
2. When rates are falling, compare your account against the current best available. Falling rates spread unevenly, and the gap between institutions tends to widen.
3. When rates are rising, give a good bank a few months before moving. Some raise on a lag rather than not at all, and chasing costs transfer days during which the money earns nothing.

Deposit beta explains most of what happens to a savings rate without any need for a theory about intentions. The bank is repricing a variable product in the direction that suits it, at the speed the market forces on it. Knowing which direction you are in tells you whether to wait or to move.

## What the asymmetry costs, in dollars

Abstract percentages understate this. Consider $40,000 held through a full cycle where market rates rise by three points and later fall by three points, and compare two accounts.

A high-beta online savings account tracks most of the move up and most of the move down. A low-beta branch savings account barely moves in either direction.

Over the rising phase, the online account earns roughly what short-term market rates paid, less a modest spread. The branch account earns close to nothing. Over three years of elevated rates, the gap on $40,000 runs to several thousand dollars.

Over the falling phase, the online account gives back most of its advantage in rate terms, but the money already earned does not come back to the bank. The cumulative difference persists.

This is why the beta conversation is not really about timing. It is about which account the money sits in during the years when rates are high, and the answer to that does not require any prediction.

## The role of CDs and Treasuries in a falling market

When betas on the way down are high, which they generally are, a variable savings rate is the least protected place for cash in a cutting cycle.

A CD converts the rate into a contract. Once locked, the bank's pricing decisions no longer reach it until maturity. A Treasury bill or note does the same thing through a market instrument, with the added feature that its price rises when rates fall, which matters if it might be sold early.

Neither is free. Both trade flexibility for rate certainty, and both look like a mistake if rates rise instead. The structural answer is a ladder, where a portion matures regularly and gets reinvested at whatever the market then offers, which averages the timing decision instead of betting on it.

## What to watch rather than predict

Forecasting the rate path is not a useful personal finance activity. Three observable things are:

1. **Your own account's APY, month over month.** It reveals your bank's actual behavior, which is the only beta that affects you.
2. **The spread between your account and the best widely available rate.** When this widens past a point worth acting on, act. When it is a tenth of a point, do not.
3. **The shape of CD offerings.** Banks frequently pay the most on terms they expect to be cheap for them, and a sudden willingness to pay well on longer terms is information about what the market expects.

None of that requires an opinion about the Federal Reserve. It requires reading your own statement, which is where the effect actually shows up.
