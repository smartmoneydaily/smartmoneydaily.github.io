The year-end statement showed $2,400 of interest and a second line that had never appeared before: federal income tax withheld, $576. Only $1,824 reached the balance. Scrolling back through the account's document center turned up an unopened item from October, mailed in an envelope stamped "IMPORTANT TAX INFORMATION ENCLOSED," asking for a signed form. In January the Form 1099-INT arrived with $2,400 in box 1 and $576 in box 4.

Nothing was stolen, and no penalty was charged. The bank was doing what federal law told it to do after the IRS reported that the name and Social Security number on the account did not match its files.

This is backup withholding, and the 24% is a prepayment of your own income tax, not a fine. It starts one of two ways: a taxpayer identification number problem, meaning no TIN was given or the name and number on the account do not match IRS or Social Security Administration records, or an IRS finding that you underreported interest or dividends on a past return. A signed Form W-9 shuts off the first kind, though a repeat mismatch takes proof from the SSA or the IRS instead. The second kind can only be turned off by the IRS. Either way the $576 is not gone. It goes on Form 1040 as federal income tax withheld, and anything beyond the tax you owe comes back as refund.

## What the 24% is and what it applies to

Backup withholding is ordinary federal income tax collected in advance out of a payment that normally arrives whole. [IRS Topic no. 307](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc307) states the mechanic plainly: "the payer must withhold at a flat 24% rate when" one of a short list of conditions applies. The Internal Revenue Manual section that runs the program, [IRM 5.19.3](https://www.irs.gov/irm/part5/irm_05-019-003r), names the statute behind it: "Internal Revenue Code section 3406(a) provides that a payor of a reportable payment must withhold at a rate of 24% if one of the four conditions exist."

The bank keeps none of it, and the rate is not the bank's to set. [Publication 1281](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/p1281.pdf), the handbook the IRS writes for payers, puts it in one line: "Rate/Percentage is 24%, effective for all subject payments after December 31, 2017." [Publication 1335](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/p1335.pdf) records what it was before, a reminder that the figure moves with the tax law: "28% for payments made in 2003 through December 31, 2017. Payments made on or after January 1, 2018 should be withheld at 24%."

What gets withheld from is the interest, not the balance. [Form W-9](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/fw9.pdf) defines it from the account holder's side: "Persons making certain payments to you must under certain conditions withhold and pay to the IRS 24% of such payments. This is called 'backup withholding.'" On a savings account the payment is the interest credited, and Pub 1335 fixes the moment: "Payments are considered made when a payment is credited to a payee's account (whether or not withdrawn)." A $50,000 balance is untouched; $2,400 of interest credited over the year produces $576 of withholding.

Some money sits outside the rule. Pub 1335 lists "Salary or wage payments," "Pensions," "IRA distributions," and "Any payment of less than $10 (provided the total of such payments would not exceed $10 for the year.)" A paycheck already carries its own withholding; savings interest does not, which is why a backup exists at all. The ordinary tax on deposit interest is worked through in [The Tax Bite on Savings Interest: What Your Real Yield Is](/2026/08/26/the-tax-bite-on-savings-interest-what-your-real-yield-is/).

## The five triggers and the form that switches them off

Form W-9's instructions set out the whole list. "Payments you receive will be subject to backup withholding if:"

1. "You do not furnish your TIN to the requester;"
2. "You do not certify your TIN when required (see the instructions for Part II for details);"
3. "The IRS tells the requester that you furnished an incorrect TIN;"
4. "The IRS tells you that you are subject to backup withholding because you did not report all your interest and dividends on your tax return (for reportable interest and dividends only); or"
5. "You do not certify to the requester that you are not subject to backup withholding... (for reportable interest and dividend accounts opened after 1983 only)."

The same page gives the negative version, which is the shortest statement of how to stay clear: "You will not be subject to backup withholding on payments you receive if you give the requester your correct TIN, make the proper certifications, and report all your taxable interest and dividends on your tax return."

The IRS administers this through [two programs](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/backup-withholding): BWH-B, imposed "because you failed to provide a correct taxpayer identification number (TIN)," and BWH-C, imposed "because you failed to report or underreported interest and dividend income you received on your federal income tax return." They arrive by different routes and stop by different means, and Pub 1281 tells a payer facing both to "backup withhold for only one reason at a time."

Within the TIN track, a missing number and a wrong one are handled differently. Pub 1281: "Missing TIN - We consider a TIN to be missing if it is not provided, has more or less than nine numbers, or it has an alpha character as one of the nine positions." Then: "Incorrect TIN - We consider a TIN incorrect if it is in the proper format but the Name/TIN combination does not match or cannot be found on IRS or SSA files." A missing TIN gets no grace period, since the instruction to the bank is to "Begin backup withholding immediately on any reportable payments... Backup withhold until you receive a TIN." An incorrect one travels through the notice process below, which takes months.

The form the bank hands you at account opening is doing more than collecting a number. [Publication 550](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p550): "For new accounts paying interest or dividends, you must certify under penalties of perjury that your TIN is correct and that you are not subject to backup withholding," and "Your payer will give you a Form W-9, or similar form, to make this certification." Part II of the current form (Rev. 3-2024) reads: "Under penalties of perjury, I certify that: 1. The number shown on this form is my correct taxpayer identification number (or I am waiting for a number to be issued to me); and 2. I am not subject to backup withholding because (a) I am exempt from backup withholding, or (b) I have not been notified by the Internal Revenue Service (IRS) that I am subject to backup withholding as a result of a failure to report all interest or dividends, or (c) the IRS has notified me that I am no longer subject to backup withholding."

Item 2 is the one people sign past. The certification instructions: "You must cross out item 2 above if you have been notified by the IRS that you are currently subject to backup withholding because you have failed to report all interest and dividends on your tax return." Leaving it in after the IRS has said otherwise is a false certification, and the form prices it: "If you make a false statement with no reasonable basis that results in no backup withholding, you are subject to a $500 penalty." Failing to furnish a correct TIN carries "a penalty of $50 for each such failure unless your failure is due to reasonable cause and not to willful neglect."

If the number does not exist yet, deposit interest gets a window other income does not. The instructions say to "apply for a TIN and enter 'Applied For' in the space for the TIN," after which "you will generally have 60 days to get a TIN and give it to the requester before you are subject to backup withholding on payments. The 60-day rule does not apply to other types of payments." The window carries a condition that surprises people who park cash and then move it. The [Instructions for the Requester of Form W-9](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/iw9) set a reserve rule: "You must backup withhold on any reportable payments made during the 60-day period if a payee withdraws more than $500 at one time, unless the payee reserves an amount equal to the current year's backup withholding rate on all reportable payments made to the account." Waiting on a number that takes time to arrive is the ordinary case for new arrivals, covered in [How to Open a U.S. Savings Account Without an SSN](/2026/09/18/how-to-open-a-us-savings-account-without-an-ssn/).

## How a mismatch reaches your bank: CP2100 and the "B" notice

Nothing happens because a teller noticed something. The sequence starts after the bank files its 1099s and the IRS tries to match each name and number against SSA and IRS files.

Pub 1281: "After the submission of Form 1099 information returns, the IRS will send you a CP2100 or a CP2100A Notice and a listing of incorrect Name/TIN(s) reported on those forms." The notice "tells a payer that he or she may be responsible for backup withholding," and the version depends on how many errors the filer had, from a CP2100A for "Less than 50 error documents" up to a data file for "250 or more."

The bank then passes the problem to you. "A 'B' Notice is a backup withholding notice," Pub 1281 explains, and "You must send the First 'B' Notice and a Form W-9 to a payee after you receive the first CP2100 or CP2100A Notice with respect to this account for soliciting a correct Name/TIN combination."

The deadlines are fixed, which is why an unread message in October turns into withholding in December.

| Step | Deadline | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Bank mails the first "B" notice with a Form W-9 | "within 15 business days from the date of the CP2100/CP2100A Notice or the date you received it (whichever is later)" | Pub 1281, Part 4 |
| Bank starts withholding if no signed W-9 comes back | "no later than 30 business days after you have received the CP2100 or CP2100A Notice" | Pub 1281, Q19 |
| Bank stops withholding once the signed W-9 arrives | "within 30 calendar days after you have received the required certification (Form W-9) from the payee" | Pub 1281, Q19 |
| Same account flagged again within three years | Second "B" notice, no Form W-9 enclosed | Pub 1281, Part 4 |
| Bank keeps tracking the account | "three years after the date of the first CP2100A or CP2100 Notice" | Pub 1281, Q26 |

The notice carries the heading "Backup Withholding Warning!" and a deadline the bank writes into a blank: "WE NEED A FORM W-9 FROM YOU BEFORE: _______________________. Otherwise; backup withholding will begin". Its explanation of the mismatch names the two ordinary causes. "Often a TIN does not match IRS records because a name has changed through marriage, divorce, adoption, etc., and the change has not been reported to SSA, so it has not been recorded in SSA's files." And: "Sometimes an account or transaction may not contain the correct SSN of the actual owner. For example, an account in a child's name may reflect a parent's SSN. (An account should be in the name and SSN of the actual owner.)" Getting that second one right at the outset is part of [Savings Accounts for Kids: Custodial Rules and the Kiddie Tax](/2026/09/17/savings-accounts-for-kids-custodial-rules-and-the-kiddie-tax/).

Return the form even when you are certain the account is correct, because the notice says so: "You must send us a signed IRS Form W-9 before the due date of this notice even if the name and number (SSN or EIN) on your account with us match the name and number (SSN or EIN) on your social security card or the document issuing you an EIN." And if the notice comes back undeliverable because the bank has an old address, withholding starts anyway: "You must begin backup withholding."

On a first notice the signed form is enough by itself, even if it repeats the same number. Pub 1281 instructs the bank to "Keep the Form W-9 on file to show that the payee certified the Name/TIN combination. Do not backup withhold."

A second flag on the same account inside three years changes that. The second "B" notice tells the account holder: "Section 3406 of the Internal Revenue Code requires that we withhold a predetermined percent in tax, called backup withholding, when you do not give us your correct Name/TIN combination." Then the consequence: "we are now required to disregard any future Name/TIN combinations you furnish us for your account (whether or not you certify your TIN under penalties of perjury) unless SSA (or, in the case of an incorrect employer identification number, the IRS) validates your Name/TIN combination."

No Form W-9 goes out with it, and a call to the branch resolves nothing, since the information "may not be solicited by telephone." What the bank needs is documentary proof: "An individual payee must provide the payor with a copy of a Social Security card with his or her correct name and SSN." And the card has to be new or different, because a payer may rely on it "only if the name and SSN combination appearing on the card differ from the name and SSN combination appearing on the second B notice, or if there is a date appearing on the Social Security card that is no earlier than six months prior to the date of the second B notice." An old card showing what the bank already has proves nothing. Entities use "IRS Letter 147C" for an EIN instead. The clock is 30 business days from the date of the second notice, and withholding runs "until you receive the validation."

## The other route: underreported interest

The second track has nothing to do with paperwork at the bank. [The "C" program](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/backup-withholding-c-program) rests on "IRC section 3406(a)(1)(C)" and "provides notice to payers, such as banks and corporations, to deduct 24 percent from future interest and dividend payments." The trigger is your return, not your account: "You may become the subject to backup withholding if you fail to: fully report all your interest and dividend income on your tax return, file a tax return including all interest and dividend income you received, or certify, under penalties of perjury, that you're not subject to backup withholding for interest and dividend accounts opened after 1983."

Nobody lands here without warning, though the two IRS descriptions of the runway differ. The C program page says "The IRS will issue up to four notices to the taxpayer during a 120-day period before notifying the payer to begin BWH on future interest or dividends payments." Pub 1335 describes a longer sequence: "the IRS will send at least four notices over a period of at least 210 days asking the payee to correct the underreporting. The final notice tells the payee that they are subject to backup withholding and the IRS will notify their payers to withhold." Either way it takes months of unanswered mail, and both agree the last notice is the one announcing that withholding is coming.

Once it starts, the bank has to say so quickly. Pub 1335: "The payer must notify the payee within 15 days after the first payment subject to backup withholding is made." Two items in that notice are worth reading twice: "The payee must get a determination letter from the IRS to stop the withholding," and "The payee may not certify to another payer that he or she isn't subject to backup withholding while subject to it." Moving the money to a new bank and signing a clean W-9 there is not an exit; it is the false certification the form charges $500 for.

The real exit runs through the return. Topic 307: "To stop backup withholding, you'll need to correct the reason you became subject to backup withholding in the first place. This can include providing the correct TIN to the payer, resolving the underreported income and paying the amount owed, or filing the missing return(s), as appropriate." The bank cannot lift it on its own, because Pub 1335 tells it to continue "until the IRS notifies the payer to stop or until the payee shows written certification from the IRS that he/she is no longer subject to backup withholding," and that certification "must have been issued after the start notice."

## Where the money shows up, and how to get it back

On the tax form, backup withholding sits in one box. The recipient instructions on [Form 1099-INT](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/f1099int.pdf) read: "Box 4. Shows backup withholding. Generally, a payer must backup withhold if you did not furnish your TIN or you did not furnish the correct TIN to the payer. See Form W-9. Include this amount on your income tax return as tax withheld."

Box 1 is not reduced by what was taken: "Box 1. Shows taxable interest paid to you during the calendar year by the payer." The $2,400 is income and the $576 is tax already paid, the way a W-2 splits wages from withholding.

A form always arrives when withholding happened, even on small interest. The [Instructions for Forms 1099-INT and 1099-OID](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i1099int) require a form for each person "From whom you withheld (and did not refund) any federal income tax under the backup withholding rules regardless of the amount of the payment." The usual $10 floor does not apply.

Claiming it is one line. On the [2025 Form 1040](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/f1040.pdf), line 25 is "Federal income tax withheld from:" with 25a for "Form(s) W-2," 25b for "Form(s) 1099," and 25c for other forms, added on 25d. Box 4 goes on line 25b. Topic 307: "If your Form 1099 shows an amount withheld under the backup withholding rules, report the amount as federal income tax withheld on your income tax return for the year you received the income."

The bank usually cannot hand the money back. Pub 1281 allows a payer refund only narrowly: "The refund must be made prior to the end of the calendar year and prior to the time the payer issues a Form 1099." And the option exists only for the bank's own mistakes, since "Payers may use refund alternatives only when backup withholding is the result of an error by the payer. The timely submission of requested TIN information including any verifications and/or certifications by the payee does not establish an error by the payer." Fixing the mismatch stops future withholding; it does not reverse what already went to the IRS.

The other constraint is the deadline for claiming it. If the withholding exceeds your tax, or you were not required to file at all, the only way to recover it is a return. [Topic no. 153](https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc153) is blunt: "You risk losing your refund if you don't file your return. If you are due a refund for withholding or estimated taxes, you must file your return to claim it within 3 years of the return due date." If a return went in without the withholding, the fix is an amended one, and the [Instructions for Form 1040-X](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i1040x) set that window: "Generally, for a credit or refund, you must file Form 1040-X within 3 years (including extensions) after the date you filed your original return or within 2 years after the date you paid the tax, whichever is later."

## Common questions

**Can the bank waive backup withholding if I explain the situation?**

No. Pub 1281 tells the payer what it needs before it may stop: "You need a TIN that the payee certifies as correct on Form W-9 in order to stop current backup withholding or prevent backup withholding from starting." After a second notice the bar rises to an SSA card or an IRS validation letter, and the instructions add that the information "may not be solicited by telephone." A branch conversation cannot substitute for the document.

**I fixed it in March. Why did withholding continue into April?**

That is inside the rules. Pub 1281: "You must stop backup withholding on payments within 30 calendar days after you have received the required certification (Form W-9) from the payee or TIN validation from the SSA or the IRS, if it was a second notification." The bank may stop sooner, since "At your option, you may start and stop backup withholding at any time during these 30-day periods," but 30 days is the outside limit, and anything withheld meanwhile is claimed on the return.

**It is a joint account. Whose problem is it?**

Generally the primary owner's. Pub 1335 states the payer's rule: "Currently the payer is required only to withhold on an account when the payee specified by the IRS is shown as the first name on the account." When the IRS notice lists more than one person the reach widens: "If the IRS notice to start withholding names several payees or co-owners, withhold on all accounts in joint names and any in which either payee is named first."

**Does backup withholding survive the account holder's death?**

Generally not. Pub 1335 answers directly: "In general, no, because in the case of an individual, the payee no longer owns the account after death." The account itself moves on to a different set of rules, described in [What Happens to a Savings Account or CD When the Owner Dies](/2026/09/11/what-happens-to-a-savings-account-or-cd-when-the-owner-dies/).

A line reading "federal income tax withheld" on a savings statement is a message with a return address. It says the IRS could not match the name and number on that account, or that a past return left interest off. The 24% comes back through the return. The mismatch does not, and it can flag again next filing season if the only thing that changed is a signature on a form.
