Two CD offers for the same term are not automatically comparable numbers. Compounding can differ, minimums can differ, the rule about where the interest goes can differ, and the penalty for needing your money early can differ by months. The percentage on the sign is one input out of several.

This gives you a way to reduce every offer to one figure you can rank: dollars in your hand at maturity. You get the arithmetic for the same-term case, the arithmetic for comparing different terms over a common window, the non-rate fields that change the answer, how insurance limits work once money is spread across banks, and a plain description of the mechanics behind a quoted rate.

All rate figures below are FDIC national averages as of August 17, 2026. They are averages across insured institutions, not offers, and any individual quote can sit above or below them. Where an example needs a number that is not a national average, it is labeled illustrative. No bank's actual rate is quoted anywhere on this page.

## APY as the common denominator

Start by refusing to compare interest rates.

APY includes the effect of compounding; a stated interest rate does not. Two institutions can post the same interest rate and hand you different money, because one compounds daily and the other pays at maturity. APY folds that difference in already. That is what it is for. If a quote gives you a rate and no APY, ask for the APY before going further.

Then confirm you are comparing the same thing on the other axes:

- Same term, in months, not "about a year"
- Same deposit amount, including any minimum required to earn the advertised APY
- Same treatment of interest — left in the CD to compound, or paid out to another account

That last one is the quiet one. An APY assumes interest stays in and compounds. If the product transfers interest to a checking account every month, the money you end up with is not what the APY implies, because the transferred interest stops earning. The disclosure states which one you are buying.

## Dollars at maturity, not percentage points

Percentage points are hard to feel. Dollars are not. Convert before you decide.

Take $25,000 for 12 months. Over exactly one year, an APY applied to a fixed balance is simply balance × APY.

- At 1.71% APY, the FDIC national average for 12-month CDs as of August 17, 2026: $25,000 × 0.0171 = $427.50, for $25,427.50 at maturity.
- At 1.90% APY, an illustrative figure 0.19 points higher: $25,000 × 0.019 = $475.00, for $25,475.00 at maturity.

The gap is $475.00 − $427.50 = $47.50 over the year.

Hold that number while you weigh the rest of the offer. Nineteen basis points sounds like something worth driving across town for. Forty-seven dollars and fifty cents on twenty-five thousand may or may not be, depending on what else differs — a penalty two months longer, a minimum that strands cash you need, an auto-renewal you forget to cancel.

Scale changes the answer, which is why you run it on your actual deposit. On $250,000 the same 0.19-point gap is $250,000 × 0.019 = $4,750.00 against $250,000 × 0.0171 = $4,275.00, a difference of $475.00 for the year.

## Comparison across different terms

Two offers with different terms cannot be ranked by APY at all. You have to pick a common window and account for what happens to the money in between.

National averages show why. Compare a 6-month CD at 1.41% with a 12-month CD at 1.71%, over a 12-month window, on $25,000. This uses simple proration — half the annual yield for a six-month holding — and ignores interest on interest, so the second six months is computed on the original $25,000. State the convention out loud whenever you simplify like this.

- 12-month path: $25,000 × 0.0171 = $427.50.
- 6-month path, first half: $25,000 × 0.0141 = $352.50 for a full year, so $352.50 ÷ 2 = $176.25 for six months.
- To tie, the second six months must produce $427.50 − $176.25 = $251.25.
- Annualized on $25,000: $251.25 × 2 = $502.50, and $502.50 ÷ $25,000 = 2.01%.

Rolling the six-month twice matches the twelve-month only if the renewal lands near 2.01% APY — 0.30 points above the current 12-month average and 0.60 above the current 6-month average.

Nothing there is a prediction. It names the rate the shorter path would need, and then you decide whether you want to depend on it.

## Non-rate terms that change the answer

Record the same fields for every offer. If a field is blank, the comparison is not finished.

| What to record | Where it is stated |
| --- | --- |
| APY, not the interest rate | Rate sheet and account disclosure |
| Term, in months | Rate sheet |
| Minimum to open, and minimum to earn the APY | Account disclosure |
| Early-withdrawal penalty | Account disclosure |
| Interest compounded in, or paid out | Account disclosure |
| Grace period at maturity, and auto-renewal | Account disclosure |
| FDIC or NCUA insured, and under which charter | FDIC BankFind or the NCUA research tool |

Two of those deserve a sentence each.

The early-withdrawal penalty is generally stated as a number of months of interest. On the $25,000 twelve-month example at 1.71%, six months of interest under simple proration is $427.50 ÷ 12 × 6 = $213.75. That is the price of changing your mind, and it can differ sharply between two offers whose APYs sit a hundredth of a point apart.

Auto-renewal is the other. Your deposit agreement states whether there is a grace period after maturity, how long it runs, and what happens if you do nothing. Put the maturity date and the end of that grace period in your calendar the day you fund the account.

## Insurance limits across multiple banks

Chasing rates across institutions eventually runs into coverage.

FDIC standard coverage is $250,000 per depositor, per insured bank, per ownership category. Credit unions are insured by the NCUA at the same standard limit. Ownership categories — single, joint, certain retirement accounts, revocable trusts — are insured separately from one another, so the arithmetic is not simply one bank, $250,000, done.

Two practical points. Deposits at a bank that fails are insured; investments are not, and an investment product sold in a branch is not automatically a deposit. And brands are not always separate banks: two names can share one charter, and coverage applies per insured bank rather than per brand. The FDIC's BankFind tool shows which brand names operate under which charter, and checking takes about a minute.

FDIC trust-account rules changed effective April 1, 2024. If a trust holds the deposit, the rules were revised and any formula you learned before that date may no longer apply. Use the FDIC's EDIE estimator to see where you stand.

## The mechanics behind a quoted rate

Two mechanisms are worth understanding, and neither lets you forecast.

The first is monetary policy. The Federal Reserve sets a target range for the federal funds rate, and that policy rate is the reference point around which short-term funding is priced across the system. Changes there flow through to what deposit products can pay.

The second is competition for deposits. Each institution sets its own rates and states them in its disclosures. Its own funding needs and the offers around it both feed into that decision, which is why two quotes for an identical term are not automatically the same number, and why the national average sits where it sits rather than at any one bank's figure.

The term structure is the third thing you can observe directly. As of August 17, 2026 the national averages run 1.14% at 3 months, 1.41% at 6, 1.71% at 12, 1.57% at 24, 1.34% at 36, 1.27% at 48, and 1.36% at 60. Read the shape, compare your offers against it, and skip the part where anyone tells you what it looks like next quarter.

## A repeatable comparison sheet

Same order, every time:

1. Convert every quote to APY, and write down the term in months.
2. Compute dollars at maturity on the amount you actually plan to deposit.
3. If the terms differ, pick a common window and compute the renewal rate the shorter offer would need to tie.
4. Fill in the penalty, minimum, interest-payout, and grace-period fields for each offer.
5. Confirm insurance — FDIC or NCUA, and which charter — before the total at any one institution approaches $250,000 in a single ownership category.
6. Calendar the maturity date and the grace period the day you fund it.

Six lines and ten minutes, and the offer that pays you the most stops being whichever one printed the biggest number.
