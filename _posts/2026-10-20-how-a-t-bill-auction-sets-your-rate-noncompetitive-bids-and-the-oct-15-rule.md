Take a hypothetical saver who keeps her emergency fund at a brokerage. On Tuesday, October 13, she asked the firm to enter a noncompetitive bid for $20,000 of a new 17-week Treasury bill. She didn't name a rate. A noncompetitive bid is an agreement to take whatever rate the auction produces, and the auction the next morning produced a high discount rate of 4.195%. The bill's issue date was October 20 and its price was 98.613319 per $100 of face value, so her $20,000 of bills cost $19,722.66, before any brokerage fee, and will pay $20,000 on February 16, 2027. Her bid was part of $559,073,200 of noncompetitive bids that Treasury filled in full. The rate came from about $207.9 billion of competitive bids, of which about $73.5 billion was accepted.

On October 15, the day after that auction, Treasury's Bureau of the Fiscal Service published a final rule amending the regulation that governs every auction of [U.S. Treasury](https://www.treasurydirect.gov/) bills, notes and bonds. Most of the amendments are technical or terminology changes. One reaches savers who buy bills through a bank or brokerage instead of TreasuryDirect. Once Treasury moves to its upgraded auction system, the firm sending in a noncompetitive bid for a customer will have to give Treasury each customer's name and bid amount before the noncompetitive bidding deadline. Under the old text, a firm with a long customer list could send one summary amount and transmit the names and amounts by the close of business on auction day. The rule doesn't touch the $10 million limit on noncompetitive bids, and it keeps the pricing rule the same in substance: noncompetitive bidders pay the same price as every competitive bidder whose bid was awarded.

The rule is the Federal Register document [Sale and Issue of Marketable Book-Entry Treasury Bills, Notes, and Bonds](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/10/15/2026-21069/sale-and-issue-of-marketable-book-entry-treasury-bills-notes-and-bonds), published October 15, 2026 at 91 FR 65456, which amends the Uniform Offering Circular at [31 CFR part 356](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-31/part-356). Auction figures come from Treasury's offering announcements and results for the week of October 13, including the 17-week bill's [announcement](https://www.treasurydirect.gov/instit/annceresult/press/preanre/2026/A_20261013_2.pdf) and [results](https://www.treasurydirect.gov/instit/annceresult/press/preanre/2026/R_20261014_1.pdf), the 13-week bill's [results](https://www.treasurydirect.gov/instit/annceresult/press/preanre/2026/R_20261013_2.pdf), and the [announcement](https://www.treasurydirect.gov/instit/annceresult/press/preanre/2026/A_20261015_5.pdf) for the 13-week bill auctioned October 19. How individuals bid is described on TreasuryDirect's pages on [Treasury bills](https://www.treasurydirect.gov/marketable-securities/treasury-bills/), [how auctions work](https://www.treasurydirect.gov/auctions/how-auctions-work/), [buying a marketable security](https://www.treasurydirect.gov/marketable-securities/buying-a-marketable-security/), [auction FAQs](https://www.treasurydirect.gov/help-center/faqs/auction-faqs/), [when auctions happen](https://www.treasurydirect.gov/auctions/when-auctions-happen/), [reinvesting](https://www.treasurydirect.gov/marketable-securities/reinvesting-a-marketable-security/), [selling](https://www.treasurydirect.gov/marketable-securities/selling-marketable-securities/) and [taxes](https://www.treasurydirect.gov/marketable-securities/tax-forms-and-withholding/), and in its [glossary](https://www.treasurydirect.gov/help-center/glossary/glossary-for-marketable-securities/). Other sources are the Deputy Secretary of the Treasury's [September 22, 2026 remarks](https://home.treasury.gov/news/press-releases/sb0633), [the FDIC's Understanding Deposit Insurance page](https://www.fdic.gov/resources/deposit-insurance/understanding-deposit-insurance), the APY formula in [Appendix A to Regulation DD](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-12/chapter-X/part-1030/appendix-Appendix%20A%20to%20Part%201030), and IRS [Publication 550](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p550). The saver and her dollar amounts are hypothetical; the auction results are real. This is general information about how Treasury auctions work, not investment or tax advice for any person.

## What the October 15 rule changes

The rule's summary states its purpose: "These amendments make technical changes to several sections of the UOC to remove outdated references and to be consistent with Treasury's current auction terminology. These amendments also modify the requirements for submitting noncompetitive customer bids, to require submitters to provide all customer bid information prior to the noncompetitive auction close." The UOC, short for Uniform Offering Circular, is the regulation that "sets out the terms and conditions for the sale and issuance by Treasury to the public of marketable book-entry Treasury bills, notes, and bonds."

The preamble sorts the amendments into five groups:

- **Definitions.** Adjusted value, dated date, index ratio, issue date and settlement amount are reworded. Treasury says, "None of the changes affect the calculation of the adjusted value, index ratio, or settlement amount for inflation-protected securities."
- **Outdated references.** Among them, a reference "to bids in paper form, which are no longer accepted" is removed.
- **Terminology.** References are aligned "more closely to Treasury's current auction terminology," and the rules now "use the words award and recognize in lieu of accept and tender."
- **Noncompetitive customer bids.** This is the change that reaches individual buyers, covered below.
- **Website references.** Two sections, 356.23(a) on auction results and 356.31(a) on STRIPS, no longer name treasurydirect.gov and refer to Treasury's website instead. Section 356.23(a) now reads: "After the conclusion of the auction, we will make the auction results available on our website." Treasury says the change "will provide Treasury more administrative flexibility while maintaining public transparency regarding auction results."

The customer-bid change is in section 356.14(e). Before the amendment, that paragraph opened with the same basic requirement that remains today and then added: "Submitters may either provide the customer's name with the bid or, if the list of customers is lengthy, the submitter may provide a summary bid amount covering all noncompetitive customers. If it provides a summary bid amount, the submitter must transmit the list of individual customers and their bid amounts by close of business on the auction day." It also required that the submitter "be able to provide the customer list details by the noncompetitive bidding deadline if requested."

The amended paragraph is one sentence: "For each noncompetitive bid, the submitter must provide the customer's name and the amount bid." The preamble spells out what goes away: "Submitters will no longer be able to submit a summary bid amount; the individual customer names and respective bid amounts may still be submitted in one consolidated list, but it must be received prior to the noncompetitive auction close." Treasury gives the reason in the same passage. The changes "will ensure all noncompetitive bid information is received prior to the noncompetitive auction close and will enable Treasury auction staff to finalize post-auction operations earlier in the day."

## When it applies

The rule has an effective date and a separate applicability date. The effective date is October 15, 2026. But "The changes to 31 CFR 356.14 and 356.23 apply to all auctions of Treasury marketable securities beginning with the first Treasury marketable securities auction conducted using Treasury's upgraded auction system, which will be communicated to market participants in advance of the first applicable auction. All other changes apply as of the effective date."

The rule itself gives no date for that first auction. It refers to Treasury's announcement in September 2026 of plans to upgrade the auction system. In those remarks, on September 22, Deputy Secretary of the Treasury Francis Brooke said: "Today I am pleased to announce that we plan to transition to the new auction system in the coming months." He described the new system as "currently in the final stages of testing."

There was no comment period. The rule explains that the Administrative Procedure Act exempts matters relating to contracts, and "The auction regulations relate to Treasury securities, which are contracts between Treasury and the owner of the security; therefore, the notice, public comment, and delayed effective date requirements of the APA do not apply to this final rule."

## Whose bids the change covers

Section 356.14 is about bids that one party submits for another. It opens: "Only depository institutions or dealers may submit bids for customers (see definitions at § 356.2), or for customers of intermediaries, under the requirements set out in this section." The definitions in section 356.2 lay out the chain:

- A **customer** is "a bidder that directs a depository institution or dealer to submit or forward a bid for a specific amount of securities in a specific auction on the bidder's behalf."
- A **submitter** is "a person or entity submitting bids directly to us for its own account, for customer accounts, or both."
- An **intermediary** is "a depository institution or dealer that forwards bids for customers to another depository institution or dealer. An intermediary does not submit bids directly to us."

So a saver whose brokerage forwards her order to another dealer, which then bids at the auction, is a customer of an intermediary. The firm that actually submits still has to name her. Section 356.14(c) says submitters "must provide the names of their direct customers as well as customers of any intermediaries who are forwarding customer bids," and that "For individuals, submitters must provide the customer's full name (first and last)."

A purchase in TreasuryDirect is a different route. Section 356.11(c), on securities held directly with Treasury, says: "You must submit your bids in accordance with the regulations governing the system in which the security will be held." TreasuryDirect's own pages say the account takes only one kind of bid: "If you are using your TreasuryDirect account, you must bid non-competitively."

The rule sets what a submitter must send Treasury and by when. It says nothing about the order cutoff a bank or brokerage gives its own customers for an auction, so that's a question for the firm. The outer limit was already in section 356.11, which requires that "competitive and noncompetitive bids" be received "prior to their respective closing times, which are stated in the auction announcement," and adds: "We will not include late bids in the auction. Our computer time stamp will establish the receipt time. You are bound by your bids after the closing time." What the amendment changes is the customer detail behind a firm's noncompetitive bid, which will also have to arrive before that deadline.

## What stays the same

The amendments revise sections 356.2, 356.11, 356.14, 356.15, 356.20, 356.21, 356.23 and 356.31 and parts of appendix B. The sections that set the noncompetitive limits, 356.12 and 356.22, aren't among them.

- **The $10 million limit.** Section 356.12(b)(1): "You may not bid noncompetitively for more than $10 million. The maximum bid limitation does not apply if you are bidding solely through a request to reinvest the proceeds of a maturing security held directly with Treasury, which is a noncompetitive bid." Section 356.22(a) caps the award the same way. TreasuryDirect's glossary describes the limit as applying "for each security type and term, for each auction" and "regardless of what method used to make the purchase (TreasuryDirect, broker, dealer, or financial institution)."
- **One kind of bid per auction.** "You may not bid noncompetitively in an auction in which you are bidding competitively."
- **Who sets the rate.** The amended definition: a noncompetitive bid is, "for a single-price auction, a bid to purchase a stated par amount of securities at the high yield, high discount rate, or high discount margin awarded to competitive bidders."
- **The price rule.** The amended section 356.20(d)(1) reads: "Both noncompetitive and competitive bidders receive the price equivalent to the high yield, high discount rate, or high discount margin at which bids were awarded." The old text said Treasury awards securities to both groups "at the price equivalent to the highest accepted yield, discount rate, or discount margin at which bids were accepted." Only the wording differs.
- **The minimum.** Each of the week's bill announcements lists a "Minimum Bid Amount and Multiples" of $100.

TreasuryDirect describes what a noncompetitive bidder gets in plain terms: "you are guaranteed to get the security you want in the amount you want" and "you agree to accept the discount (high) rate, (high) yield, or (high) discount margin determined at auction." Its auction FAQ adds: "Most individual investors bid noncompetitively."

## How the auction turns bids into one rate

The amended section 356.20(a)(1) sets the order. "After the competitive auction closes, we award noncompetitive bids that were submitted prior to the noncompetitive auction close, subject to the noncompetitive bidding limitations and restrictions described in this part. Then we award competitive bids at the lowest yields, discount rates, or discount margins, through successively higher yields, discount rates, or discount margins, up to the amount required to meet the offering amount."

For bills, a competitive bid is a discount rate. Section 356.12(c) says it "must show the discount rate bid, expressed with three decimals in .005 increments." Only a bank, broker or dealer can enter one for an individual. TreasuryDirect: "To bid competitively, you must use a bank, broker, or dealer."

Treasury fills the offering from the lowest rate up. The rate at which the offering runs out is the high rate. Bids below it are filled completely, bids at it are usually filled in part, and bids above it get nothing. The regulation qualifies the middle case: Treasury prorates at the high rate "When necessary," and section 356.20(a)(2) says the bids at that rate "Generally" add up to more than what's left of the offering. TreasuryDirect lays this out for competitive bidders: a bid less than the rate the auction settles on gets "all you bid for," a bid equal to it gets "some of what you bid for," and a bid above it gets "nothing."

The partial fill is a percentage. Treasury derives it "by dividing the remaining par amount needed to fill the offering amount by the par amount of the bids at the high yield, high discount rate, or high discount margin and rounding up to the next hundredth of a whole percentage point." Awards are then rounded to the bill's bidding multiple. The rule's own example is an $18,000 bid at the high rate when 88.27% is awarded at the high: "We would award $15,900 to that bidder, which is an upward adjustment from $15,888.60 ($18,000 x .8827) to the nearest multiple of $100."

All the awarded bids end up at one price. Treasury's FAQ puts it this way: "In a single-price auction, all successful competitive bidders and all noncompetitive bidders are awarded securities at the price based on the highest rate, yield, or discount margin of awarded competitive tenders." A competitive bidder who asked for less than the high rate still receives the high rate's price. So does the noncompetitive bidder who asked for nothing in particular.

A results release reports the outcome in a few lines. The "High Rate" is the rate that set the price; its footnote says "All tenders at lower rates were accepted in full." "Allotted at High" is the percentage filled at that rate. The "Median Rate" is the rate at or below which 50% of the accepted competitive amount was bid, and the "Low Rate" is the same measure at 5%.

## The 17-week bill, line by line

The 17-week bill from the opening was announced on Tuesday, October 13, auctioned on Wednesday, October 14, and issued on Tuesday, October 20. Its announcement set these terms:

- Offering amount: $75,000,000,000
- Maximum award to any one bidder: $26,250,000,000, which is 35% of the offering
- Maximum noncompetitive award: $10,000,000
- Minimum bid amount and multiples: $100
- Noncompetitive closing time: 11:00 a.m. ET
- Competitive closing time: 11:30 a.m. ET

The results release, dated October 14, gives the rate figures: a high rate of 4.195%, 83.69% allotted at the high, a price of 98.613319, an investment rate of 4.313%, a median rate of 4.175% and a low rate of 4.090%. The dollar lines:

| Category | Tendered | Accepted |
|---|---|---|
| Competitive | $207,901,665,000 | $73,541,490,700 |
| Noncompetitive | $559,073,200 | $559,073,200 |
| FIMA (noncompetitive) | $900,000,000 | $900,000,000 |
| Subtotal | $209,360,738,200 | $75,000,563,900 |
| SOMA | $5,053,976,200 | $5,053,976,200 |
| Total | $214,414,714,400 | $80,054,540,100 |

FIMA and SOMA are the two less familiar lines. FIMA stands for Foreign and International Monetary Authority, and the announcement allows "up to $2,000 million in noncompetitive bids from Foreign and International Monetary Authority not to exceed $500 million per account." SOMA is the [Federal Reserve](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy.htm)'s System Open Market Account, which Treasury's FAQ lists among the entities that bid directly. The announcement marks SOMA amounts as not included in the offering amount, which is why the total accepted is larger than $75 billion.

The noncompetitive line shows the same number in both columns: everything tendered was accepted. On the competitive side, the release breaks out the accepted amount by bidder type: $27,777,600,000 to primary dealers, $5,908,690,000 to direct bidders and $39,855,200,700 to indirect bidders. The FAQ defines an indirect bidder as "a customer placing a competitive bid through a primary dealer or direct submitter." The bid-to-cover ratio, the subtotal tendered divided by the subtotal accepted, is shown as "$209,360,738,200/$75,000,563,900 = 2.79."

To see how the high rate sorts competitive bids, take three hypothetical bids of $1,000,000 each in that auction:

- **At 4.190%,** below the high rate: filled in full, $1,000,000.
- **At 4.195%,** the high rate: filled at 83.69%, or $836,900.
- **At 4.200%,** above the high rate: nothing.

The first two bidders, and the saver with her $20,000 noncompetitive bid, all pay 98.613319 per $100. The bidder who asked for 4.190% doesn't get a lower yield for having asked for one; in a single-price auction, everyone awarded gets the high rate's price.

In every one of the six, noncompetitive bids were accepted in the full amount tendered. And in every one, they were a small slice of the bill sold, from under 1% of the 17-week to about 5% of the 4-week. The rate those savers received was set by the competitive side.

The Deputy Secretary's September remarks give a sense of who is on that side. Describing July and August, when Treasury raised the net supply of bills by more than $550 billion, he said: "Money market mutual funds absorbed approximately 85% of the additional supply, and there was limited pressure on funding markets."

Rates also don't line up neatly by term. The 4-week bill auctioned October 15 had a higher high rate than the 6-week bill auctioned two days earlier. Five of the six were also reopenings, sales of more of a bill already outstanding. The 13-week bill's announcement, for example, lists an original issue date of July 16, 2026 and $82,201,000,000 already outstanding. The 17-week bill was the only new issue, with "Currently Outstanding" shown as $0.

## Deadlines that decide which auction you're in

The closing times aren't fixed in the regulation. TreasuryDirect says each announcement includes "Closing times for non-competitive and competitive bidding," and that's where they're set. Five of the six announcements above used 11:00 a.m. ET for noncompetitive bids and 11:30 a.m. ET for competitive bids. The 6-week bill auctioned October 13 used "12:00 Noon ET" and "1:00 p.m. ET."

The days follow a weekly pattern that TreasuryDirect publishes. The 13-week and 26-week bills are announced on Thursday, auctioned the following Monday and issued on Thursday. The 17-week bill is announced on Tuesday, auctioned Wednesday and issued the following Tuesday. The 4-week and 8-week bills are announced Tuesday, auctioned Thursday of that week and issued the following Tuesday. The pattern can shift; the schedule page notes that "Holidays and special circumstances may also disrupt the schedule," and the 13-week and 26-week auctions of the week of October 12 were held on Tuesday, October 13.

The next 13-week auction was announced on October 15. That announcement set the auction for Monday, October 19, with an offering amount of $95,000,000,000, an issue date of October 22, 2026, a maturity date of January 21, 2027, and the usual 11:00 a.m. and 11:30 a.m. ET closing times.

For a TreasuryDirect purchase, the account shows the result the same day: "On auction day, you can see the results after 5 PM Eastern time." Payment comes from the linked bank account or Certificate of Indebtedness, and TreasuryDirect's instruction is to "Make sure enough money is in your bank account to pay for the security before the issue date for that security." A scheduled rollover has its own earlier deadline: "We close the option to reinvest four business days before the relevant auction."

Neither the rule nor the September remarks gives a start date for the upgraded auction system. For a bank or brokerage customer, the rule's effect arrives with the first auction run on it, which the rule says "will be communicated to market participants in advance of the first applicable auction."

## Three ways to state one bill's return

The 17-week bill can be described with three different percentages, and only one of them is calculated the way a savings account's rate is. Using the saver's $20,000 of face value and the published price of 98.613319:

- **Cost:** $20,000 ÷ 100 × 98.613319 = $19,722.66, after rounding to the cent. That method comes straight from appendix B of the regulation: "divide the par amount by 100 and multiply the resulting quotient by the price per $100."
- **Interest at maturity:** $20,000 − $19,722.66 = $277.34. TreasuryDirect: "For bills, 'interest' is the difference between what you paid and the face value you get when the bill matures."

**The discount rate, 4.195%.** This is the number bidders compete on. The regulation defines it as "a rate of return, on an annual basis, on bills held until they mature," "based on a 360-day year." It's figured on the face value. Appendix B converts it to a price with P = 100 (1 − dr/360), where d is the discount rate and r the days to maturity. For this bill: 100 × (1 − 0.04195 × 119 ÷ 360) = 98.6133194, which rounds to the published 98.613319.

**The investment rate, 4.313%.** The results release footnotes it as "Equivalent coupon-issue yield." The glossary calls it "An annualized simple interest rate based on a 365-day year (366-day basis for a leap year), without compounding." It's figured on the price paid. For a bill of not more than a half-year, appendix B's formula comes to the interest divided by the price, scaled to the year: $277.34 ÷ $19,722.66 × 365 ÷ 119 = 4.313%.

**An APY-style figure, about 4.38%.** Savings accounts and CDs quote an annual percentage yield under Regulation DD, the Truth in Savings rule. Its Appendix A gives the general formula as APY = 100 [(1 + Interest/Principal)^(365/Days in term) − 1] and explains that the APY "measures the total amount of interest paid on an account based on the interest rate and the frequency of compounding." Plugging the bill's numbers into it: 100 × [(1 + 277.34 ÷ 19,722.66)^(365 ÷ 119) − 1] = about 4.38%. Regulation DD governs deposit accounts, not Treasury securities. This conversion is only an illustration, so the bill's return can be stated on the same footing as a deposit's.

The same conversion for the week's six bills, using each auction's published price and term:

| Bill | Investment rate (published) | APY-style figure (calculated) |
|---|---|---|
| 4-week | 4.068% | 4.15% |
| 6-week | 4.055% | 4.13% |
| 8-week | 4.132% | 4.21% |
| 13-week | 4.185% | 4.25% |
| 17-week | 4.313% | 4.38% |
| 26-week | 4.351% | 4.40% |

Read that way, a savings account would have needed to pay about 4.38% APY, at a rate that held steady for 119 days, to match the 17-week bill before taxes. The comparison has limits. A savings account's rate can change during those 119 days; the bill's return is set at auction and fixed until it matures. Then the money is back in hand, or rolled into a new bill at whatever rate that auction sets. Taxes also differ, as covered below, and for a saver who pays state income tax, the gap that matters is the after-tax one. That calculation is laid out in [Treasury Bills vs CDs: The Tax Break That Changes the Comparison](/2026/09/23/treasury-bills-vs-cds-the-tax-break-that-changes-the-comparison/).

## A bill is not a deposit

Moving money from a savings account into a bill changes what protects it. The [FDIC](https://www.fdic.gov/resources/deposit-insurance) says: "FDIC deposit insurance only covers deposits, and only if your bank is FDIC-insured." Its list of products that are not covered includes "U.S. Treasury bills, bonds, or notes," and the page adds that FDIC insurance "does not cover non-deposit investment products, even those offered by FDIC-insured banks." Treasury's own description of its marketable securities is that "All these securities are backed by the full faith and credit of the United States government." For bills held at a brokerage, how the account itself is protected is covered in [Where Brokerage Cash Actually Sits, and What Insures It](/2026/08/30/where-brokerage-cash-actually-sits-and-what-insures-it/).

Getting money out before maturity works differently too. "To sell a Treasury marketable security, you must work through a bank, broker, or dealer." A bill held in TreasuryDirect has to be transferred out first, and new purchases there carry a holding period: "In Treasury Direct, when you buy a Treasury marketable security, you must hold it in your TreasuryDirect account for 45 days before selling or transferring it. This means you can't sell or transfer a 4-week bill from TreasuryDirect because it matures in less than 45 days."

## Taxes and the year the interest lands

The regulation states the tax rule in section 356.32: the securities "are subject to all applicable taxes imposed under the Internal Revenue Code of 1986," and they are "exempt from taxation by a State or political subdivision of a State, except for State estate or inheritance taxes and other exceptions as provided in that section." TreasuryDirect's tax page says what you earn "is subject to federal tax but is exempt from state and local taxes," including "Bill 'interest' (the difference between the price you pay and the face value you get when the bill matures)."

The timing follows maturity. Publication 550: "Generally, you report this interest income when the bill is paid at maturity." It also says you "should receive Form 1099-INT showing the interest (in box 3) paid to you for the year." The 17-week bill from the opening matures on February 16, 2027, so its $277.34 is generally 2027 interest, even though most of its 119 days fall in 2026. The 13-week bill auctioned October 19 matures January 21, 2027, and lands in 2027 the same way. Publication 550 notes that this is the general rule, not the only one. For a short-term obligation, a holder "generally can choose to include any discount and interest payable on the obligation in income currently," and the discount must be included as it accrues for certain holders, including one "Held by an accrual-basis taxpayer." Year-end timing for bills and short CDs is covered in [T-Bills and Short CDs Bought Now: Which Tax Year Gets the Interest](/2026/10/16/t-bills-and-short-cds-bought-now-which-tax-year-gets-the-interest/).

Rolling a bill over doesn't defer the tax. Publication 550: "If you reinvest your Treasury bill at its maturity in a new Treasury bill, note, or bond, you will receive payment for the difference between the proceeds of the maturing bill (par amount less any tax withheld) and the purchase price of the new Treasury security. However, you must report the full amount of the interest income on each of your Treasury bills by the time it reaches maturity."

## Rolling over in TreasuryDirect

A scheduled reinvestment in TreasuryDirect is itself a noncompetitive bid; section 356.12(b)(1) calls a reinvestment request exactly that, and exempts a bid made "solely through a request to reinvest the proceeds of a maturing security held directly with Treasury" from the $10 million limit. Each rollover takes the rate of the auction it lands in. For a bill, the new security's time to maturity "must be the same as the maturing one," and "bills may be scheduled for multiple reinvestments, up to two years," which works out to as many as 6 for a 17-week bill and 7 for a 13-week bill. For bills held at a bank or brokerage, TreasuryDirect's reinvesting page simply says to "contact that bank, broker, or dealer."

## Common questions

**Does the October 15 rule change anything for bills bought in TreasuryDirect?**

The customer-bid paragraph, section 356.14(e), governs bids that a depository institution or dealer submits for customers. A TreasuryDirect purchase is submitted under the rules of that system, as section 356.11(c) says. The rule's other changes apply to all auctions, but none of them changes the $10 million limit, the noncompetitive-only rule in TreasuryDirect or the single price that every awarded bidder pays.

**When does the broker change start?**

With the first auction run on Treasury's upgraded auction system. The rule says that date "will be communicated to market participants in advance of the first applicable auction." The September 22 remarks said Treasury plans the transition "in the coming months."

**Can a noncompetitive bid be filled for less than the amount bid?**

TreasuryDirect says a noncompetitive bidder is "guaranteed to get the security you want in the amount you want," within the $10 million limit. Section 356.20 makes the award "subject to the noncompetitive bidding limitations and restrictions described in this part," which include the limit and the rule against bidding both ways in one auction. In all six bill auctions listed above, the noncompetitive amount accepted equaled the amount tendered.

**Do competitive bidders get a better rate than noncompetitive bidders?**

No. Treasury's FAQ says bills, like its other marketable securities, "are sold at single-price auctions," and in those "all successful competitive bidders and all noncompetitive bidders are awarded securities at the price based on the highest rate, yield, or discount margin of awarded competitive tenders." What a competitive bid adds is the chance to get less than the full amount, or nothing, in exchange for naming a minimum rate.

**Is the investment rate the same as an APY?**

No. The investment rate is simple interest on a 365-day basis "without compounding." An APY under Regulation DD reflects compounding. For the 17-week bill, the published investment rate was 4.313% and the APY-style figure works out to about 4.38%.

**Does the $10 million limit apply per brokerage account?**

TreasuryDirect's glossary describes it as "$10 million for each security type and term, for each auction," and says it applies "regardless of what method used to make the purchase (TreasuryDirect, broker, dealer, or financial institution)." Section 356.12 states it from the bidder's side: "You may not bid noncompetitively for more than $10 million."

For a saver buying bills, the October 15 rule leaves the parts that set the price alone. A noncompetitive bid takes the high rate that competitive bidders set, is filled in full up to $10 million, and pays the same price as every other awarded bid. What changes, once Treasury's new auction system is running, is the back office of a broker-placed bid: the customer's name and amount must reach Treasury before the noncompetitive deadline in the auction announcement, instead of following in a list at the end of the day.
