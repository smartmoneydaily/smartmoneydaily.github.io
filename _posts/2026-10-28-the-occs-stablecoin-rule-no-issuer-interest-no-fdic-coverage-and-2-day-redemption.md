![The OCC's Stablecoin Rule: No Issuer Interest, No FDIC Coverage, 2-Business-Day Redemption](/assets/pin-images/2026-10-28-the-occs-stablecoin-rule-no-issuer-interest-no-fdic-coverage-and-2-day-redemption.png)

On Oct. 21, the Office of the Comptroller of the Currency announced its final rule for payment stablecoins, the digital tokens built to hold a fixed value, usually $1, that a 2025 law, the GENIUS Act, brought under federal regulation. The rule was published in the Federal Register on Oct. 23 at 91 FR 67000. The OCC's release says, "The final rule and the GENIUS Act take effect on January 18, 2027."

Three provisions bear directly on anyone comparing a stablecoin with a savings account. An issuer must not pay a holder "any form of interest or yield (whether in cash, tokens, or other consideration) solely in connection with the holding, use, or retention of such payment stablecoin." Issuers must disclose that their coins are "not deposits, not backed by the full faith and credit of the United States, not subject to deposit insurance by the [FDIC](https://www.fdic.gov/resources/deposit-insurance), and not subject to share insurance by the [National Credit Union Administration](https://ncua.gov/consumers/share-insurance-coverage)." And an issuer's redemption policy must state "that timely redemption may not exceed two business days following the date of the requested redemption."

The final rule also drops a presumption from the OCC's proposal that would have treated certain payments through affiliates and related third parties as prohibited yield. In the preamble, the OCC writes that "after reviewing the comments, the OCC has determined to eliminate the rebuttable presumption from the final rule." It will look at each arrangement on its facts instead.

A savings account or CD at an insured bank works differently on every one of these points. The bank can pay interest, and the deposit is insured up to $250,000 per depositor, per bank, per ownership category. A stablecoin under the new rule can't earn interest from its issuer and isn't insured. What stands behind it is a pool of reserves the issuer must hold and report on every month.

This article is based on the OCC's [final rule](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/10/23/2026-21706/implementing-the-guiding-and-establishing-national-innovation-for-us-stablecoins-act-for-the) and its [Oct. 21 news release](https://www.occ.gov/news-issuances/news-releases/2026/nr-occ-2026-92.html), along with OCC Bulletins [2026-50](https://www.occ.gov/news-issuances/bulletins/2026/bulletin-2026-50.html) and [2026-51](https://www.occ.gov/news-issuances/bulletins/2026/bulletin-2026-51.html); the text of the [GENIUS Act](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/PLAW-119publ27/html/PLAW-119publ27.htm), Public Law 119-27; the FDIC's [April 10 proposal](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/04/10/2026-06974/genius-act-requirements-and-standards-for-fdic-supervised-permitted-payment-stablecoin-issuers-and) on insurance for stablecoin reserves and tokenized deposits, published at 91 FR 18534; the FDIC's [deposit insurance overview](https://www.fdic.gov/resources/deposit-insurance/understanding-deposit-insurance); TreasuryDirect's [Treasury bill page](https://www.treasurydirect.gov/marketable-securities/treasury-bills/); the IRS [digital assets page](https://www.irs.gov/filing/digital-assets); and the list of federal holidays in [5 U.S.C. 6103](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/5/6103). Nothing here is a recommendation to buy, hold or avoid any stablecoin.

## Who the OCC rule covers

The GENIUS Act splits oversight among several regulators. The OCC's rule applies to national banks, federal savings associations and federal branches and their subsidiaries, to foreign payment stablecoin issuers, to nonbank companies that seek approval or are approved as "Federal qualified payment stablecoin issuers," and to some state-qualified issuers. The law assigns subsidiaries of credit unions to the NCUA. Subsidiaries of state-chartered insured banks fall to the bank's own federal regulator, the FDIC or the [Federal Reserve](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy.htm). State-qualified issuers with no more than $10 billion of stablecoins outstanding may opt for a state regime, provided it is "substantially similar to the Federal regulatory framework." An insured bank doesn't issue coins itself under the act; it does so through an approved subsidiary. The reserve, redemption and reporting rules described below apply to permitted payment stablecoin issuers. Foreign issuers offering coins in the U.S. are handled under a separate section, 12 CFR 15.31, with their own conditions.

The FDIC's own proposal, which includes the deposit insurance questions discussed below, was still a proposal as of Oct. 26. No final FDIC rule on the subject had appeared in the Federal Register. The OCC's release quotes Comptroller of the Currency Jonathan V. Gould: "We are providing a clear, practical framework for payment stablecoin innovation, with strong standards for reserve backing, redemption, and risk management."

Starting Jan. 18, 2027, the statute's basic rule applies: "It shall be unlawful for any person other than a permitted payment stablecoin issuer to issue a payment stablecoin in the United States." Existing issuers with a substantially complete application pending by then can ask the OCC for a waiver, for "a period not to exceed 12 months beginning on January 18, 2027," according to Bulletin 2026-51. The OCC says it "generally expects to grant requests for waivers from the Prior Approval Requirement" for existing issuers, so they can keep issuing while their applications are reviewed. It also says it "has not identified compelling, generally applicable reasons to grant waiver requests with respect to requirements of the GENIUS Act or final rule other than the Prior Approval Requirement provision." It will evaluate all waiver requests "on their merits," but the interest ban, the reserve rules and the redemption deadline aren't in the category it expects to grant generally. The OCC will start taking applications once the Office of Management and Budget approves the forms; as of Oct. 26, its newsroom hadn't announced that.

A second date matters for people who buy stablecoins on trading platforms. Under section 3(b) of the act, "beginning on the date that is 3 years after the date of enactment of this Act, it shall be unlawful for a digital asset service provider to offer or sell a payment stablecoin to a person in the United States, unless the payment stablecoin is issued by a permitted payment stablecoin issuer." The act was signed on July 18, 2025, so that date is July 18, 2028. The act provides exceptions, including a separate path for coins from foreign issuers.

## A stablecoin is not a deposit

The act defines a payment stablecoin as a digital asset used, or designed to be used, "as a means of payment or settlement," whose issuer is obligated to redeem it for a fixed amount of money and represents that it will hold a stable value. The definition then excludes several things, including "a deposit (as defined in section 3 of the Federal Deposit Insurance Act (12 U.S.C. 1813)), including a deposit recorded using distributed ledger technology."

Because of that exclusion, deposit insurance doesn't reach a stablecoin, and the stablecoin rules, including the interest ban, don't reach a bank deposit. The OCC said as much in the preamble: "The OCC confirms that the GENIUS Act's yield prohibition does not apply to deposits." It added that "the prohibition does not extend to paying interest on deposits, including deposits in tokenized form."

Section 4(e) of the act sets the insurance status: "Payment stablecoins shall not be backed by the full faith and credit of the United States, guaranteed by the United States Government, subject to deposit insurance by the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation, or subject to share insurance by the National Credit Union Administration." Claiming otherwise is illegal, and the OCC rule repeats that ban for issuers. Issuers also can't use "United States," "United States Government" or "USG" in a coin's name, though "USD" is allowed.

The FDIC's consumer page draws the same line from the bank side. It says FDIC insurance "covers deposits in all types of accounts at FDIC-insured banks, but it does not cover non-deposit investment products, even those offered by FDIC-insured banks." Its list of what isn't covered includes Treasury bills, mutual funds and "Crypto assets." The page adds: "Additionally, FDIC deposit insurance doesn't cover default or bankruptcy of any non-FDIC-insured institution."

## The interest ban

Section 4(a)(11) of the act is short: "No permitted payment stablecoin issuer or foreign payment stablecoin issuer shall pay the holder of any payment stablecoin any form of interest or yield (whether in cash, tokens, or other consideration) solely in connection with the holding, use, or retention of such payment stablecoin."

The OCC wrote the prohibition into its rule at 12 CFR 15.10(c)(4), in nearly the same words, and turned down several requests to soften it.

- **No retail and institutional split.** One commenter wanted issuers to pass reserve income to institutional holders but not retail ones. The OCC's answer: "The GENIUS Act does not distinguish between retail and institutional payment stablecoin holders."
- **No small-payment exemption.** The OCC declined a de minimis exception "because the statutory yield prohibition, by its terms, does not contemplate an exemption for such payments."
- **No cure period.** The OCC won't build in a grace period for violations. It says it will use enforcement discretion "based on the given facts and circumstances."

The OCC also refused to define "interest" or "yield." Its reasoning: "Interest does not stop being interest merely because it is given a different name. An approach that looked to labels rather than economic substance would render the yield prohibition meaningless."

## The presumption that was dropped

The proposal tried to address payments that go through a middleman. It would have presumed that an issuer was paying prohibited yield when two things were true: the issuer had an arrangement to pay interest or yield to an affiliate or a "related third party," and that party had an arrangement to pay interest or yield to holders of the issuer's coins. An issuer could rebut the presumption with written materials.

Comments split sharply. Many commenters wanted a strong ban that would catch indirect payments, and some wanted certain indirect payments barred outright. Others argued that the statute names issuers, not anyone else, and that Congress chose not to ban indirect payments. The preamble summarizes the banks' worry: commenters said "permitting indirect payments could lead to deposit flight that would disproportionately affect community banks and reduce lending."

The final rule removes the presumption but keeps the OCC's power to act. It writes that "the OCC retains its authority to address evasion of section 4 of the Act," and the interest ban is part of section 4. One case is clearly covered, payments made by an agent on the issuer's behalf: "Even if the issuer were not considered to be the payor, it would clearly evade the statutory prohibition on yield for an issuer to merely pay another entity to make an interest or yield payment that the issuer could not make directly."

The OCC also marks the other edge. Indirect payments "that are not made solely in connection with the holding, use, or retention of a payment stablecoin are outside the scope of the prohibition by the Act's own terms." Payments for services are generally outside it too: "Absent additional facts to the contrary, payments for bona fide services generally would not be prohibited because such payments generally are not interest or yield paid solely in connection with the holding, use, or retention of a payment stablecoin." For arrangements in between, the OCC promises a case-by-case review: "Rather, the OCC will apply the language of the statute to the facts and circumstances of any particular arrangement so that any such arrangement can be evaluated based on all relevant facts and circumstances."

Companies other than issuers have offered "rewards" to people who hold stablecoins, and many comments were about those programs. The rule doesn't approve them, and it doesn't ban them by name. On programs that already existed, the OCC was direct: "The OCC declines to presume that rewards programs that predated the GENIUS Act comply with the GENIUS Act." Commenters asked for safe harbors for staking rewards, lending interest, loyalty programs, white-label partners and other arrangements, and the OCC adopted none. Some commenters pointed to pending legislation that could change the interest rules. The OCC's response: "Unless and until such legislation passes, the OCC must implement the GENIUS Act's yield prohibition, as enacted."

For a saver, the label on a payment doesn't settle anything. Whether a platform's reward on a stablecoin balance runs into the issuer's interest ban depends on who is really paying, why and how, and the OCC hasn't said in advance how it will decide. One thing doesn't depend on that answer: a reward on a stablecoin balance isn't interest on an insured deposit, because the coin isn't a deposit.

## Where the reserve income goes

Every coin must be backed. Under 12 CFR 15.11(a), the reserves must be identifiable, kept separate from the issuer's other assets, and must "at all times have a reserve asset value that equals or exceeds the outstanding issuance value." Outstanding issuance value is the total par value of the coins issued.

The permitted reserves are listed in 15.11(b):

- U.S. coins and currency, or money held in an account at a Federal Reserve Bank
- Demand deposits, or other deposits withdrawable on request, and insured shares at an insured depository institution
- Treasury bills, notes or bonds "with a remaining maturity of 93 days or less"
- Cash received under overnight repurchase agreements in which the issuer sells Treasury bills maturing in 93 days or less
- Overnight reverse repurchase agreements collateralized by Treasury securities, subject to conditions
- Shares of registered government money market funds that invest only in the assets above
- Other similarly liquid federal government-issued assets the OCC approves
- Tokenized versions of some of the assets above

Two portfolio limits apply on top of the list. An issuer must keep "at least 5 percent of its reserve assets" as demand deposits, share accounts payable on demand or money at a Federal Reserve Bank. And its exposure to any one counterparty is generally capped at 40% of reserves, with exceptions that include exposure to the U.S. government.

Several of those assets, such as Treasury bills and money market fund shares, earn a return. The rule doesn't say outright who keeps that income, but the reserves are the issuer's assets, and the issuer can't pay holders interest or yield "solely in connection with" holding the coin. So whatever the reserves earn doesn't reach holders through the coin itself. Holders get the redemption promise: one coin for its fixed dollar value.

**A worked example at the issuer level.** Suppose an issuer has $2 billion of coins outstanding and holds $1.9 billion of Treasury bills plus $100 million of demand deposits, which meets the 5% minimum. If the bills earn a hypothetical 4% a year, they produce $76 million a year. Holders receive none of it as interest from the issuer, because the law forbids that payment.

**A worked example for one saver.** Take about $10,000 set aside for a year three ways, using hypothetical round rates.

| Where the $10,000 sits | Paid by the institution | Insurance |
|---|---|---|
| Payment stablecoin | $0 interest from the issuer | None |
| Savings account at a hypothetical 4% APY | $400 over 12 months | FDIC, up to $250,000 per ownership category |
| One 52-week Treasury bill at a hypothetical 4% discount rate | $404.44 at maturity, on a purchase price of $9,595.56 | Not FDIC-insured; the Treasury pays face value at maturity |

The bill's price uses the standard discount formula over 364 days: $10,000 × (1 − 0.04 × 364 ÷ 360) = $9,595.56. So the bill ties up $9,595.56, not $10,000, for the year. TreasuryDirect says that for bills, interest is the difference between the purchase price and the face value paid at maturity, and that the minimum purchase is $100, in $100 increments. Short-term bills are among the assets stablecoin reserves may hold. Someone who owns a bill directly receives its interest; a stablecoin holder doesn't receive the interest on the issuer's bills. Our guide to [how a T-bill auction sets your rate](/2026/10/20/how-a-t-bill-auction-sets-your-rate-noncompetitive-bids-and-the-oct-15-rule/) walks through the pricing. These rates are examples, not quotes; savings rates vary by bank, and bill rates change with every auction.

## Deposit insurance and the FDIC proposal

Reserves held as bank deposits raise a separate question: is any of that money insured for the people holding the coins?

The FDIC proposed an answer on April 10. Its proposal would change the deposit insurance rules in 12 CFR part 330 "by clarifying that deposits held as reserves backing a payment stablecoin would be insured to the PPSI under the FDIC's coverage rules for corporate deposits, but would not be insured to payment stablecoin holders on a pass-through basis." PPSI stands for permitted payment stablecoin issuer.

Pass-through coverage, in the proposal's words, is "a mechanism that allows deposits placed at an IDI by a third party on behalf of one or more owners to be insured as if deposited directly at the IDI by the owner(s)." An IDI is an insured depository institution, such as an FDIC-insured bank. For stablecoins, the FDIC reasoned that treating holders as insured depositors "seems inconsistent with the GENIUS Act's prohibition" on stablecoins being subject to deposit insurance. Our article on [when "FDIC insured" means a partner bank, not your app](/2026/09/08/when-fdic-insured-means-a-partner-bank-not-your-app/) covers how pass-through coverage works for ordinary fintech accounts.

Under the proposal, an issuer's reserve deposits at a bank would be added to its other corporate deposits there, such as an operating account, and insured up to $250,000 in total. Comments were due June 9, 2026, and as of Oct. 26 no final FDIC rule had appeared in the Federal Register. The OCC's final rule stays out of the question: "The OCC declines to opine on pass-through insurance in this rulemaking as that topic is subject to the FDIC's notice of proposed rulemaking that has not yet been finalized."

**A worked example.** An issuer with $2 billion of coins outstanding keeps $300 million of its reserves as demand deposits at one insured bank, within the 40% counterparty limit, and has no other accounts there. Under the FDIC's proposal, coverage would be $250,000, insured to the issuer, which is less than 0.1% of the balance. A person holding $10,000 of that issuer's coins would have no deposit insurance claim at all. By contrast, a saver with $10,000 in a savings account and $240,000 in CDs at one insured bank, all in her own name and with no other accounts there, has $250,000 in the single-account category, which is within the limit. For how categories add up, see [how FDIC ownership categories stack coverage at one bank](/2026/08/21/how-fdic-ownership-categories-stack-coverage-at-one-bank/).

The FDIC describes its Deposit Insurance Fund, or DIF, this way: "The DIF is backed by the full faith and credit of the United States government." No federal fund stands behind a payment stablecoin.

## Tokenized deposits are a different product

A bank can also record deposits on a blockchain, a product often called a tokenized deposit, which can look like a stablecoin on a screen but is a different product in law. Referring to the Federal Deposit Insurance Act, the FDIC's April proposal says "The FDI Act's definition of deposit is technology neutral, and therefore, tokenized forms of deposits are not a separate category of deposits under the statute." The FDIC says that "a depositor using tokenized deposits is afforded the same Federal deposit insurance coverage under the FDI Act as a depositor using non-tokenized deposits," and its proposal would write that principle into the regulations. And because the OCC says the interest ban doesn't apply to deposits, a bank's tokenized deposit could pay interest.

The FDIC adds a warning: "Although a tokenized deposit is a deposit, there may be tokenized bank liabilities that are not deposits, irrespective of an intention or representation that such constitute a deposit." A token with a bank's name on it isn't automatically insured. What counts is whether the product meets the legal definition of a deposit.

## The two-business-day redemption rule

Redemption is how a holder turns a coin back into dollars from the issuer. Section 15.12 of the rule sets the timing and disclosures.

The clock starts once the issuer has received a redemption order from a customer and, if its onboarding procedures are reasonably designed not to unduly delay onboarding, the customer has been onboarded and other legal conditions are met. Redemption counts as done when the issuer "has fully processed and initiated the outgoing transfer of monetary value" to the customer or the customer's financial institution, or, if the customer asks, credited the customer's account with the issuer. The deadline is about when the issuer sends the money, not when it lands in your bank account.

The redemption policy must be public. It must state "that timely redemption may not exceed two business days following the date of the requested redemption," and that discretionary limits on redemptions can be imposed only by regulators. It must also give the minimum redemption amount, "provided that the issuer must redeem any number greater than or equal to one payment stablecoin, subject to appropriate customer screening and onboarding." The rule defines a business day as "any day other than a Saturday, Sunday, or Federal holiday."

**A worked example.** A customer who has already been onboarded with an issuer submits a redemption order on Friday, Feb. 12, 2027. Under 5 U.S.C. 6103, Washington's Birthday falls on "the third Monday in February," which in 2027 is Feb. 15. Counting business days after Feb. 12, Tuesday, Feb. 16 is the first and Wednesday, Feb. 17 is the second. So the issuer must have processed the redemption and initiated the transfer by Wednesday, Feb. 17, unless the deadline is extended as described below. If the issuer charges a disclosed $10 redemption fee, a $10,000 redemption would return $9,990.

Two limits matter. First, the rule defines a customer as someone who buys from the issuer directly or makes a redemption request directly to the issuer. Someone who bought coins on a trading platform can ask the issuer directly but may first have to be onboarded, or can sell the coins at whatever price the market offers. The OCC says the onboarding condition is meant to ensure an issuer "cannot use deficiencies in its onboarding process as a pretext to artificially extend the timeline for redemption." The deadline applies to the issuer's redemptions, not to a platform's withdrawal times.

Second, the deadline can stretch under stress. An issuer that gets redemption requests above 10% of the outstanding issuance value of any one brand of coin in a single 24-hour period must notify the OCC within 24 hours and may ask for more time. The OCC can grant or deny the extension, or impose limits on redemption itself. For a coin with $2 billion outstanding, requests above $200 million in a 24-hour period would cross that line.

Fees must be disclosed "publicly, clearly, and conspicuously," and a fee change requires at least seven calendar days' notice.

## The monthly reserve report

Section 15.11(e) requires each issuer to publish a monthly reserve report. "By noon on the last day of each month," the issuer must post the composition of its reserves "as of noon on the last day of the previous month." The report follows an OCC template showing the coins outstanding and each reserve category, with average tenor and where it is held.

A registered public accounting firm must examine each month-end report, and the firm's report must be posted at the same time. The chief executive and chief financial officers must certify each month to the OCC that the report is accurate, and the act makes a knowingly false certification a crime. The template has separate lines for "Insured deposits" and "Uninsured deposits," followed by Treasury securities, repurchase agreements, money market fund shares and tokenized reserves.

**A worked example of reading one.** Say a report published by noon on Feb. 28, 2027, shows figures as of noon on Jan. 31, 2027, in thousands of dollars:

| Line | Amount (thousands) |
|---|---|
| Outstanding payment stablecoins | 2,000,000 |
| Insured deposits | 750 |
| Uninsured deposits | 399,250 |
| Treasury bills, notes or bonds | 1,200,000 |
| Reverse repurchase agreements | 300,000 |
| Government money market fund shares | 110,000 |
| Total reserve assets | 2,010,000 |

Total reserves of $2.01 billion against $2 billion of coins is a ratio of 100.5%. Of the $400 million on deposit, $750,000 is insured, which is under 0.2%. That would fit an issuer holding deposits at three banks, with $250,000 insured at each under the FDIC's proposal. The report is also about a month old by the time it appears. A savings account statement tells you what the bank owes you. A reserve report tells you, with that lag, what the issuer held against everyone's coins.

## If an issuer fails

Section 11 of the act sets the order of payment in an issuer's insolvency. "The claim of a person holding payment stablecoins issued by the permitted payment stablecoin issuer shall have priority, on a ratable basis with the claims of other persons holding such payment stablecoins, over the claims of the permitted payment stablecoin issuer and any other holder of claims against the permitted payment stablecoin issuer, with respect to required payment stablecoin reserves."

Put simply, holders as a group come first on the reserves and share them in proportion to their coins. In a Bankruptcy Code case, the act's amendments exclude required reserves from the bankrupt estate, though the automatic stay still applies to them. If holders can't be paid in full from the reserves, the rest of their claim gets first priority against the estate, "to the extent compliance with section 4 of the GENIUS Act would have required additional reserves to be maintained." The court is to use "best efforts" to enter an order to begin distributions within 14 days after the required hearing. That target runs from the hearing, not from the failure, and it is a goal for the court, not a payment deadline.

**A worked example.** An issuer fails with $2 billion of coins outstanding. After a loss, its reserves are worth $1.96 billion, which is 98% of what is owed. A holder of $10,000 in coins would be entitled to a ratable $9,800 from the reserves. The remaining $200 becomes a claim against the issuer's estate with first priority, to the extent the issuer should have held more reserves. Whether that $200 is paid depends on what else the estate holds. A $10,000 deposit at a failed insured bank, by comparison, is within FDIC coverage.

## Holding a stablecoin on a platform

Many people never hold a stablecoin in their own wallet; a trading platform or app holds it for them. The act's custody section is narrower than that arrangement. It covers firms that safekeep "the payment stablecoin reserve, the payment stablecoins used as collateral, or the private keys used to issue permitted payment stablecoins." The OCC's version, at 12 CFR 15.20, reaches coins used as collateral only "where a covered custodian has actual knowledge that such payment stablecoins are being used as collateral." Within that scope, a custodian must treat the property as the customer's and "take such steps as are appropriate to protect" it "from the claims of creditors of the person."

A stablecoin balance simply sitting on a trading platform isn't on that list. What happens to it if the platform fails depends on the platform's account terms and other law, which this rule doesn't address. None of it is deposit insurance.

## Taxes and reporting

Banks report savings and CD interest on Form 1099-INT. Stablecoins fall under the rules for digital assets, which the IRS defines as "any digital representation of value recorded on a cryptographically secured, distributed ledger (blockchain) or similar technology." Its list of examples includes stablecoins.

Form 1040 and several other federal returns ask a version of this question: "At any time during the tax year, did you: (a) receive (as a reward, award or payment for property or services); or (b) sell, exchange, or otherwise dispose of a digital asset (or a financial interest in a digital asset)?" The IRS says to check "Yes" if you received digital assets as "A reward or award," among other cases. A saver who gets a platform reward paid in stablecoins, or sells stablecoins for dollars, would answer yes. The IRS also says broker reporting of digital asset sales "is required to be made on Form 1099-DA beginning with transactions on or after Jan. 1, 2025," and that for certain stablecoin sales, brokers may report on an aggregate basis above de minimis thresholds. The OCC rule doesn't address taxes.

## Common questions

**Does the final rule ban rewards that a trading platform pays on my stablecoin balance?**

The rule doesn't answer that in general terms. The OCC dropped the presumption that would have treated some affiliate and third-party payments as prohibited. It kept its power to act against evasion and said it will judge each arrangement on its facts, and that programs predating the act aren't presumed to comply. As of Oct. 26, no list of permitted programs existed.

**If the issuer keeps reserves at an FDIC-insured bank, is my coin insured?**

No. The act says stablecoins aren't subject to deposit insurance, and the OCC rule requires issuers to say so. Under the FDIC's April proposal, reserve deposits would be insured only to the issuer, as corporate deposits up to $250,000 in total at each bank, and not to holders on a pass-through basis. As of Oct. 26, that proposal hadn't been finalized.

**How quickly can I get dollars back?**

The rule requires the issuer to process the redemption and initiate the transfer within two business days after the order, once you are an onboarded customer and other legal conditions are met. When the money reaches your bank is a separate question. The OCC can extend the deadline if redemption requests for a coin brand exceed 10% of its outstanding value in a single 24-hour period. On a trading platform, you would usually sell at the market price instead, under the platform's terms.

For cash a household relies on, the comparison comes down to a few points. An insured savings account or CD can pay interest and is backed by the FDIC up to the limit. A payment stablecoin under the new rule pays no interest from its issuer and has no deposit insurance. It is backed by reserves reported monthly with a lag, and the issuer must process a customer's redemption within two business days.
