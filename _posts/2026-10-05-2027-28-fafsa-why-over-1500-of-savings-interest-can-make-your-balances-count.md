A parent sitting down this month to fill in the 2027-28 FAFSA for a high school senior usually has two questions about the family's savings: will the form ask about them at all, and if it does, how much will they count? The short answer comes from the federal statute. Under its income-and-schedules test, a dependent student's family with parents' adjusted gross income under $60,000 skips asset reporting only if the parents did not file Schedule A, B, D, E, F or H (and either filed no Schedule C or filed one within a $10,000 gain or loss) with the return for the second preceding tax year, and the IRS requires Schedule B once taxable interest or ordinary dividends go over $1,500. A well-funded high-yield savings account can produce that much interest on its own. (Two other routes to the exemption, covered below, do not depend on tax schedules.) Once assets are in the picture, the statute counts parents' assets above an asset protection allowance at 12%, and that figure is then added to the parents' income and assessed again. A dependent student's assets count at 20% and go straight into the Student Aid Index. For 2027-28 the asset protection allowance, which the statute's base table sets above zero for most parent ages, is $0 at every age.

None of that tells a family what its aid will be. It does explain which balances belong on the form, whose name each one goes under, and why a savings account that throws off a few extra hundred dollars of interest can change which questions get asked. The rules below come from the Federal Student Aid announcement, three sections of the Higher Education Act as printed in the U.S. Code 2024 edition, the Department of Education's June 2, 2026 notice and the IRS instructions for Schedule B.

## The 2027-28 FAFSA is open now

Federal Student Aid posted its announcement, [2027–28 FAFSA Form Now Available](https://financialaidtoolkit.ed.gov/tk/announcement-detail.jsp?id=fafsa-updates), on its Financial Aid Toolkit with a date stamp of Sept. 23, 2026. The announcement says students "can complete the form to apply for financial aid for attending college or career/trade school between July 1, 2027, and June 30, 2028." That window is the 2027-28 award year, and it is the reference point for every date-based rule that follows.

Two other lines from the announcement matter for families who started early. Students who "successfully submitted their FAFSA form for the 2027–28 academic year during the early testing period" do not need to resubmit it. And on timing, the announcement says "Most FAFSA forms are processed immediately. Some can take one to three days." Once a form is processed, the student can review the FAFSA Submission Summary, and the information goes to the schools the student selected.

The award year matters because the statute does not look at this year's tax return. In the need analysis definitions at [20 U.S.C. 1087vv](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-2024-title20/html/USCODE-2024-title20-chap28-subchapIV-partF-sec1087vv.htm), "total income" is built from "adjusted gross income for the second preceding tax year," and the asset-reporting exemption uses the same phrase for the tax schedules a family filed. Counting back two years from an award year that begins July 1, 2027 points to the 2025 tax year. The Schedule B instructions quoted later in this post are the IRS's 2025 instructions, the ones that applied to 2025 returns.

So the form uses two different reference points. Income, and the tax-schedule test for skipping asset questions, come from a past return. Account balances and other asset values, when they are asked about at all, are measured at a different point: "the market value at the time of application," in the words of the statute. The income figures are fixed in the past, while account balances can change until the day the form is completed.

## Who is exempt from asset reporting

The exemption lives in [20 U.S.C. 1087ss](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-2024-title20/html/USCODE-2024-title20-chap28-subchapIV-partF-sec1087ss.htm), subsection (b). It opens with this instruction: "the Secretary shall not use asset information from an eligible applicant or, as applicable, the parent or spouse of an eligible applicant." The rest of the subsection defines who counts as an eligible applicant. An applicant needs to meet only one of the criteria.

For a dependent student, three of them apply:

1. **Automatic zero or negative SAI.** The applicant "qualifies for an automatic zero student aid index or negative student aid index" under another section of the law, 20 U.S.C. 1087mm. The income tests in that section are outside the scope of this post.
2. **The income-and-schedules test.** The student's parents "have a total adjusted gross income (excluding any income of the dependent student) that is less than $60,000 and do not file a Schedule A, B, D, E, F, or H (or equivalent successor schedules) with the Federal income tax return for the second preceding tax year." On top of that, the parents either do not file a Schedule C for that year, or they file one "with net business income of not more than a $10,000 loss or gain."
3. **A means-tested benefit.** The applicant, or the applicant's parent or spouse as applicable, "at any time during the previous 24-month period, received a benefit under a means-tested Federal benefit program."

The statute's list of means-tested programs includes Supplemental Security Income, SNAP, TANF, WIC, Medicaid and federal housing assistance, plus other programs the Secretary finds approximately consistent with their income eligibility rules.

There is one carve-out that cuts the other way. Under paragraph (3), a dependent student's family is not exempt if the parents do not "reside in the United States or a United States territory" or do not "file taxes in the United States or a United States territory," unless the nonfiling happens because the parents were not required to file "due to a low income."

The second criterion involves more than the $60,000 figure. The statute defines each schedule by what it reports:

| Schedule | What the statute says it reports | Effect on the test |
|---|---|---|
| A | Itemized deductions | Rules it out |
| B | Interest and ordinary dividend income | Rules it out |
| C | Income or loss from a business or profession run as a sole proprietor | Allowed only within a $10,000 loss or gain |
| D | Sales, exchanges or some involuntary conversions of capital assets, certain capital gain distributions, nonbusiness bad debts | Rules it out |
| E | Income from rental properties, royalties, partnerships, S corporations, estates, trusts and residual interests in real estate mortgage investment conduits | Rules it out |
| F | Farm income and expenses | Rules it out |
| H | Household employment taxes | Rules it out |

Any one of these schedules is enough to fail the test. A family with AGI well under $60,000 that itemized deductions, sold some shares, reported rental income on Schedule E, or paid a household employee can fail this test for reasons that have nothing to do with savings. A family that tripped none of those can still fail it on Schedule B alone. Meeting one of the other criteria, such as a means-tested benefit in the previous 24 months, would still qualify the family, because one criterion is enough.

## How Schedule B gets triggered at $1,500

The IRS sets the Schedule B filing rules in its [Instructions for Schedule B (Form 1040)](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i1040sb). The 2025 instructions list several situations that require the schedule. The first is the one that matters for savers: "You had over $1,500 of taxable interest or ordinary dividends."

Two words in that sentence matter most. "Over" means a family with exactly $1,500 of taxable interest is not caught by that line; the schedule is required once the amount goes past it. And "taxable interest" is the total that appears on the return, from every account that paid it, added up. The sentence does not set a separate line for each account. A family with three modest accounts at three banks can cross the line even though no single 1099-INT looks large. For couples who hold a joint account, how that interest is reported on a return is covered in [Joint Savings Account, One 1099-INT: Who Reports the Interest and How](https://smartmoneydaily.github.io/2026/09/30/joint-savings-account-one-1099-int-who-reports-the-interest-and-how/).

The $1,500 line is not the only route onto Schedule B. The same instructions list other triggers, and a few of them show up in college-saving households:

- Interest received from a seller-financed mortgage where the buyer used the property as a personal residence.
- Accrued interest from a bond.
- Claiming "the exclusion of interest from series EE or I U.S. savings bonds issued after 1989." A family that claimed that exclusion on a 2025 return filed Schedule B for that reason.
- Receiving interest or ordinary dividends as a nominee.
- Having "a financial interest in, or signature authority over, a financial account in a foreign country," or a connection to a foreign trust as the instructions describe.

Any one of them means a Schedule B was part of the return, and the statute's test asks only whether the parents filed one.

The connection to the FAFSA is the statute's own definition. Section 1087ss(b)(4)(B) says "The term 'Schedule B' means a form or information filed by a taxpayer to report interest and ordinary dividend income." Read together, the two documents connect like this: more than $1,500 of taxable interest in 2025 meant a Schedule B on the 2025 return, and a Schedule B on that return removes the second criterion for skipping asset reporting on the 2027-28 FAFSA, even if the parents' AGI was below $60,000.

## How much savings produces $1,500 of interest

The interest figure is a product of two things: the balance and the yield. The table below shows the balance needed to cross $1,500 at several assumed yields.

Here is the arithmetic, using assumed round yields chosen only to show the math, not quotes for any real account. Annual percentage yield already folds in compounding, so a balance held for a full year at a stated APY earns that percentage of the balance.

| Assumed APY, held all year | Balance that earns exactly $1,500 | What it takes to go "over $1,500" |
|---|---|---|
| 2% | $75,000 | Any balance above $75,000 |
| 3% | $50,000 | Any balance above $50,000 |
| 4% | $37,500 | Any balance above $37,500 |
| 5% | $30,000 | Any balance above $30,000 |

Each row is $1,500 divided by the yield: $1,500 ÷ 0.02 = $75,000, $1,500 ÷ 0.03 = $50,000, $1,500 ÷ 0.04 = $37,500 and $1,500 ÷ 0.05 = $30,000. Real accounts rarely sit at one balance and one rate for 12 months, so the actual interest on a 1099-INT can differ from a simple calculation. The point of the table is scale: an emergency fund and a down payment kept in one high-yield account can produce more than $1,500 of interest a year.

**Worked example: one account versus two.** Take a married couple filing jointly with 2025 AGI of $54,000 who filed no other schedules. Assume they kept $40,000 in an online savings account paying an assumed 4% APY for all of 2025.

- Interest: $40,000 × 0.04 = $1,600.
- $1,600 is over $1,500, so the 2025 instructions required Schedule B.
- Under 20 U.S.C. 1087ss(b)(2)(B), filing Schedule B means the second criterion is not met. AGI under $60,000 does not cure it.

Now change one fact. Suppose the same couple held $28,000 in that account at the same assumed 4% APY.

- Interest: $28,000 × 0.04 = $1,120.
- $1,120 is not over $1,500, so the interest line alone did not require Schedule B.
- If nothing else on the 2025 return required Schedule B or any of the other listed schedules, and their AGI stayed under $60,000, the second criterion could be met, and under the statute the Secretary "shall not use asset information" from that family.

The same logic covers interest spread over several accounts. If the couple instead had $18,000 at an assumed 4% and $9,000 in a CD at an assumed 3%, the interest is $18,000 × 0.04 + $9,000 × 0.03 = $720 + $270 = $990, still under the line. What matters for Schedule B is the total on the return.

For the 2027-28 form, the 2025 return is already filed, so the useful step is to read it: whether a Schedule B was attached, and why. Low AGI alone does not settle the question.

## What the statute counts as an asset

When asset information is used, the definition is broad. Section 1087vv(f)(1) says "assets" means "the amount in checking and savings accounts, time deposits, money market funds, investments, trusts, stocks, bonds, derivatives, securities, mutual funds, tax shelters, qualified education benefits (except as provided in paragraph (3)), the annual amount of child support received and the net value of real estate, vacation homes, income producing property, and business and farm assets." One exclusion follows in paragraph (2): "the term 'assets' shall not include the net value of the family's principal place of residence."

Sorted into the categories a saver would recognize, the list looks like this:

| Category | Items the statute names | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Cash and deposits | Checking and savings accounts, time deposits, money market funds | Time deposits include CDs |
| Market investments | Investments, stocks, bonds, derivatives, securities, mutual funds | Net value, per subsection (g) |
| Trusts and shelters | Trusts, tax shelters | Named directly in the definition |
| Education savings | Qualified education benefits: 529 plans, state prepaid tuition plans, Coverdell education savings accounts | Whose asset depends on paragraph (3) |
| Child support | The annual amount of child support received | Listed as an asset, not as a balance |
| Property | Real estate, vacation homes, income producing property | Net value: market value minus debt |
| Home | The family's principal place of residence | Excluded |

**Time deposits.** The [Federal Reserve](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy.htm)'s Regulation D, at [12 CFR 204.2](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-12/chapter-II/subchapter-A/part-204/section-204.2), defines a time deposit as one the depositor cannot withdraw from within six days of deposit unless the deposit carries an early withdrawal penalty of at least seven days' simple interest on the amount withdrawn, and says it may be represented by a certificate. A CD fits that description, so a CD ladder built for a tuition bill is an asset on the same footing as a savings account. Locking money up for a term does not change that.

**Child support.** The definition counts "the annual amount of child support received." That is unusual: it treats a stream of payments as an asset item. A parent who receives child support should expect the annual amount to be part of the asset picture when assets are reported.

This post does not cover how a small business or family farm is valued; the statute quoted here is the U.S. Code 2024 edition, and the treatment of those assets should be checked in the current FAFSA instructions. Retirement accounts and custodial accounts for minors are also outside the scope of this post. Families with any of those should look to the current FAFSA instructions or a school's financial aid office.

## Whose asset a 529 plan is

Paragraph (3) of the definition answers a common question: whose name does a 529 balance go under? The statute says a qualified education benefit "shall be considered an asset of— (A) the student if the student is an independent student; or (B) the parent if the student is a dependent student and the account is designated for the student, regardless of whether the owner of the account is the student or the parent."

Paragraph (4) defines a qualified education benefit as "a qualified tuition program (as defined in section 529(b)(1)(A) of title 26) or other prepaid tuition plan offered by a State" and "a Coverdell education savings account (as defined in section 530(b)(1) of title 26)."

For a dependent student, then:

- A 529 owned by a parent, with the student as the designated beneficiary, is a parent asset.
- A 529 owned by the student, with the student as beneficiary, is also a parent asset under the statute's wording, "regardless of whether the owner of the account is the student or the parent."
- The same rules apply to a state prepaid tuition plan and a Coverdell account.

For an independent student, a qualified education benefit is the student's asset.

The paragraph is specific about what it covers: an account "designated for the student," owned by the student or the parent. It does not address, in that text, an account a parent holds for a different child or an account owned by someone other than the student or a parent. Families with those arrangements should check the current FAFSA guidance or ask the aid office rather than assume.

The classification matters because parent and student assets run through different formulas, covered two sections below.

## Net value, measured at the time of application

The statute does not ask for a year-end statement or an average balance. Section 1087vv(g) defines "net value" as "the market value at the time of application of the assets (as defined in subsection (f)), minus the outstanding liabilities or indebtedness against the assets."

**Timing.** For accounts, investments and property, the net value is measured as things stand when the form is completed, not from a tax return and not from last December's statement. Balances move daily and investments move with prices, so those figures are the ones on the day the form is filled in. The income side of the form looks back to 2025; account balances and market values are taken as of the application date. Child support is the one item in the definition stated differently, as "the annual amount of child support received," so it is an annual figure rather than a balance on that day.

**Debts against an asset.** Net value subtracts "the outstanding liabilities or indebtedness against the assets." The plainest example is real estate other than the home. Assume a family owns a vacation cabin with a market value of $240,000 and an outstanding mortgage of $150,000 on it.

- Net value: $240,000 − $150,000 = $90,000.
- That $90,000 is the asset figure, not the $240,000 sale value.

The definition pairs each debt with the asset it is "against." It does not describe subtracting general household debts from the total. Families unsure whether a particular loan counts as indebtedness against a particular asset can check the current FAFSA instructions. The statute's rule is a measurement date, and the figures reported should be the true values on that date.

## The 12% and 20% rates, and a $0 protection allowance

The assessment rules sit in [20 U.S.C. 1087oo](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/USCODE-2024-title20/html/USCODE-2024-title20-chap28-subchapIV-partF-sec1087oo.htm), the section that determines the Student Aid Index for a dependent student.

For parents, subsection (d) says "the parents' available assets are equal to— (i) the difference between the parents' assets and the asset protection allowance (determined in accordance with paragraph (2)); multiplied by (ii) 12 percent." It adds that the result "shall not be less than zero."

For the student, subsection (h) says "The student's assets are determined by calculating the assets of the student and multiplying such amount by 20 percent, except that the result shall not be less than zero."

The two numbers do not land in the same place. Subsection (a) builds a dependent student's SAI from three pieces: "the assessment of the parents' adjusted available income," "the assessment of the student's available income," and "the student's available assets." Under subsection (b), the parents' 12% figure is added to the parents' available income to form adjusted available income, and that combined amount then runs through an assessment schedule. The student's 20% figure goes into the SAI directly as its own piece.

The statute's base table for the asset protection allowance rises with the age of the older parent, but the Secretary revises it each year. The Department of Education's notice for this award year, [Federal Need Analysis Methodology for the 2027-28 Award Year](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/06/02/2026-10986/federal-need-analysis-methodology-for-the-2027-28-award-year-federal-pell-grant-federal-work-study), published in the Federal Register on June 2, 2026, describes the allowance as one that "protects a portion of NW (assets less debts) from being considered available for postsecondary educational expenses." Its table for parents of dependent students and for independent students lists every age, from "25 or less" through "65 or older," with an allowance of 0 for married and 0 for single. The notice also describes adjusted available income as "a measure of a family's financial strength, which considers both income and assets."

With the allowance at $0, the parent formula for 2027-28 reduces to parents' assets × 12%. No first slice of savings is shielded by age.

**Worked example: parent and student assets.** Assume a dependent student's parents report, on the day they complete the form, a $4,000 checking balance, $38,000 in savings, a $10,000 CD and a $22,000 529 plan with the student as beneficiary. The student has $3,000 in a savings account of their own.

| Item | Whose asset | Amount |
|---|---|---|
| Checking | Parent | $4,000 |
| Savings | Parent | $38,000 |
| CD (time deposit) | Parent | $10,000 |
| 529 plan designated for the student | Parent, under 1087vv(f)(3) | $22,000 |
| Parents' assets | | $74,000 |
| Student's savings | Student | $3,000 |

The arithmetic:

- Parents: ($74,000 − $0 allowance) × 0.12 = $8,880 in parents' available assets. That $8,880 is added to the parents' available income to form adjusted available income, which is then assessed under the schedule in the June 2 notice.
- Student: $3,000 × 0.20 = $600 in student's available assets, which enters the SAI directly.

Per $1,000: $1,000 of parent assets adds $1,000 × 0.12 = $120 to adjusted available income, while $1,000 of student assets adds $1,000 × 0.20 = $200 directly to the SAI. The $120 is not the end of the parent calculation. The June 2 notice assesses adjusted available income at 22% from −$8,900 to $22,600, rising in steps to 47% on the amount over $45,600, and sets a flat −$1,958 below −$8,900. For a family whose adjusted available income sits in the 22% band, the $120 becomes $120 × 0.22 = $26.40; in the 47% band it becomes $120 × 0.47 = $56.40. This post does not translate the parents' $8,880 into a final SAI, because that depends on the family's income, taxes and allowances, none of which are part of this example.

## Reporting accurately on the day you complete the form

A parent filling in the 2027-28 form can work through the asset questions in this order.

**Check the exemption first, using the 2025 return.** Look at the parents' 2025 federal return. Was AGI under $60,000? Was any Schedule A, B, D, E, F or H attached? Was a Schedule C attached, and if so, was net business income within a $10,000 loss or gain? Separately, did the student or a parent receive a benefit from one of the listed means-tested programs at any time in the previous 24 months? Meeting any one criterion is enough under the statute, unless the paragraph (3) rule for parents who live or file taxes outside the United States applies.

**If Schedule B is there, find out why.** The reason might be interest over $1,500, ordinary dividends over $1,500, a savings bond interest exclusion, a foreign account, or another trigger from the IRS list. Whatever the reason, for this exemption route what matters is whether the parents filed the schedule.

**List every account by owner.** Checking, savings, money market funds, CDs, brokerage holdings, trusts, 529 plans and Coverdell accounts. Put each one under the parent or the student according to the statute's rules, with a dependent student's 529 under the parent.

**Use values from the day of completion.** Pull balances and market values on the day the form is filled in. For property other than the home, subtract only the debt against that property.

Two cautions. First, nothing here is a reason to move money around before completing the form; the statute's job is to measure what a family has, and the figures belong on the form as they are. Second, the documents read for this post cover the federal formula only. For anything a particular school or state program asks for, check with that school's financial aid office.

## Common questions

**Our AGI was under $60,000. Why is the FAFSA still asking about our savings?**

Income below $60,000 is only half of the second criterion in 20 U.S.C. 1087ss(b). The parents also must not have filed Schedule A, B, D, E, F or H with the 2025 return, and any Schedule C must show net business income within a $10,000 loss or gain. A Schedule B filed because taxable interest went over $1,500 is enough to fail that test. The family could still be exempt through another criterion, such as an automatic zero SAI or receipt of a listed means-tested benefit in the previous 24 months.

**Does a 529 plan count against a dependent student more than a parent's savings account?**

No. Under 20 U.S.C. 1087vv(f)(3), a 529 designated for a dependent student counts as a parent asset, "regardless of whether the owner of the account is the student or the parent." That puts it in the same 12% parent formula as the parents' checking, savings and CDs. For 2027-28, with the asset protection allowance at $0, the whole net balance goes into that calculation. The 20% student rate applies to assets that are the student's own, which under the statute does not include that 529.

**Which balance should we report: today's, or the one on our year-end statement?**

The statute defines net value as "the market value at the time of application" minus debts against the asset. That points to the values on the day the form is completed, not a December statement or the interest figures on a tax return. Balances change with deposits, withdrawals and market prices, so pull them on the day you fill in the form.

**Is the family home counted?**

No. Section 1087vv(f)(2) says assets "shall not include the net value of the family's principal place of residence." Other real estate is a different matter: vacation homes, other real estate and income producing property are listed as assets at net value. Net value is market value minus the outstanding debt against that property. Mortgage debt on the principal residence does not enter the asset figure, because the home itself is excluded.

**What is the asset protection allowance for 2027-28?**

It is $0. The Department of Education's notice published June 2, 2026 lists an allowance of 0 for both married and single parents at every age from "25 or less" through "65 or older." The same single table applies to parents of dependent students and to independent students. With a $0 allowance, the statutory parent formula becomes parents' assets × 12%, with a floor of zero.

**Where does the 2025 tax year come from, if the form is for 2027-28?**

The statute bases income and the tax-schedule test on the "second preceding tax year." The 2027-28 award year covers attendance from July 1, 2027 through June 30, 2028, according to the Federal Student Aid announcement. Counting back two years from 2027 gives 2025. That is why the relevant Schedule B rule is the IRS's 2025 instruction about taxable interest or ordinary dividends over $1,500.
