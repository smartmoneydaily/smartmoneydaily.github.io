For six months after a depositor dies, the FDIC insures that person's accounts as if the owner were still alive. The rule takes up a short paragraph in the deposit insurance regulations, and it matters most in the weeks when a family is still deciding what to do with the money.

The IRS put out a short checklist for survivors on Nov. 5, [Tax Tip 2026-80](https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/loss-of-a-loved-one-steps-to-manage-tax-responsibilities). It covers who handles the person's tax matters, how to get old returns and transcripts, and the point that the person's own final return and the estate's return are separate filings. It doesn't get into bank accounts, which is where a lot of the paperwork starts. This post fills in that part from the 2025 edition of the IRS's longer guide for survivors, [Publication 559](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p559), and the FDIC's coverage rules.

## The six-month grace period at the bank

The rule is 12 CFR 330.3(j): "The death of a deposit owner shall not affect the insurance coverage of the deposit for a period of six months following the owner's death unless the deposit account is restructured." It adds that the grace period can't reduce coverage, and that if an account isn't restructured within six months, coverage is figured on actual ownership.

What a family does with the account during those months can change how much of it is insured. Say a parent leaves $200,000 in a single-owner savings account, and the son or daughter who inherits it already holds $150,000 in single accounts with no beneficiaries at the same bank. While the parent's account is still insured as the parent's, both balances are fully covered. If the inherited money is moved into another single account in the heir's own name, the heir's single-account total there becomes $350,000. The limit for that category is $250,000, so $100,000 sits uninsured until some of it moves to another bank or another ownership category.

Two more details from the [FDIC's guide to insured deposits](https://www.fdic.gov/resources/deposit-insurance/brochures/insured-deposits/) belong here:

- An account established for or representing a deceased person's funds, which the FDIC calls a decedent's estate account, is insured in the single-account category. It's added together with the deceased person's other single accounts at the same bank.
- The grace period follows the death of an owner, not a beneficiary. When a beneficiary on a payable-on-death account dies, coverage usually drops right away. The FDIC's example is a mother with $500,000 in a POD account naming her two children, insured up to $500,000 while both are alive and up to $250,000 after one dies.

None of this is a reason to hurry. If anything, it argues for adding up what an heir already holds at a bank before retitling a large balance there.

## Which return gets the interest

Pub 559 starts from the person's accounting method. Most individuals use the cash method, according to the IRS's Publication 538, and under it the final Form 1040 includes only income the person actually or constructively received before death. The credit date matters. Interest the bank credited on July 31 belongs on the final return of someone who died in August. Interest the bank credits after the date of death generally doesn't, even when part of it built up while the person was alive. It goes on the return of whoever receives it, either as income earned after death or as what the IRS calls income in respect of a decedent.

Who receives it depends on how the account was set up. A payable-on-death account goes to the named beneficiaries, and a joint account with survivorship rights stays with the surviving owner. Money in a trust account follows the trust's terms, and the trust may keep holding it after the death. Who reports that interest then depends on the trust income tax rules, which look at whether the income is distributed or required to be distributed. An account in the decedent's name alone, with no beneficiary designation or trust arrangement, generally becomes part of the estate. Pub 559 tells the personal representative to get an employer identification number for the estate as soon as possible and give it to the bank, because payers report interest under the number of whoever the account is payable to. A surviving joint owner gives the bank their own taxpayer identification number, such as a Social Security number or ITIN.

Banks don't always split the year cleanly. When a Form 1099-INT in the decedent's name includes interest earned after death that belongs to the estate or to someone else, Pub 559 says to ask the payer for a form that shows the right recipient and amount. If that isn't possible, or if the form includes income in respect of a decedent, the final return reports the full amount on Schedule B, subtracts the other recipient's share and labels the subtraction "Nominee Distribution" or another fitting description.

## A $60,000 account, worked through

The numbers are made up to show the mechanics. Say a saver starts 2026 with $60,000 in a single-owner account paying 4% APY, makes no deposits or withdrawals, and dies on Aug. 14. Interest is credited at the end of each month and left in the account, each credit figured at the monthly rate that compounds to 4% over a year and rounded to the cent. There's no joint owner, beneficiary or trust, so the account becomes part of the estate and stays there through Dec. 31.

- The credits from Jan. 31 through July 31 come to about $1,388.54. They go on the final 1040.
- The credits from Aug. 31 through Dec. 31 come to about $1,011.45. They belong to the estate.

Pub 559 says a domestic estate with gross income of $600 or more for a tax year has to file Form 1041, and this account alone crosses that line. If even one beneficiary is a nonresident alien, the estate files regardless of how much income it had. The $600 exemption is a separate thing. It comes off the estate's taxable income, applies in full even for a short first year, and doesn't change the filing test.

If the personal representative picks a calendar year, the estate's first tax year ends Dec. 31, 2026, and its Form 1041 is due April 15, 2027. A calendar year isn't required, though. The first tax year can end on the last day of any month as long as it doesn't run past 12 months, and the personal representative makes that choice when filing the first Form 1041.

Now run the same five months at the [FDIC's national average rate](https://www.fdic.gov/national-rates-and-rate-caps) for savings, which was 0.38% as of October 19, 2026. Starting from the same $60,000 in January, the estate earns about $95.11 from this account over those months. The 4% account earns more than ten times as much interest, and in this example it's also what creates the Form 1041 filing requirement.

## Savings bonds come with an election

Series EE and Series I bonds work differently, because many owners never report the interest until they cash them. Pub 559's rule covers bonds owned by a cash-method taxpayer who chose not to report the interest each year and bought the bonds entirely with personal funds. For those, the person required to file the final return can elect to put all the interest earned before death on that return, and whoever gets the bonds then reports only the interest earned after death. Without the election, the interest earned before death is income in respect of a decedent, and the new owner reports all of it. A new owner who is also on the cash method and doesn't report the interest yearly can wait until the bonds are cashed or mature, whichever comes first.

This is where the choice can matter. If the final return doesn't itemize, it gets the full standard deduction no matter when in the year the person died. For 2026, the basic standard deduction is $16,100 for a single filer and $32,200 on a joint return, according to the IRS's inflation adjustments for 2026. A single person who died in March may have three months of income set against a full year's deduction. In that case I'd run the tax both ways, since bond interest that fits under the deduction can end up taxed lightly or not at all on the final return, while an heir who cashes the bonds later pays at the heir's own rate.

Pub 559's own example is a $1,000 Series EE bond bought for $500, with $94 of interest built up at the date of death. With no election, the heir who cashes it at maturity reports $500 of interest. With the election, the $94 goes on the final return and the heir later reports $406.

Cashing the bond brings a reporting step of its own. The Form 1099-INT an heir gets shows all the interest since purchase, and it isn't reduced by what the final return already picked up. Pub 559 says to enter the full amount on Schedule B and subtract the earlier part, labeled "U.S. Savings Bond Interest Previously Reported."

## Records, refunds and the April deadline

Tax Tip 2026-80 lists the ways to get the person's records. Form 4506 gets a copy of a filed return, with a fee for each one. A transcript is free. It can be requested online and is mailed to the person's address of record, or it can be requested with Form 4506-T. To send the person's IRS mail to the representative, the tip points to Form 8822 with either Form 2848 or Form 56 attached. Pub 559 adds that Form 56 should be filed as soon as the needed information, including the estate's EIN, is available.

The final return is due when the person's return would have been due anyway, generally April 15 of the year after death. For a 2026 death that's April 15, 2027. A surviving spouse can usually file it as a joint return, but not after remarrying before the end of the year of death. If the person died early in the year before filing the prior year's return, that return is still due on its normal date, and the personal representative files it.

Refunds take one more form. A refund claim needs Form 1310, with two exceptions in Pub 559. A surviving spouse filing a joint return doesn't need it, and neither does a court-appointed representative who attaches the court certificate to the original return.

The federal estate tax return, Form 706, is a different filing from the estate's Form 1041. For a death in 2026, it's required when the gross estate, plus adjusted taxable gifts and any specific gift tax exemption, comes to more than $15,000,000. A smaller estate files one anyway if it elects to pass the unused exclusion to a surviving spouse, the election Pub 559 calls portability.

## What I'd do in the first month

If I were handling a parent's accounts, I'd do three things before moving any money. I'd get the estate's EIN, file Form 56 once that number exists, and give the EIN to each bank holding an account that passes to the estate. I'd write down the date six months after the death. And I'd add up what each heir already keeps at the same banks before anything gets retitled. Keep the year's interest records and match them against the 1099s when they arrive, but the six-month FDIC date is the one most likely to slip past a family that's busy with everything else.
