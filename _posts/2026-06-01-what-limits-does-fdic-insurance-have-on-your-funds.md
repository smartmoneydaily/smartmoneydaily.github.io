The number everyone remembers is $250,000. The words that decide your actual coverage are the ones printed after it: per depositor, per insured bank, per ownership category. Two households holding identical balances at the same bank can end up with very different amounts insured, purely because of how the accounts are titled.

This post takes those three phrases apart one at a time, then runs a household's accounts through the arithmetic so you can see where the limit gets applied and in what order. After that come the specific points where coverage stops without anything on your statement changing, what the 2024 trust rule revision means for older formulas, and the trade-off in splitting a balance across two banks.

Nothing here replaces the FDIC's own estimator. The point is to understand the calculation well enough to know when you need to run it.

## Three words that do the work

**Per depositor.** The limit attaches to you as a person, not to each account you open. A second savings account at the same bank in the same name adds no coverage.

**Per insured bank.** The limit attaches to the insured institution — the charter — not to the brand on the website. One institution can operate more than one consumer brand, and deposits across those brands can land in the same $250,000 bucket. The FDIC's BankFind tool shows which names belong to which insured institution, and that lookup is the only reliable way to settle it.

**Per ownership category.** Titling puts an account into a category, and the categories are insured separately from each other at the same bank. Within a category, your accounts are added together first and the limit is applied to that sum.

The order matters: add up, then cap. Not cap each account.

## The categories, one at a time

| Ownership category | Whose limit applies | Coverage at one insured bank |
|---|---|---|
| Single | The one owner | $250,000 for all single accounts combined |
| Joint | Each co-owner separately | $250,000 per co-owner for their share of all joint accounts |
| Certain retirement accounts | The account owner | $250,000 for the deposit balances combined |
| Revocable trust or payable-on-death | Rules revised effective April 1, 2024 | Use the FDIC's EDIE estimator |

A joint account with two co-owners holding equal shares therefore supports up to $500,000 of coverage in the joint category at one bank: $250,000 against each owner's half. Credit unions follow the same standard limit through the NCUA.

Two clarifications that change answers. The retirement category covers the deposit balances inside the account — a CD or savings balance held in an IRA — and not the securities held there, which are investments and carry no deposit insurance. And a category applies per bank, so the same person can hold a fully insured single-name balance at each of three different insured institutions without any of the three affecting the others.

## A worked example at one bank

Alex and Sam keep everything at a single insured bank.

- Alex, single-name savings: **$180,000**
- Sam, single-name savings: **$140,000**
- Alex and Sam, joint accounts — a checking account plus a CD: **$300,000**

Total on deposit: $180,000 + $140,000 = $320,000, and $320,000 + $300,000 = **$620,000**.

Now apply the categories.

- Single category, Alex: $180,000, under the $250,000 limit, fully insured.
- Single category, Sam: $140,000, under the limit, fully insured.
- Joint category: each co-owner's share is $300,000 ÷ 2 = $150,000, under the limit, so both halves are fully insured.

All $620,000 is insured at one bank. No second bank, no restructuring.

Change one number. Suppose the joint balance is **$620,000** rather than $300,000.

- Each co-owner's share: $620,000 ÷ 2 = $310,000.
- Insured per co-owner in the joint category: $250,000. Uninsured per co-owner: $310,000 − $250,000 = $60,000.
- Uninsured in total: $60,000 × 2 = **$120,000**. Cross-check: $620,000 − ($250,000 × 2) = $620,000 − $500,000 = $120,000.

Household totals in that second version: deposits of $180,000 + $140,000 + $620,000 = **$940,000**, of which $180,000 + $140,000 + $500,000 = **$820,000** is insured. The two sides agree, because $820,000 + $120,000 = $940,000.

The single-name accounts never entered the joint calculation. That separation is what "per ownership category" buys you.

## The point where coverage stops

Coverage can lapse with no notice on the statement.

- **Growth past the line.** Interest posts, a transfer lands, a CD matures into savings, and a category total that was under $250,000 is now over it. Insurance is measured against the balance at the moment a bank closes, not the balance on the day you opened the account.
- **Two brands, one charter.** If two accounts you think of as separate banks belong to the same insured institution, they share one limit in each category. BankFind settles that in under a minute.
- **A beneficiary designation assumed to add coverage.** The FDIC revised its trust account rules effective April 1, 2024. Any per-beneficiary formula from before that date should not be applied to a current account without checking.
- **A change in who owns the account.** The FDIC publishes rules on what happens to insurance after an account owner dies. Check the current guidance rather than assuming coverage carries on unchanged.
- **Products that are not deposits.** Investments bought through a bank do not sit in a low-coverage category; they sit in no category at all. Deposits at a bank that fails are insured. Investments are not.

## Trust accounts after the 2024 change

The rules covering trust accounts — revocable, payable-on-death, and the irrevocable side — were revised effective April 1, 2024. Guidance written before that date may describe a per-beneficiary multiplication, and applying such a formula to a current account produces a number you cannot rely on.

The instruction here is short. Do not reconstruct trust coverage from memory or from an undated article. Enter the actual account titles and beneficiaries into the FDIC's EDIE estimator, which applies the rules in force, then file the result with your account paperwork so the next person who looks gets the same answer.

## The cost of splitting a balance

Back to the household with $120,000 uninsured. There are two fixes: retitle at the same bank so the money lands in a category with room, or move the excess to a second insured bank.

Retitling costs nothing, but it is not a paperwork trick. The titling has to reflect actual ownership, and your deposit agreement spells out what rights a co-owner has to the balance. Read that document before adding a name to anything.

Moving money has a measurable cost only when the two banks pay different rates. Using FDIC national average rates as of August 17, 2026, on that $120,000 over one year:

- At the national average savings rate of 0.38% APY: $120,000 × 0.0038 = **$456.00**.
- At the national average 12 month CD rate of 1.71% APY: $120,000 × 0.0171 = **$2,052.00**.
- Difference: $2,052.00 − $456.00 = **$1,596.00**.

If both banks pay the same rate, splitting the balance changes your interest by $0.00 and your insured total by $120,000. If the second account is a CD, the larger figure comes with a term commitment and an early withdrawal penalty stated in the disclosure, often quoted as a number of months of interest. Those national averages are FDIC figures across insured institutions as of that date — a yardstick, not an offer. The rate on any particular account is whatever that bank's disclosure says.

One detail worth noting in the same data: on August 17, 2026 the national average for a 12 month CD (1.71% APY) sat above the 24 month average (1.57% APY) and the 36 month average (1.34% APY). A longer term did not automatically carry a higher average rate.

## A short audit you can run

- List your institutions by legal name, then check each in BankFind, or the NCUA's tool for credit unions.
- Group balances by charter, then by ownership category, and compare each group's sum against $250,000.
- Recheck any group sitting within about 10% of the limit, since posted interest and incoming transfers move the number.
- Run EDIE for anything involving beneficiaries or trusts, and keep the output with your records.

The cap is fixed. What varies is how many separate $250,000 buckets your accounts legitimately occupy, and that is a titling question you can answer today.
