A balance above $250,000 at one bank is partly uninsured. That is uncomfortable rather than catastrophic in most cases, and there are three practical ways to fix it. They differ in effort, in yield, and in how much paperwork lands in your lap.

## Route one: ownership categories

The $250,000 limit applies per depositor, per insured bank, per ownership category. Using more than one category at the same bank multiplies coverage without moving any money.

A married couple at one bank can reach $1 million with no unusual structures: two single accounts at $250,000 each, and a joint account insured to $500,000, $250,000 per owner. Adding payable-on-death beneficiaries extends coverage further, since trust coverage runs per owner per beneficiary up to five beneficiaries.

The advantages are that nothing leaves the bank and no third party is involved. The limits are that it depends on your family structure, and that beneficiary designations have consequences beyond insurance. A POD designation passes the account outside a will, which is either exactly what you want or a problem for your estate plan, depending on the plan.

## Route two: deposit networks

Several networks let one bank relationship spread deposits across many institutions. IntraFi's programs are the most widely used, with CDARS for certificates and ICS for demand and savings balances.

The mechanics: you deposit at your bank, the network places funds at other member banks in increments below $250,000, and each placement is insured at the receiving bank. You get one statement, one relationship, and one rate.

What it costs is yield. The network takes a spread, and the rate is typically below what you would get shopping directly among the same banks. What it saves is administration, particularly for balances that would otherwise require opening and monitoring eight or ten separate accounts.

Two details matter. You cannot hold a direct deposit at a bank the network places money into, or the balances combine; networks generally let you exclude institutions for this reason. And the service is offered through participating banks rather than directly, so availability depends on your bank being a member.

## Route three: leave the banking system

Treasury securities are backed by the federal government without any dollar limit, which sidesteps the coverage question rather than solving it.

Short-term Treasury bills bought through a brokerage or directly through TreasuryDirect carry no cap. Interest is exempt from state and local income tax, which raises the effective yield in a high-tax state relative to a bank account paying the same nominal rate.

The trade is liquidity and mechanics. A T-bill held to maturity returns cash on a known date; selling early means a market price. Buying at auction takes a few days to settle. For a large balance that has no near-term claim on it, a short bill ladder is frequently the simplest answer and the highest after-tax yield.

Money market funds holding government paper are a fourth variant, with daily liquidity, no [FDIC](https://www.fdic.gov/resources/deposit-insurance) insurance, and no dollar limit, since the protection comes from what the fund holds rather than from an insurance scheme.

## Choosing between them

The decision usually comes down to how long the money is staying and how much administration you will tolerate.

| Situation | Practical answer |
|---|---|
| Large balance, short horizon, want simplicity | Deposit network |
| Large balance, family structure available | Ownership categories at one or two banks |
| Large balance, no near-term need, high-tax state | Short Treasury ladder |
| Temporary spike from a house sale or inheritance | Network or Treasuries, not a new account sprawl |

A temporary excess deserves mention, since it is the most common version of the problem. Proceeds from a sale sitting for six weeks before closing on the next purchase do not justify restructuring anything. The exposure is real but brief, and a network placement or a T-bill maturing near the closing date handles it without leaving a permanent structure behind.

## What not to do

Splitting a balance between two accounts at the same bank does not help. The limit is per depositor per bank across all accounts in the same ownership category, not per account.

Adding a family member's name to reach a higher joint limit changes legal ownership of the money. That person can withdraw it, their creditors can reach it, and the transfer may have gift tax implications. Insurance coverage is not a good reason to give someone ownership of your money.

And the FDIC's own coverage estimator settles most of these questions in a few minutes, using your actual account titling rather than a general rule.

## What happens to uninsured money in a failure

Being partly uninsured is not the same as losing the excess, and the historical record is worth knowing before restructuring anything in a panic.

When a bank fails, the FDIC either arranges for a healthy bank to assume the deposits or pays out insured amounts directly. In an assumption, the acquiring bank frequently takes on all deposits, insured and uninsured, and depositors experience no loss at all. In a payout, uninsured depositors receive a receivership certificate and are paid from the proceeds as assets are sold, over months or years, at a recovery rate that depends on what the bank held.

Recovery on uninsured deposits has historically been substantial in many resolutions and incomplete in others. It is a real risk, and it is a risk of delay and partial loss rather than a certainty of total loss.

That framing helps size the response. A six-week overage while a house sale settles is a modest exposure. A permanent seven-figure balance sitting uninsured at one institution is a structural choice worth fixing.

## Business and organizational accounts

The limit applies to the depositor, and for a business the depositor is the entity.

A corporation, partnership, or LLC is insured to $250,000 for its deposits at one bank, separately from the personal accounts of its owners, provided the entity is engaged in an independent activity rather than existing solely to increase coverage. A sole proprietorship is not a separate depositor; those deposits combine with the owner's personal single accounts.

This is where operating balances create exposure quickly. A small business running payroll through one account can exceed the limit routinely without ever thinking of itself as holding a large balance. Deposit networks are widely used for exactly this reason, since spreading an operating balance across banks manually is impractical when the balance moves weekly.

Nonprofits and government depositors have their own treatment, and public unit deposits follow separate rules that do not map onto the personal categories.

## The estimator settles it

Nearly every question in this area is answered faster by running the numbers than by reasoning about the rules. The FDIC publishes a coverage estimator that takes your actual account titling, ownership category, and beneficiary count and returns the insured and uninsured amounts.

Two minutes there beats an hour of interpreting the categories, particularly for households with a mix of joint accounts, POD designations, and retirement accounts at the same bank.
