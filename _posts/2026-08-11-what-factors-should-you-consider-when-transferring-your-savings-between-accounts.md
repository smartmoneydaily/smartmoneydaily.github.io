Moving savings from one account to another is one of the few money decisions you can compute in advance. The rate difference, the fees, the days in transit, and any early-withdrawal penalty are all knowable numbers. You can write them on the back of an envelope and get a yes or a no.

The rate comparison is the easy part. The money leaks out of the parts around it: a minimum balance you cannot consistently hold, a monthly fee that quietly outweighs the extra interest, a CD penalty bigger than the gain, or a consolidated balance that slips past insurance coverage without anyone noticing.

Every rate used below as a reference point is an [FDIC](https://www.fdic.gov/national-rates-and-rate-caps) national average as of August 17, 2026 — an average across reporting institutions, not an offer available to you. Your own numbers go in the same slots.

## The Rate Gap in Dollars

Percentage points sound larger than they are. Convert them to dollars before you decide anything, using one line: balance x (new APY - old APY) = extra interest per year.

Take $25,000 sitting in a savings account earning the national average of 0.38% APY. That is $25,000 x 0.0038 = $95.00 a year. Move it into a [money market account](/2026/08/22/money-market-account-vs-money-market-fund-whats-insured/) earning the money market national average of 0.63% APY and it becomes $25,000 x 0.0063 = $157.50 a year. The gap is $62.50 a year, or $5.21 a month.

That $62.50 is the number every other factor below gets subtracted from.

## The Cost of the Move Itself

Take the transit cost first, because it is the smallest number in this whole exercise. At 0.38% APY, $25,000 earns about 26 cents a day. If the transfer takes two or three business days and the money earns nothing while in flight, you gave up roughly 52 to 78 cents. Against a $62.50 annual gain, transit time is noise. Do not pay a wire fee to avoid it.

The real costs of the move are operational. A transfer can be rejected when the name on the two accounts does not match, so check both before you link them. Micro-deposit verification can add days before you are able to move anything. Your bank may also cap a single ACH transfer or a day's total, which turns a large move into several smaller ones spread over a week; that limit sits in the transfer agreement or on the transfer screen, so look it up before you start rather than after the first attempt bounces off it.

Then there is the mistake that shows up on nobody's rate comparison: closing the old account too early. One autopay that hits a closed account can produce a returned-item fee, a late fee, and a missed payment. Reroute each direct deposit and each automatic debit one at a time, leave the old account open with a cushion, and confirm on two full statements that nothing is still arriving there before you close it.

## Breaking a CD Early

A CD differs from savings because leaving early has a stated price. That price is written in your disclosure, usually as a number of months of interest. Read your own paperwork for two things: how many months, and whether the penalty can come out of principal when the interest you have accrued does not cover it.

### The break-even test

Say you hold $10,000 in a 12-month CD at 1.71% APY, the FDIC national average for 12-month CDs on August 17, 2026, used here as an illustration. A full term would pay $10,000 x 0.0171 = $171.00. A penalty of three months of interest is $171.00 x 3/12 = $42.75.

Break it at the six-month mark and you have accrued roughly $171.00 x 6/12 = $85.50. Subtract the $42.75 penalty and you keep $42.75. Half of what you earned went to the penalty.

Now the test that decides it. To come out ahead, the replacement account has to earn back that $42.75 on $10,000 over the six months remaining, on top of whatever the CD would have paid you. That works out to $42.75 / $10,000 / 0.5 = 0.00855, which is 0.855%, or about 0.86 percentage points of extra APY, sustained for the entire six months.

On those same national averages, a money market account paid 0.63% — that is 1.08 percentage points *below* the 12-month CD, not above it. Breaking that CD to sit in a money market account would lose twice: once on the penalty, again on the lower rate. The arithmetic only turns positive when the replacement genuinely beats the CD by a wide margin and holds it.

Waiting usually wins on numbers like these. Let the CD mature, then redirect the money. Check your disclosure for the grace period after maturity and whether the CD renews automatically, because an automatic renewal locks you into a new term while you are still deciding.

## Fees, Minimums, and Rate Tiers

Look for three separate minimums in the disclosure, and do not assume they are the same number: the minimum to open, the minimum to earn the advertised APY, and the minimum to avoid the monthly maintenance fee. An account can accept your deposit, pay you a lower tier rate, and charge you a fee, all while honoring exactly what it advertised.

Tiered rates deserve a direct question. If an account pays its top rate above a threshold, ask whether that rate applies to the whole balance or only to the portion above the threshold. The two answers produce very different results on a balance near the line.

Then run the fee against the gain. Using the earlier example, a $5 monthly maintenance fee is $60 a year against a $62.50 annual improvement, leaving $2.50. You would have moved $25,000 for the price of a coffee. Check the account you are leaving behind for per-item and dormancy fees as well.

## APY, Fixed Rates, and Variable Rates

APY includes the effect of compounding; a stated interest rate does not. Compare APY to APY and the comparison is honest. Compare an APY on one page with a bare interest rate on another and you are not comparing the same thing.

The second distinction is durability. Savings and money market rates are variable, so the bank can change them, and the rate that attracted you is not a commitment. A CD's rate is fixed for its term. You are rarely comparing two static numbers; you are comparing a locked number with one that can move underneath you.

That is also why the length of a lock deserves a lookup rather than an assumption. The full CD term structure on one day:

| CD term | National average APY (Aug 17, 2026) |
|---|---|
| 1 month | 0.22% |
| 3 months | 1.14% |
| 6 months | 1.41% |
| 12 months | 1.71% |
| 24 months | 1.57% |
| 36 months | 1.34% |
| 48 months | 1.27% |
| 60 months | 1.36% |

On that date the averages peaked at 12 months and were lower at every longer term, so a longer commitment did not buy a higher average rate. Check the current averages, and the terms your own bank is quoting, before you tie money up for years on the assumption that longer pays more.

## Insurance After Consolidation

FDIC standard coverage is $250,000 per depositor, per insured bank, per ownership category. Credit unions are insured by the [NCUA](https://ncua.gov/consumers/share-insurance-coverage) at the same standard limit. Ownership categories — single accounts, joint accounts, certain retirement accounts, revocable trust accounts — are insured separately from each other, and each co-owner's interest in the joint category is separately insured up to the standard limit.

Consolidation is where this bites. Three balances at three banks, each comfortably covered, can become one balance at one bank that is not. Before you combine, add up everything you would hold at the destination bank in the same ownership category, including any CD you forgot about, and check it against the limit. If it goes over, split the move or use a different ownership category rather than accepting uninsured exposure for a fraction of a percentage point.

Two more points. Deposits at a bank that fails are insured; investments are not, which matters if the higher yield you are chasing turns out to be a fund rather than a deposit. And if you are moving into an app that sweeps balances to partner banks, find out which institution actually holds the deposit and in whose name, because that is where your coverage is counted.

## Access You Would Be Trading Away

A rate is only half the account. Regulation D's six-transfer-per-month limit was suspended in 2020, so any limit on your savings or money market account now comes from the deposit agreement rather than from federal rule. Find the number and the fee for exceeding it before you move.

Ask what you are giving up in reach: branch access, ATM network, wire fees, whether checks are available, how fast an outgoing transfer settles. If this money is your emergency fund, an account you cannot reach on a Sunday is not doing the job you hired it for, whatever it pays. Some accounts also attach conditions such as a direct deposit requirement or a minimum number of transactions to keep the advertised rate, and a condition you will not reliably meet is a rate you will not reliably get.

## A Checklist Before the Transfer

Run these six lines and let the last number decide:

- Write down the old APY, the new APY, and the balance, then compute balance x (new APY - old APY) for the annual gain.
- Subtract twelve months of any monthly fee you would owe on the new account.
- Subtract any early-withdrawal penalty on what you are leaving.
- Confirm the minimum required to *earn* the rate, not just to open the account.
- Confirm the total you would hold at the destination bank stays inside $250,000 per ownership category.
- Send a small test transfer first, and keep the old account open through two statement cycles.

If the number that survives all six is still positive and the access still fits how you use the money, move it. If it is close to zero, the paperwork is not worth it, and you have your answer just as clearly.
