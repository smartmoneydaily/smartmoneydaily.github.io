Sell a position or deposit money into a brokerage account and the cash lands somewhere. Where it lands is a setting, usually chosen when the account was opened and rarely looked at again, and the three common destinations differ in what protects them and how much they pay.

The gap between the default option and the best available option at the same firm is frequently more than a percentage point.

## The three places cash goes

**A bank sweep.** The brokerage moves your cash to one or more partner banks as an ordinary deposit. It is [FDIC](https://www.fdic.gov/resources/deposit-insurance) insured at each destination bank, subject to the standard $250,000 per depositor, per bank, per ownership category. This is the default at most large brokerages, and it is usually the lowest-paying option, because the spread between what the bank pays the broker and what the broker passes to you is a meaningful revenue line.

**A money market fund.** Your cash buys shares in a fund holding short-term instruments. This is a security, not a deposit. There is no FDIC insurance. SIPC protection applies to the custody of the shares, not to their value. Yields are typically closer to short-term market rates than sweep rates are.

**A free credit balance.** Cash held by the broker, awaiting instruction, often paying little or nothing. SIPC covers cash held for securities transactions up to $250,000 within its overall $500,000 limit per customer.

## What SIPC does and does not do

SIPC is routinely misread as an investment version of FDIC insurance. It is not.

SIPC protects against the failure of the brokerage firm, specifically against securities or cash going missing when a member firm fails. It covers up to $500,000 per customer, including a $250,000 sublimit for cash.

It does not protect against a fund losing value. If a money market fund's share price fell, SIPC would be irrelevant. If the brokerage collapsed and your fund shares were still there, SIPC's job would be seeing them transferred to another firm, not making up any market loss.

FDIC insurance answers a different question: what happens if the bank holding a deposit fails. Both can be true in a sweep arrangement, with SIPC covering the brokerage relationship and FDIC covering the destination bank, and neither covers investment losses.

## Program banks and the limit

Sweep programs usually spread cash across a list of program banks, precisely so that balances above $250,000 can stay insured. A program with eight banks can carry $2 million for a single owner.

Two things can undermine that.

If you already hold deposits at one of the program banks directly, those balances combine with the swept portion at the same bank against the same limit. The program has no way to know about your outside account. Brokerages publish their program bank lists and generally let you exclude a bank; that exclusion is the fix.

And the last increment of a large balance sometimes exceeds the program's total capacity, at which point the excess is held as an uninsured free credit balance or moved to a fund. The disclosure describes what happens at the top of the range.

## The rate gap

Sweep rates and money market fund yields at the same brokerage can differ substantially, and the difference is largest on small balances, where sweep tiers pay their lowest rates.

On $50,000 held for a year, the difference between a sweep paying under one percent and a fund yielding near short-term market rates runs into four figures. Nothing about the account changes except the setting.

Some brokerages sweep to a money market fund by default; some default to a bank program and require you to buy the fund yourself, sometimes with a manual purchase each time cash arrives. Automatic reinvestment of idle cash into a chosen fund is a setting worth locating.

## What to check on your own account

Four questions, and the answers are all in the account's cash management disclosure:

1. What is the current destination of uninvested cash, whether sweep, fund, or credit balance?
2. What rate is that paying right now, as opposed to when the account was opened?
3. If it is a bank sweep, which banks are on the program list, and do you hold deposits at any of them elsewhere?
4. What alternatives does the platform offer for the same cash, and can they be made automatic?

For cash that is genuinely waiting to be invested in a few days, none of this is worth much attention. For a balance that has been sitting for months because it never got a decision, it is the difference between the cash working and the cash sitting.

## The alternatives to a sweep

For cash that will sit more than a few days, a brokerage account offers instruments that are not sweep options at all.

**Treasury bills** bought at auction or on the secondary market pay short-term government rates with no dollar limit on the backing and a state tax exemption on the interest. A bill maturing on a known date is the cleanest match for cash with a known use.

**Ultra-short bond funds and ETFs** hold slightly longer paper and accept a small amount of price movement in exchange for a bit more yield. These are not cash equivalents and can lose value, which makes them wrong for money needed at a specific moment and reasonable for money with a flexible horizon.

**Money market funds** sit between the two, with daily liquidity and a share price that is stable by policy rather than by guarantee.

The right comparison for each is the after-tax yield, not the headline. In a high-tax state, the Treasury exemption changes the ranking between a bill and a bank sweep more than a tenth of a point of stated yield does.

## When a sweep changes without asking you

Brokerages modify sweep arrangements, and the notice arrives as a disclosure update rather than a decision point. Changes worth watching for include a new default destination for new accounts, a change to the program bank list, revised rate tiers, and in some cases a reclassification of existing balances.

Existing customers are usually left in place, but not always, and a program bank added to the list can create an overlap with deposits you hold directly at that bank without any action on your part.

The practical habit is to look at the cash line once a quarter rather than once at account opening. The rate on that line changes with the market, the destination changes with the firm's arrangements, and neither generates the kind of notification that interrupts anyone's day.

## A note on multiple accounts at one firm

Insurance limits apply per depositor per bank per ownership category, and the sweep does not change that logic. Cash swept from an individual taxable account and cash swept from an IRA at the same firm may land at the same program bank, and whether those combine depends on the ownership category each falls into.

Retirement accounts sit in their own category, so an IRA sweep does not combine with an individual account's sweep at the same bank. Two individual accounts at the same firm do combine. A joint account is a third category again. The arithmetic is the same one that applies at a bank, applied to destinations you did not personally choose.
