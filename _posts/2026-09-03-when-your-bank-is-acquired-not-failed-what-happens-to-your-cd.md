Bank failures get attention. Bank mergers get a letter in the mail. The second is far more common, and it changes your accounts in ways that are easy to miss because nothing dramatic happens on the day.

A depositor at an acquired bank faces three questions: what happens to the CD rate, what happens to the account terms, and what happens to insurance coverage if you now hold deposits at two banks that became one.

## The insurance question first

This is the one with a specific rule attached.

When two insured banks merge, deposits assumed from the acquired bank remain separately insured for at least six months after the merger. That grace period exists so depositors who happened to hold accounts at both institutions are not suddenly over the limit through no action of their own.

CDs get longer treatment. A certificate from the acquired bank remains separately insured until the earliest maturity date after the six-month period ends. A CD maturing well beyond that stays separately insured until that first maturity date, at which point it combines with your other deposits at the surviving bank.

The practical implication: if you held $200,000 at Bank A and $200,000 at Bank B, and they merge, you are fully insured today and potentially $150,000 uninsured six months from now. The grace period is time to act, not a permanent fix. A CD renewing automatically after that window renews as a deposit at the combined bank, subject to the single $250,000 limit.

## What happens to the CD rate

An existing CD is a contract. The acquiring bank takes on the obligation, and the stated rate generally holds until maturity. That is the normal outcome, and it applies whether or not the acquirer's own CD rates are lower.

Two things frequently change at maturity. The renewal rate becomes the acquirer's rate, which may be considerably lower if a high-rate online bank was bought by a large branch bank. And the renewal terms may change, including the length of the grace period for withdrawing without penalty.

Automatic renewal is the trap. A CD that renewed itself at a good rate for three years can renew into a low rate after an acquisition, and the notice that says so arrives with the merger paperwork rather than as a separate warning. Marking the maturity date on a calendar is worth more after a merger than before one.

Some acquirers offer an early exit without penalty when terms change materially. That is a courtesy in most cases rather than a requirement, and it appears in the merger notice if it is offered.

## What happens to everything else

Account terms, fee schedules, and rate structures generally convert to the acquirer's version on a stated date, usually a few months out. Savings account rates are variable and can be changed with notice, so a high-yield account at an acquired bank often drifts toward the acquirer's lower rate over the following year.

Other changes worth watching in the conversion notice:

- **Account numbers and routing numbers**, which affect direct deposits and automatic payments.
- **Minimum balance requirements** to avoid fees, which are sometimes higher at the acquirer.
- **ATM networks and fee reimbursement**, often different.
- **Online banking migration**, including whether transaction history transfers.
- **Beneficiary designations**, which normally carry over but are worth confirming, since they affect insurance coverage.

## Failure is a different process

For contrast: when a bank fails, the [FDIC](https://www.fdic.gov/resources/deposit-insurance) typically arranges for another bank to assume the deposits, often over a weekend. Insured depositors usually have access by the next business day, and CDs are either assumed by the acquiring bank at the same terms or paid out.

The one difference that catches people is that an assuming bank in a failure may reset CD rates and allow penalty-free withdrawal, since it did not negotiate for those liabilities. That option, when offered, is time-limited.

## What to do when the letter arrives

1. Note the merger date and count six months forward. That is when combined balances become the number the insurance limit applies to.
2. List your CDs at both institutions with their maturity dates, since each stays separately insured until its first maturity after the window.
3. Check whether total deposits at the combined bank will exceed $250,000 in any ownership category, and plan the fix before the grace period ends rather than after.
4. Find the maturity and renewal terms for each CD, and decide in advance whether to let it renew.

None of this is urgent on the day the letter arrives. All of it has a deadline attached, which is the reason it is worth reading rather than filing.

## The savings rate timeline after an acquisition

CDs are protected by contract. Savings accounts are not, and the drift is predictable enough to plan around.

A typical sequence runs like this. The merger is announced, and nothing changes for several months while regulatory approvals proceed. The systems conversion happens, at which point account terms move to the acquirer's version. The rate on a variable savings account is then set by the acquirer's pricing, and if the acquired bank was competing for deposits with a high rate, that rate has no reason to survive.

The drop is rarely a single dramatic cut. It is more often a series of small reductions over the following year, each one individually unremarkable, ending at the acquirer's standard rate.

The practical response is to treat the merger notice as a prompt to check the rate quarterly rather than annually, and to have a comparison in mind before the number moves rather than after.

## Branches, access, and the operational side

Overlapping branch networks get consolidated, which means closures. If the account was opened at a branch you use, the conversion notice will say whether that location is staying.

Other operational items that surface during a conversion:

- **Debit and ATM cards** are typically reissued, with a cutover date after which the old card stops working.
- **Automatic payments and direct deposits** may need updating if routing numbers change, and the bank's transition guidance will say whether it is rerouting them for a period.
- **Online banking history** does not always transfer completely. Downloading statements before the conversion date is the safe move, particularly for tax records.
- **Zelle, bill pay, and external transfer links** frequently need to be re-established.

None of these are financial risks. They are the reason a conversion week is a bad time to have a large payment scheduled.

## The decision worth making early

The insurance grace period gives you six months, and CDs longer. That is enough time to decide deliberately rather than react.

The decision is simply whether the combined bank is still where this money should be. If the acquired institution was chosen for its rate and the acquirer does not compete on rate, the answer is often no, and the cleanest exit point is a CD maturity date rather than an early withdrawal. Mapping those dates against the end of the grace period turns the whole thing into a calendar exercise.
