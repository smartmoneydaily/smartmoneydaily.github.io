Rate shopping is the visible part of picking a deposit account. The fee schedule is the part that decides what you keep. At the balances most households hold, one recurring charge can outweigh the entire difference between a below-average account and an above-average one — and the charge is disclosed in advance, in a document you can read before you open anything.

This post covers the fee types that show up on savings accounts, money market accounts, and CDs, where each one is written down, and how to turn an advertised APY into the net APY you actually earn after charges. There is a full worked example with every line of arithmetic shown, so you can run the same calculation against your own statement.

Nothing here is presented as universal. Accounts differ, and the only authority on yours is your own paperwork. The point is to know the names, so you can search for them instead of meeting them on a statement.

## The documents that list fees

Two documents govern the money: the deposit agreement (sometimes called the account agreement) and the fee schedule (often titled schedule of fees and charges). Both belong in your hands before the account is funded. If you cannot find either one on the bank's site or in the opening packet, ask for it by name and wait for it.

One habit is worth building: compare the fee schedule you received at opening against the version currently posted. Terms can change, and your deposit agreement states how much advance notice you get and through which channel. That clause is the reason to keep the opening documents rather than the reason to ignore them.

Your monthly statement is the third source, and the only one that tells you what happened. Scan it for any line that is neither interest paid nor a transaction you initiated. A fee that has run for a year is easier to spot as a repeating amount than as a single entry.

## Fee names to search for

Search both documents for these terms. Each one has a dollar amount and, often, a condition that switches it off:

- **Monthly maintenance or service fee** — note the exact waiver test. A minimum *daily* balance and a minimum *average* balance are different rules, and one dip below the floor can matter under the first but not the second.
- **Excess transaction fee** — charged per item over the bank's own limit on certain withdrawals.
- **Outgoing wire fee**, domestic and international, plus any incoming wire fee.
- **Paper statement fee** and check-order charges.
- **Dormancy or inactivity fee** — triggered after a stated period with no customer-initiated activity.
- **Early account closure fee** — applies when an account is closed within a stated number of days after opening.
- **Stop payment, returned item, and overdraft charges** — relevant when the account has check or card access.
- **Early withdrawal penalty** on CDs, quoted in the disclosure as a number of months of interest.

## Net APY arithmetic

Take $12,000 held for a full year in a money market account paying 0.63% APY, the FDIC national average as of August 17, 2026. Assume the rate does not change and the balance does not move, which is simple annual proration.

Gross interest: $12,000 × 0.0063 = $75.60.

Now apply two ordinary charges: a $5 monthly maintenance fee and two excess-transaction fees of $10 each.

| Line | Amount |
|---|---|
| Gross interest, 0.63% on $12,000 | $75.60 |
| Monthly maintenance, $5 × 12 | −$60.00 |
| Two excess-transaction fees at $10 | −$20.00 |
| Net for the year | −$4.40 |

The arithmetic step by step: $75.60 − $60.00 = $15.60, then $15.60 − $20.00 = −$4.40. Net APY is −$4.40 ÷ $12,000 = −0.0367%, which rounds to about −0.04%. A year of interest at an above-average rate finished below zero.

### The same year with the fee waived

Suppose you meet the minimum-balance condition and the maintenance fee is waived. Then $75.60 − $20.00 = $55.60, and the net APY is $55.60 ÷ $12,000 = 0.4633%, or about 0.46%.

That waiver is worth $60.00 over the year. For comparison, moving $12,000 from the savings national average to the money market national average is worth $12,000 × (0.63% − 0.38%) = $12,000 × 0.0025 = $30.00. The fee condition on one account was worth twice the entire category gap on this balance.

## Minimum balance tests

The waiver that rescued $60.00 above depends on a test, and the two common versions behave differently. A minimum daily balance rule requires the balance to sit at or above the floor on every day of the cycle. A minimum average balance rule adds the daily balances and divides by the number of days.

Say the floor is $2,500. Your balance holds at $3,000 for 28 days of a 30-day cycle, then drops to $2,400 for 2 days. The daily test fails, because two days fell below the floor. The average test passes: ($2,400 × 2) + ($3,000 × 28) = $4,800 + $84,000 = $88,800, and $88,800 ÷ 30 = $2,960, which clears $2,500.

Identical balance history, identical floor, opposite results. When a fee schedule names a minimum, read the word in front of it — daily, average, or combined across linked accounts — because that word decides whether a two-day dip costs you the waiver.

## Transfer limits after Regulation D

Regulation D once capped certain transfers out of savings and money market accounts at six per calendar month. That limit was suspended in 2020, and many banks still set limits of their own. Your deposit agreement states whether a limit applies to your account, which transaction types it counts, and what each excess item costs.

Two numbers settle it: how many outgoing transfers you realistically make in a month, and the per-item charge for each one above the limit. Multiply the charge by the excess you expect and compare the result against the interest the balance earns in a year. If the charges win, the account is the wrong container for money you pay bills from, whatever its APY.

## CD penalties as a fee category

A CD generally charges an early-withdrawal penalty, stated in the disclosure and often quoted as a number of months of interest. It behaves like a fee, so price it before you commit.

Take $10,000 in a 12-month CD at 1.71% APY, the FDIC national average as of August 17, 2026, with a penalty of three months of interest. A full year at that rate is $10,000 × 0.0171 = $171.00. Three months of interest is $171.00 × 3 ÷ 12 = $42.75. Cash out at the six-month mark and, using the same simple proration, you have earned $171.00 × 6 ÷ 12 = $85.50, leaving $85.50 − $42.75 = $42.75 after the penalty.

Two details from the disclosure change that picture. First, whether the penalty is computed on the rate you were paid or on some other basis. Second, whether the penalty can reach principal when the interest earned is smaller than the penalty. On the same CD, one month of interest is $171.00 ÷ 12 = $14.25, well short of the $42.75 penalty, so a month-one withdrawal is exactly the case that clause governs.

## Dormancy, paper, and closure terms

Three charges are easy to skip on a first read. A dormancy or inactivity fee begins after a stated stretch with no customer-initiated activity, which is a live risk for an account you deliberately leave alone. A paper statement fee applies unless electronic delivery is selected. An early closure fee applies if you shut the account within a stated window after opening, which matters if you are moving money to chase a rate.

Each has a trigger written in the document. The dormancy clause defines what the bank counts as activity, the statement clause names the delivery setting that avoids the charge, and the closure clause gives the date after which moving your money costs nothing.

## A pre-opening checklist

Before funding any deposit account, get written answers to five questions: What is the monthly fee, and what exactly waives it? What withdrawal limit applies, and what does an excess item cost? What does an outgoing wire cost? What triggers a dormancy or early closure charge? For a CD, how many months of interest is the penalty, and can it touch principal?

Then run the arithmetic above on your own balance. If the fees exceed the interest, as they did at $12,000 in the first example, the account's advertised rate never mattered.
