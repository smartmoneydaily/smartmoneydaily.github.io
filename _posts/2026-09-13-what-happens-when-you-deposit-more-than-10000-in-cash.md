The teller counts $14,200 in hundreds twice, slides it into a strap, and then asks what you do for a living. A minute later she is filling in a form you did not know existed and cannot see. The deposit posts to the savings account the same afternoon, and nothing about it is wrong.

Almost everything people believe about this moment is off by one detail, and the one detail decides whether the paperwork stays paperwork.

## The threshold is a reporting trigger, not a limit

There is no cap on how much cash you may deposit. [31 CFR 1010.311](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.311) requires a financial institution to file a report on each deposit, withdrawal, exchange of currency, or other payment or transfer that involves a transaction in currency of more than $10,000. The obligation belongs to the bank. It has fifteen days from the transaction to file and keeps its copy for five years, both set by [31 CFR 1010.306](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.306). You are not asked to consent, nothing is withheld, and no tax event is created by the report.

Notice the word more. A deposit of exactly $10,000 does not cross the line. A deposit of $10,000.01 does.

Notice the other word too. Currency means physical cash. Wire a $60,000 car payment into the same savings account and no currency transaction report is generated, because no currency moved. This is where the folk version of the rule goes wrong: people believe the bank reports large deposits, when the rule is about cash specifically. Checks, wires, ACH transfers, and the proceeds of a matured CD arrive with their own audit trail already attached, which is exactly why they are not the subject of this report.

What the bank collects for the report is identifying information about the person conducting the transaction and the person the transaction is for. Occupation is one of the fields. The question at the window is not curiosity.

The identification is not optional on either side of the counter. [31 CFR 1010.312](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.312) requires the institution, before concluding a reportable transaction, to verify and record the name and address of the individual presenting it, along with the identity, account number, and taxpayer identification number of anyone on whose behalf it is being made. The rule specifically forbids writing known customer or signature card on file in place of that information. A depositor who will not produce a document has not found a loophole. He has found a transaction the bank cannot finish.

## What counts as one transaction

Here is where people create problems without intending to.

[31 CFR 1010.313](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.313) treats a financial institution as including all of its domestic branch offices. Multiple currency transactions are then treated as a single transaction where the institution has knowledge that they are by or on behalf of any person and result in cash in or cash out totaling more than $10,000 during any one business day. Deposits made at night or over a weekend or holiday count as received on the next business day.

So $7,000 at the branch near work in the morning and $5,000 at the branch near home at four o'clock is one reportable transaction, not two unreported ones. The teller at the second branch may say nothing at all; the aggregation happens in the bank's systems.

| What you deposit into savings | Currency transaction report | Other record created |
|---|---|---|
| $14,200 in cash at one window | Yes | Bank keeps its usual deposit record |
| $14,200 by check, wire, or transfer | No, no currency moved | The instrument itself |
| $7,000 cash and $5,000 cash, same day, two branches | Yes, aggregated as one transaction | |
| $9,900 cash Monday and $9,900 cash Thursday | Not aggregated by the daily rule | Possibly a suspicious activity report |
| $4,000 cash used to buy a cashier's check | No | A record kept five years under 31 CFR 1010.415 |

That fourth row is the one worth staring at, because it is the row where the reporting question stops being about reporting.

## Splitting the deposit is the offense

[31 U.S.C. 5324(a)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5324) makes it unlawful, for the purpose of evading the reporting requirements, to cause or attempt to cause a domestic financial institution to fail to file a required report, or to structure or assist in structuring, or attempt to structure, any transaction with one or more domestic financial institutions.

Read what is absent from that sentence. There is no requirement that the money be dirty. Where the cash came from is not an element of the offense. A retired contractor breaking a legitimately earned $18,000 into two $9,000 deposits on separate days, specifically so the form is not filed, has committed the violation as completely as anyone laundering anything.

Subsection (d) supplies the consequences: a fine under title 18, imprisonment of not more than five years, or both. The aggravated version doubles the fine and extends imprisonment to ten years where the structuring is committed while violating another federal law, or as part of a pattern of illegal activity involving more than $100,000 in a twelve-month period.

The intent requirement is real, which is the part people skip past when they repeat this rule as folklore. Depositing $9,000 twice because that is what two paychecks happened to be is not a crime. Depositing $9,000 twice because $18,000 at once would generate a form is. The difference lives in your head, and it gets reconstructed afterward from your account history, your questions at the window, and the shape of the amounts.

The shape is the tell. Real-world sums are lumpy. A sequence of deposits that hovers just under a round federal threshold looks designed, because it usually is.

Using a second person does not solve the arithmetic either. The aggregation language in 1010.313 covers transactions by or on behalf of any person, so handing a spouse $9,000 to deposit while you deposit the other $9,000 the same day is still one transaction totaling more than $10,000. The statute reaches assisting in structuring as well, which puts the second person inside the offense rather than outside it. Two accounts at two banks avoid the daily aggregation, and they do not avoid anything else.

## The customers who are exempt, and why you are not

Restaurants, grocers, and cash-heavy retailers cross $10,000 constantly, and their banks are not filing a report every time. [31 CFR 1020.315](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1020.315) is the reason. A bank may designate certain customers as exempt persons and stop reporting their currency transactions: other banks, departments and agencies of federal, state, and local government, entities exercising governmental authority, companies listed on the New York Stock Exchange, the American Stock Exchange, or Nasdaq, majority-owned subsidiaries of those listed companies, non-listed businesses that satisfy the rule's conditions, and payroll customers for withdrawals.

Every category there is an institution or a business. The rule has no category for an individual depositing his own money. A sole proprietorship can qualify, but only by meeting the non-listed business test, which is a statement about the enterprise rather than about the person behind it.

That matters mainly for what it takes off the table. The exemption is a designation the bank makes, not a request you can make at the window, and the fact that a neighbor's large cash deposits pass without a form tells you nothing about yours.

## The report you are never shown

The currency transaction report is routine. The other one is not, and you will not be told about it.

The rule binding national banks, [12 CFR 21.11](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/12/21.11), sets four triggers for a suspicious activity report. Insider abuse is reportable in any amount. A criminal violation aggregating $5,000 or more is reportable where the bank has a substantial basis for identifying a possible suspect, and one aggregating $25,000 or more is reportable whether or not any suspect can be named. The fourth trigger is the one that reaches an ordinary depositor: a transaction of $5,000 or more that the bank knows, suspects, or has reason to suspect involves funds derived from illegal activity, or is designed to evade the Bank Secrecy Act, or has no business or apparent lawful purpose. A deposit pattern built around the $10,000 line sits squarely in the middle clause of that sentence.

Filing is due no later than 30 calendar days after initial detection. Where no suspect has been identified by then, the bank may take one additional 30 days to find one, and reporting may not be delayed beyond 60 days in any case.

Secrecy is written into the rule rather than left to bank discretion. No bank, and no director, officer, employee, or agent of one, may disclose a report or any information that would reveal its existence. A bank served with a subpoena for one must decline to produce it, citing the rule and 31 U.S.C. 5318(g)(2)(A)(i), and must notify the OCC and FinCEN of the request. Comparable requirements bind state member banks, savings associations, and credit unions through their own regulators.

Two practical consequences follow. Asking a teller how to keep a deposit off the form is itself the kind of statement that gets written down, and attempting to structure is covered by the statute in any case. And the absence of any reaction at the branch tells you nothing at all about whether a report exists.

## When the money was legitimately yours

The old horror stories about seized bank accounts belong to a period when the IRS could take a balance on a structuring pattern alone, with no showing about where the money came from. The statute now reads differently.

[31 U.S.C. 5317(c)(2)(B)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/31/5317) permits the Internal Revenue Service to seize property on a claimed section 5324 violation only where the property was derived from an illegal source, or the funds were structured for the purpose of concealing a violation of a criminal law or regulation other than section 5324 itself. Clean money structured for no reason except avoiding the form falls outside both branches of that sentence.

Read the limit for what it is. It restrains the IRS in structuring cases. It does not soften the criminal offense, which stands on its own and carries prison time whether or not a dollar is ever seized.

The same subparagraph puts the government on a clock. Notice of the seizure and of the owner's rights must reach anyone with an ownership interest within 30 days, extendable by a further 30 where notice would endanger national security or someone's safety. If the owner requests a hearing within 30 days of that notice, the property is returned unless a court holds an adversarial hearing and finds probable cause, within 30 days of the request, that the property fits one of the two descriptions above. Both clocks require the owner to file something. Neither one runs in favor of a person who waits.

## Paperwork in the same neighborhood

Three other records get generated by ordinary cash behavior, and none of them has the $10,000 threshold people expect.

**Cashier's checks and money orders.** Under [31 CFR 1010.415](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.415), no institution may issue or sell a bank check or draft, cashier's check, money order, or traveler's check for $3,000 or more in currency without keeping records of the purchase. For an accountholder that means name, date, instrument type, serial number, and amount, verified against the signature card or an acceptable identification document. For a non-accountholder it extends to address, Social Security number, and date of birth. The records are kept five years under [31 CFR 1010.430(d)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/1010.430). A $4,000 cash purchase of a cashier's check generates no currency transaction report and a durable record anyway.

**The business that took your cash.** If you paid a dealer, a contractor, or a law firm in cash, the [IRS](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/irs-form-8300-reference-guide) requires that trade or business to report cash payments of more than $10,000 on Form 8300, generally within 15 days of receiving it. Transactions between the same payer and recipient within a 24-hour period are related, and payments further apart than that are related too where the business knows, or has reason to know, they are part of one connected series. In certain designated transactions, cashier's checks, bank drafts, traveler's checks, and money orders with a face value of $10,000 or less count as cash for this purpose, which catches people who thought a cashier's check was the quiet option.

That last rule comes with something the bank report does not have. The business must give each customer and each party named on the form a written statement saying it was filed, on or before January 31 of the year following the payment. If you paid a dealer in cash last spring, that notice is coming.

## Moving cash into a high-yield account

Online banks pay the better rates and have no cash window. Physical currency cannot be deposited into most of them at all, which means the cash lands at a branch somewhere first and then moves by transfer.

The reporting happens at the cash step. Once the money is in a deposit account, an ACH transfer to a higher-yielding savings account at another institution is not a currency transaction and generates no report, no matter the size. People sometimes reverse this in their heads and worry about the transfer while treating the cash deposit as the informal part. It is the other way around.

Buying a certificate of deposit directly with cash sits on the reportable side. The rule covers each deposit, payment, or transfer involving currency above the threshold, and handing a teller $15,000 in bills to open a CD is a payment in currency however long the term is. The report attaches to the form the money takes at the counter, not to the product it ends up inside.

One timing note for anyone moving a large sum toward a rate. The report is filed after the fact, within fifteen days, and it has no effect on the deposit itself. Nothing is held, delayed, or placed under review because a currency transaction report exists. Whatever hold applies to your money is the ordinary funds availability schedule, and cash deposited to a teller is among the fastest categories there is.

## What to do with a large sum of cash

1. **Deposit it in one transaction.** One deposit, one report, one clean explanation. Splitting it is the only version of this that carries criminal exposure.
2. **Bring identification and answer the questions at the window.** The bank cannot conclude a reportable transaction without verifying and recording who you are, and occupation and source of funds are fields on a form it is required to complete. A refusal is itself noteworthy.
3. **Keep the document that explains the money.** A bill of sale, a settlement statement, a closing document, a signed gift letter. Nobody may ever ask. If they ask, it will be long after you have stopped remembering the details.
4. **If the cash arrives over weeks, deposit each amount as it actually is.** Deposit the $6,300 when you have $6,300. What creates the problem is shaping the amounts around the threshold rather than around your life.
5. **Do not ask anyone at the bank how to avoid the form.** There is no lawful technique on the other side of that question.
6. **Expect the January statement if a business received the cash.** The Form 8300 notice arriving in the mail is not an audit notice.

The form itself is close to meaningless in isolation. Banks file these constantly, on car sales, on inheritances, on restaurant owners making a Monday deposit, and nothing happens afterward in the overwhelming majority of cases. The deposit that draws attention is the one that was engineered to stay quiet, which is a decision made by the depositor and visible in the numbers forever.
