A money market fund is not a bank account, and the difference shows up under stress rather than in normal weeks. The rules governing that stress were rewritten by the SEC in July 2023, with the main provisions phasing in through October 2024.

The changes matter to anyone holding cash in a fund rather than a deposit, which includes most brokerage sweep balances at firms that default to funds.

## What broke, twice

The reference point is 2008, when the Reserve Primary Fund held Lehman Brothers paper and its share price fell below $1. Breaking the buck triggered a run across prime funds and a federal guarantee program to stop it.

The 2010 and 2014 reform rounds responded with liquidity requirements, floating share prices for institutional prime funds, and a framework allowing funds to impose fees or gates when liquidity fell below a threshold.

That framework caused its own problem in March 2020. Because the fee and gate powers were tied to a visible liquidity level, investors could see a fund approaching the threshold and had an incentive to redeem before it was reached. The tool meant to slow a run gave people a reason to start one.

## What the 2023 rules did

Three changes.

**Gates are gone.** Funds can no longer suspend redemptions temporarily. Removing the possibility removes the incentive to redeem ahead of it.

**Liquidity buffers went up.** Daily liquid assets rose to 25% of total assets and weekly liquid assets to 50%, from 10% and 30%. Funds hold materially more in instruments that convert to cash quickly.

**A mandatory liquidity fee replaced the threshold-linked tools.** Institutional prime and institutional tax-exempt funds must impose a fee when daily net redemptions exceed 5% of net assets, unless the cost of that liquidity is determined to be minimal. Other non-government funds may impose a discretionary fee of up to 2% when the board determines it is in shareholders' interest.

The design intent is that redeeming shareholders bear the cost of the liquidity they consume, rather than that cost falling on those who stay.

## Which funds this touches

The distinction that matters is fund type, and it is stated in the prospectus.

| Fund type | Share price | Mandatory fee |
|---|---|---|
| Government | Stable $1 | No |
| Retail prime | Stable $1 | No, discretionary possible |
| Institutional prime | Floating | Yes, above the redemption threshold |
| Retail tax-exempt | Stable $1 | No, discretionary possible |
| Institutional tax-exempt | Floating | Yes, above the redemption threshold |

Most individual investors hold government funds, which are exempt from the mandatory fee and maintain a stable share price. A brokerage sweep into a government money market fund is the common case, and the 2023 changes affect it mainly through the higher liquidity requirements, which is a strengthening rather than a restriction.

## What a fund is and is not

None of this makes a money market fund equivalent to a deposit.

There is no [FDIC](https://www.fdic.gov/resources/deposit-insurance) insurance. SIPC protects the custody of the shares if the brokerage fails, not their value. A fund's share price can move, and a stable $1 price is a policy the fund pursues under specific rules, not a guarantee.

Against that, funds hold short-term government and high-grade instruments, they yield close to short-term market rates rather than the administered rate a bank chooses to pay, and government fund interest may carry the same state tax exemption as the Treasuries inside them, depending on the fund's composition and your state's rules.

## The practical read

For cash in a brokerage account, the question is not whether a fund is safe in the abstract. It is which fund, and what the alternative pays.

Three things to check on a fund holding your cash:

1. **The fund type**, which determines the share price behavior and the fee framework.
2. **The seven-day yield**, which is the standard comparison figure against a savings rate or a bill yield.
3. **The expense ratio**, which comes straight out of what you receive.

For cash that needs to be certain to the dollar on a specific day, a deposit account or a Treasury maturing on that date is the cleaner instrument. For cash that is waiting, a government money market fund is a reasonable place to wait, and it is now sitting behind larger liquidity buffers than it was before 2024.

Run your own numbers instead: your balance, the APY on offer, and the period you plan to hold.

## Reading what a fund actually holds

A fund's category tells you most of what you need, and the holdings report tells you the rest.

A government fund holds Treasury securities, government agency debt, and repurchase agreements collateralized by them. A Treasury-only fund narrows that further, which usually raises the share of interest that qualifies for a state tax exemption. A prime fund adds commercial paper, certificates of deposit, and other corporate short-term debt, which is where the extra yield and the extra credit exposure both come from.

Two figures in the monthly holdings disclosure are worth a look: weighted average maturity, which indicates how quickly the portfolio reprices when rates move, and the share of daily and weekly liquid assets, which is now held to a higher standard than before 2024.

For a Treasury-focused fund, the year-end tax documentation states the percentage of income attributable to government obligations. That percentage is what your state exemption applies to, and it varies from year to year.

## What the prime fund spread pays you for

Prime funds yield more than government funds, usually by a modest margin. That spread is compensation for credit risk and for the possibility of a liquidity fee under the current rules.

In calm periods the spread is close to free money, which is precisely why it exists and why it periodically stops being free. The 2008 and 2020 episodes both involved prime funds specifically, because commercial paper is the part of the portfolio that becomes hard to sell when short-term credit markets seize.

For an individual holding a few months of expenses in a brokerage account, the spread rarely justifies the difference. For an institution managing operating cash at scale, the calculation is different, which is why the mandatory fee provision applies to institutional prime funds rather than to the ones most individuals hold.

## Comparing a fund against a bank account honestly

Three adjustments make the comparison real.

**Net yield, not gross.** The seven-day yield is quoted after expenses, which makes it directly comparable to an APY.

**After state tax.** A government fund's Treasury income may be exempt from state tax; a bank APY is not. In a high-tax state this can reverse the ranking between two figures that look identical.

**Certainty of principal.** A deposit is a fixed claim on the bank, insured to the limit. A fund share is a claim on a portfolio. In normal conditions the distinction is invisible, and it is not the kind of thing to discover during the week it starts mattering.

For a genuine emergency fund, the insured deposit is the more appropriate instrument even at a slightly lower yield. For cash waiting on an investment decision inside a brokerage account, a government money market fund is a reasonable and now better-buffered place to hold it.
