The application stops on the fourth screen. A second-year graduate student on an F-1 visa has typed her name, date of birth, and apartment address into an online bank's savings application, and the next field reads Social Security number, nine digits, required. Her uncle, who moved to the U.S. in the middle of 2025 and received an Individual Taxpayer Identification Number from the IRS so he could file his first return, tries the same bank that evening on his phone. Same field, same dead end.

Both come away believing federal law keeps people without an SSN out of American banks. It does not.

You can open a U.S. savings account or CD without a Social Security number. The federal identification rule for banks lets a non-U.S. person supply an ITIN, a passport number and country of issuance, an alien identification card number, or the number of another government-issued photo document. What stopped both applicants was a product choice: an application built to accept a single kind of number. The workable route is usually a bank that will open the account with a person in front of it, an unexpired passport, a street address or one of the listed substitutes, and an ITIN if one has been issued. How the interest is taxed is a separate question, and it turns on residency status rather than on which number went on the form.

## What the identification rule requires

Every bank that has to run an anti-money laundering program also needs a written Customer Identification Program, set out at [31 CFR 1020.220](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-31/subtitle-B/chapter-X/part-1020/subpart-B/section-1020.220). Before opening an account, the bank "must obtain, at a minimum," a name, a date of birth for an individual, an address, and an "Identification number."

The fourth item is where the SSN assumption comes from, and the regulation splits it in two. The identification number "shall be: (i) For a U.S. person, a taxpayer identification number; or (ii) For a non-U.S. person, one or more of the following: A taxpayer identification number; passport number and country of issuance; alien identification card number; or number and country of issuance of any other government-issued document evidencing nationality or residence and bearing a photograph or similar safeguard."

Even the TIN option is wider than an SSN. The IRS page on [backup withholding](https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/backup-withholding) states that "A TIN can be either your social security number (SSN), employer identification number (EIN), or individual taxpayer identification number (ITIN)."

The term U.S. person is narrower than most applicants assume. The definitions at [31 CFR 1010.100](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-31/subtitle-B/chapter-X/part-1010/subpart-A/section-1010.100) define it, for an individual, as "A United States citizen," and add: "Non-U.S. person means a person that is not a U.S. person." A lawful permanent resident is therefore a non-U.S. person here, which is why an alien identification card number appears on the list.

The rule also allows for someone still waiting on a number. A bank's program "may include procedures for opening an account for a customer that has applied for, but has not received, a taxpayer identification number," provided it confirms the application was filed and obtains the number "within a reasonable period of time after the account is opened." A pending ITIN application gets a yes at a bank that built that procedure and a no at one that did not.

## Why applications still demand an SSN

The rule sets a floor. A bank's program must include those items "at a minimum," and nothing in the text obliges a bank to accept every document on the non-U.S. person list. A bank that takes only Social Security numbers has chosen a stricter policy, not broken the rule.

The verification paragraph shows why an online application can be the strictest channel. The procedures "must be based on the bank's assessment of the relevant risks," including those presented by "the various methods of opening accounts provided by the bank" and "the various types of identifying information available." A bank using non-documentary verification must address situations where "the account is opened without obtaining documents" and where "the customer opens the account without appearing in person at the bank." The methods the rule lists include comparing what the customer provides "with information obtained from a consumer reporting agency, public database, or other source."

The regulation does not tell a bank which method to use. But an application with no employee on the other end has nobody to look at a passport, so it leans on the matching kind of check, and someone who has lived in the country for a year or two gives that check little to match.

Every program must also describe "When the bank should not open an account," and an SSN-only form has made that call for a whole category of customers in advance. The sample notice printed in the rule points the other way: "We may also ask to see your driver's license or other identifying documents."

## Documents to bring, branch versus online

Where a bank relies on documents, the rule says they "may include," for an individual, "unexpired government-issued identification evidencing nationality or residence and bearing a photograph or similar safeguard, such as a driver's license or passport." A passport does two jobs: its number and country of issuance fill the identification-number item, and the booklet itself serves for verification. Unexpired is in the text, so a passport that lapsed in June is a problem at the desk in September.

An ITIN letter plays a narrower part. The [IRS ITIN page](https://www.irs.gov/individuals/individual-taxpayer-identification-number) calls the number "a 9-digit number the IRS issues if you need a U.S. taxpayer identification number for federal tax purposes, but you aren't eligible for a Social Security number (SSN)," and says it does not "Serve as identification outside the federal tax system." It supplies the number, and the TIN for tax reporting, but not the photo document.

For the address, the bank must collect "a residential or business street address." Someone without one, which is common for a student between a dormitory contract and a first lease, may instead give "an Army Post Office (APO) or Fleet Post Office (FPO) box number, or the residential or business street address of next of kin or of another contact individual." A personal post office box is not on that list. Nothing in the text requires a U.S. street address, or a lease or utility bill as proof; where a bank asks for those, that is bank policy.

| Item | What 31 CFR 1020.220 allows | What to ask the bank first |
|---|---|---|
| Identification number | For a non-U.S. person: a TIN, passport number and country, alien identification card number, or other government photo ID number | Does the program accept an ITIN or passport number, and in which channel? |
| Verification | Documents, non-documentary methods, or both | Can a passport be shown in person instead? |
| Address | Street address; if none, APO/FPO or a next-of-kin or contact person's street address | Is a foreign address accepted, and what proof is required? |
| TIN not yet issued | Only if the bank's program includes the applicant procedure | Is proof of a filed ITIN application accepted? |

Once a bank agrees, the checklist in [High-Yield Savings Accounts: What to Check Before Opening](/2026/06/15/what-to-consider-when-opening-a-high-yield-savings-account/) applies unchanged.

## Deposit insurance and citizenship

The [FDIC](https://www.fdic.gov/resources/deposit-insurance) settles this in its brochure [Your Insured Deposits](https://www.fdic.gov/resources/deposit-insurance/brochures/insured-deposits): "Any person or entity can have FDIC insurance coverage in an insured bank. A person does not have to be a U.S. citizen or resident to have his or her deposits insured by the FDIC."

The limits are the ones every depositor faces: "$250,000 per depositor, per insured bank, for each account ownership category." For accounts owned by one person with no beneficiaries, "The FDIC adds together the balances in all Single Accounts owned by the same person at the same bank and insures the total up to $250,000."

A nonresident who keeps $180,000 in savings and a $90,000 CD at the same insured bank, both in her name alone with no beneficiaries, has $270,000 in one category. The FDIC insures $250,000, and $20,000 sits above the limit. Her passport country and visa type change none of that arithmetic.

Coverage attaches to the institution, "in an insured bank," so the check that matters is the one in [How to Verify a Bank Is Actually FDIC-Insured Before You Transfer](/2026/09/09/how-to-verify-a-bank-is-actually-fdic-insured-before-you-transfer/).

## The residency tests behind the tax form

[IRS Publication 519](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p519), in its current edition for calendar year 2025, starts from two tests: "You are a resident alien of the United States for tax purposes if you meet either the green card test or the substantial presence test for calendar year 2025."

The green card test reads: "You are a resident for tax purposes if you are a lawful permanent resident of the United States at any time during calendar year 2025." The substantial presence test counts days. A person must be "physically present in the United States on at least: 31 days during 2025; and 183 days during the 3-year period that includes 2025, 2024, and 2023, counting: All the days you were present in 2025, 1/3 of the days you were present in 2024, and 1/6 of the days you were present in 2023."

The uncle arrived on July 1, 2025, on a visa that does not make him an exempt individual, and stayed. That is 184 days in 2025, past both thresholds, so he meets the substantial presence test even though he has never been eligible for an SSN. Pub 519 adds that his "residency starting date is generally the first day you are present in the United States during that calendar year," so 2025 is a dual-status year and he is a resident alien from July 1 on.

The student's days are treated differently. Pub 519 says not to count days for which you are an "exempt individual," a term that "does not refer to someone exempt from U.S. tax." The categories include "A student temporarily present in the United States under an "F," "J," "M," or "Q" visa who substantially complies with the requirements of the visa." The exemption ends for a student who has been exempt "for any part of more than 5 calendar years," unless the student shows no intent to reside permanently and has substantially complied with the visa. Excluding those days requires filing "a fully completed Form 8843 with the IRS."

So the student is a nonresident alien and the ITIN-holding uncle is a resident alien: two non-U.S. persons to the bank, and two different tax forms.

For the uncle, Pub 519 is blunt: "Resident aliens are generally taxed in the same way as U.S. citizens." The [Form W-8BEN instructions](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/iw8ben) rule that form out for any "U.S. person (including a resident alien individual)" and say "Instead, use Form W-9 to document your status as a U.S. person." [Form W-9](https://www.irs.gov/forms-pubs/about-form-w-9) exists "to provide your correct Taxpayer Identification Number (TIN) to the person who is required to file an information return with the IRS." His ITIN goes on it, and his interest falls under the "Interest payments (Form 1099-INT)" the IRS lists on its backup withholding page.

## The nonresident deposit interest exclusion

The student's interest is covered by a specific exclusion. Pub 519 says "Nonresident aliens can exclude the following items from their gross income," and under interest: "Interest income that is not connected with a U.S. trade or business is excluded from income if it is from: Deposits (including certificates of deposit) with persons in the banking business; Deposits or withdrawable accounts with mutual savings banks, cooperative banks, credit unions, domestic building and loan associations, and other savings institutions chartered and supervised as savings and loan or similar associations under federal or state law (if the interest paid or credited can be deducted by the association)."

$20,000 in a 12-month CD at a hypothetical 4% APY, left untouched, earns $800. For the nonresident student, whose CD has no connection to a U.S. trade or business, the $800 is excluded from gross income. For the resident uncle holding an identical CD, it is income, and "Income of resident aliens is subject to the graduated tax rates that apply to U.S. citizens."

The bank learns which treatment applies from the form on file. The IRS [Form W-8BEN page](https://www.irs.gov/forms-pubs/about-form-w-8-ben) says to "Submit Form W-8 BEN when requested by the withholding agent or payer whether or not you are claiming a reduced rate of, or exemption from, withholding."

Skipping the form has a price. Under [26 CFR 1.6049-8](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-26/section-1.6049-8), a payor without "either a valid Form W-8BEN or valid Form W-9" must, where the presumption rules require it, report the payment "as made to a U.S. non-exempt recipient," and "the payor must also backup withhold under section 3406." The IRS backup withholding rate is "the current rate of 24 percent." On the $800, that is $192 withheld from interest the student could have excluded, to be reported "on your return for the year you received the income."

## Form 1042-S and the treaty-country list

Excluded does not mean unreported. The same regulation defines reportable deposit interest as interest "paid to a nonresident alien individual who is a resident of a country that is identified, in an applicable revenue procedure," as a country with a tax convention or agreement "relating to the exchange of tax information," one under which "the United States agrees to provide, as well as receive, information." The list exists for information exchange, so interest the U.S. does not tax can still generate a report.

The 2026 [Instructions for Form 1042-S](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i1042s) put it in operational terms: withholding agents "must report certain interest described in section 871(i)(2)(A) aggregating $10 or more paid with respect to a deposit maintained at an office within the United States if such interest is paid to a nonresident alien individual who is a resident of a country identified in Rev. Proc. 2024-42," or in a superseding revenue procedure effective as of January 1, 2026. They add that "A payer may elect to report interest described above paid to any nonresident alien individual by reporting all such interest."

The W-8BEN also decides which country counts. The regulation lets a payor "rely upon the permanent residence address provided on a valid Form W-8BEN" unless it "knows or has reason to know that such documentation of the country of residence is unreliable or incorrect." The home-country address on that form, not the U.S. apartment, is the one that matters. Because a payer may choose to report all such interest, a Form 1042-S in the mail does not by itself show that the student's country is on the list.

## After a Social Security number arrives

When an SSN does arrive, three records need updating, and none updates itself.

The IRS record comes first. The ITIN page says "You can't have both an ITIN and an SSN," and "When you get an SSN, notify the IRS. We'll combine all your tax records under your SSN." The IRS will then "void your ITIN and associate all prior tax information filed under the ITIN with the SSN."

The bank's TIN record is second. Backup withholding can apply "because you failed to provide a correct taxpayer identification number (TIN) to the payer," and an account still reporting under a voided ITIN invites exactly that mismatch. The IRS remedy includes "providing the correct TIN to the payer," and because account holders "must furnish your TIN in writing to the bank," the practical vehicle is a new Form W-9 carrying the SSN.

Residency status is third, and an SSN does not change it. Neither Pub 519 test mentions a Social Security number. A student who gets an SSN for campus work and is still an exempt individual remains a nonresident alien, and the W-8BEN stays. A green card, or a year in which counted days cross the substantial presence thresholds, is what changes the form.

At that point the W-8BEN instructions set a deadline. After a change in circumstances that makes the form incorrect, "you must notify the withholding agent, payer, or FFI with which you hold an account within 30 days of the change in circumstances and you must file a new Form W-8BEN or other appropriate form," which for a new resident alien is the W-9. Even with no change, the form lasts only until "the last day of the third succeeding calendar year" after signing. The IRS example is a form "signed on September 30, 2015," that "remains valid through December 31, 2018."

## Checklist by status

| | Nonresident alien with a passport | Resident alien with an ITIN | New SSN holder |
|---|---|---|---|
| Number for the bank | Passport number and country of issuance, or ITIN if issued | ITIN | SSN |
| Photo document | Unexpired passport | Unexpired government photo ID, such as a passport | Unexpired government photo ID |
| No street address | APO/FPO, or a next-of-kin or contact person's street address | Same | Same |
| Tax form | W-8BEN | W-9 | W-9 if a resident alien; W-8BEN if still a nonresident |
| Deposit interest | Excluded if not connected with a U.S. trade or business | Taxed like a citizen's | Set by the residency tests, not the SSN |
| Year-end form | Form 1042-S if resident in a listed country, or if the payer reports all | Form 1099-INT | Follows tax status |
| Dates to track | New form within 30 days of a change; renew by end of third calendar year after signing | Notify the IRS and the bank when an SSN is issued | Give the bank the SSN in writing on a W-9 |

Before the next application, ask the bank three things: whether its identification program accepts a passport or ITIN for a non-U.S. person, whether that works online or only at a branch, and which tax form it wants at opening. The regulation already allows the account. Those answers show whether the bank does.
