Almost nobody gets the number wrong. Ask a saver what the [FDIC](https://www.fdic.gov/national-rates-and-rate-caps) covers and you will hear $250,000. The errors live in the words around the number — per depositor, per insured bank, per ownership category — and that is where deposits quietly end up outside the guarantee while the account statements look perfectly normal.

What follows is a catalog of the mistakes that actually cost people coverage, roughly in the order they show up in real accounts. Each one gets the belief, why it sounds reasonable, what it costs, and the specific fix. One section carries arithmetic, because the accrued-interest mistake is the one you can make while doing everything else right.

Almost none of this requires rearranging your finances. Most of it comes down to two questions — how is this account titled, and which bank charter is it actually at — and both can be settled in an afternoon.

## Three Words That Do All the Work

**Per depositor** means per legal owner, identified by name and taxpayer ID. Not per account number, not per debit card, not per login.

**Per insured bank** means per charter, and a charter is identified by an FDIC certificate number. One certificate can sit behind several consumer brands and several websites.

**Per ownership category** is the only one of the three that multiplies your coverage. Single accounts, joint accounts, certain retirement accounts, and revocable trust accounts are insured separately from each other, each with its own $250,000 ceiling at the same bank.

Here is the short version of where beliefs and rules part company.

| What people assume | What actually applies |
|---|---|
| $250,000 per account | $250,000 per depositor, per bank, per ownership category |
| Six accounts, six limits | Six accounts in one category at one bank, one limit |
| Two bank brands, two sets of limits | Two brands can share one FDIC certificate and one set of limits |
| Only my principal counts | Principal plus interest accrued to the day the bank fails |
| Everything sold at a bank is insured | Deposits are insured; investments are not |
| The app I use is a bank | Many apps place funds at a partner bank, and coverage follows that bank |

Credit unions run the same limit under a different agency. The [NCUA](https://ncua.gov/consumers/share-insurance-coverage) insures share accounts to the same $250,000 standard, with its own rules and its own fund.

## Mistake 1: Accounts Instead of Ownership Categories

The belief: spreading money across more accounts at the same bank spreads the insurance. Five accounts, five ceilings.

Why it sounds right: every account has its own number, its own statement, and often its own rate. They feel like separate things.

They are not. Within one ownership category at one bank, every deposit account you hold is added together and measured against a single $250,000 ceiling. Checking, savings, a [money market account](/2026/08/22/money-market-account-vs-money-market-fund-whats-insured/), and three CDs in your name alone are one bucket. Different terms do not split them. Different branches do not split them. Opening a fourth CD does not open a fourth ceiling.

The fix is to group before you add. List the accounts at each bank, sort them by titling rather than by nickname, and total each group. Coverage multiplies when the *category* changes — you and a spouse open a genuine joint account, or money sits in a qualifying retirement deposit — or when the *charter* changes. Nothing else moves the ceiling.

## Mistake 2: Two Brands, One Charter

The belief: money at Brand A and money at Brand B is money at two banks.

Sometimes it is. Often it is not. Banks acquire each other and keep the acquired name on the door. Banks also launch online-only brands that are legally divisions of the parent institution rather than separate banks. In both cases there is one FDIC certificate, and every deposit under it combines.

This mistake is expensive precisely because it feels solved. Someone deliberately splits $400,000 across two brand names to stay under the limit, and both names report to the same certificate, so $150,000 sits uninsured by design.

The check takes about a minute per brand. Open [FDIC BankFind](https://banks.data.fdic.gov/bankfind-suite/bankfind), search the name on your statement, and write down the FDIC certificate number. Two brands returning the same number are one bank for every calculation you make. [BankFind](https://banks.data.fdic.gov/bankfind-suite/bankfind) also lists trade names associated with an institution, which is usually where the surprise turns up.

## Mistake 3: The Interest That Grows Into the Limit

The belief: fund the account under $250,000 and it stays under $250,000.

Coverage is measured as of the day the bank closes, and it includes interest accrued to that day. A CD makes this concrete, because you set it up once and then it grows on a schedule you agreed to in advance.

Take a 24-month CD funded with $245,000, held in one person's name at a bank where they hold nothing else. Use the FDIC national average for a 24-month CD, 1.57% APY as of August 17, 2026, and let the interest compound inside the CD rather than paying out.

- After year one: $245,000 × 1.0157 = **$248,846.50**
- After year two: $248,846.50 × 1.0157 = **$252,753.39**

The deposit was $5,000 under the ceiling on day one and $2,753.39 over it at maturity. Nobody made a decision in between.

Run the same calculation backwards to size the deposit properly. To land at or under $250,000 after two years at that rate, the opening deposit has to be no more than $250,000 ÷ 1.0157² ≈ $242,331 — call it $242,300 for a margin. Alternatively, have the interest paid out to an account at an unrelated bank instead of compounding in place, or plan to move the excess during the grace period after maturity.

Those figures are the FDIC's published national averages for all reporting institutions as of August 17, 2026, not an offer from any bank. Use the APY on your own disclosure when you run this for real.

## Mistake 4: Assumptions About Joint and Beneficiary Coverage

Two subcategories of the same error: assuming a titling arrangement does something it does not.

On joint accounts, the rule is $250,000 per co-owner, so two owners on one account reach $500,000 at that bank — but the category has conditions. Every co-owner must be a living person, each must have equal withdrawal rights, and the account records have to reflect it. An account you added a relative's name to informally, so they could help pay bills, may not sit in the joint category the way you assume. Check the signature card, not the app.

On beneficiaries, naming a payable-on-death beneficiary moves that account out of your single-account bucket and into the trust category. That cuts both ways. It can open a separate ceiling, which is a genuine gain. It also means the balance no longer counts where you thought it counted, and people who do the arithmetic on the old assumption end up counting the same $250,000 twice.

Trust coverage was rewritten effective April 2024, and outdated advice is still circulating. Rather than working from anything you half-remember, run your actual accounts through the FDIC's own estimator, [EDIE](https://edie.fdic.gov/), which applies the current rules and produces a report you can save.

## Mistake 5: Deposits Confused With Investments

The belief: it came from the bank, so the bank's insurance covers it.

Deposit insurance covers deposits. Money you place in investments is not insured against loss by the FDIC, no matter which lobby you were sitting in when you bought it. Mutual funds, money market *funds* — which are not money market deposit accounts — stocks, bonds, annuities, and life insurance products all fall outside it. A market loss is not a bank failure, and no federal deposit fund makes it good.

The confusion is easiest to fall into with two products. Money market deposit accounts and money market funds sound identical and are not: the first is a bank deposit, the second is a fund holding securities. And brokered CDs are deposits at an issuing bank purchased through a brokerage, which means the coverage depends on which bank issued them — and on whether you already hold deposits at that same bank through the front door.

The fix is a habit. Before assuming coverage, ask which institution actually holds the money and whether the product is a deposit obligation of that institution. If the answer involves a market value that can move, it is not a deposit.

## Mistake 6: The App on Your Phone as the Bank

Many financial apps are not banks. They place customer money at one or more partner banks, and insurance depends on those banks, on their solvency, and on whether the records identify you as the owner of the funds.

That arrangement can work exactly as advertised. It can also leave people surprised about who owes them what, particularly when the app's own records are the only place their ownership is written down. The brand on the icon is not the answer to the coverage question.

What to do: open the app's deposit agreement and find the named partner institution or institutions. Run each name through BankFind. Then check whether you already hold deposits directly at any of those banks, because those balances combine with the app's balances in the same ownership category. Members of credit unions can run the equivalent check at [NCUA](https://ncua.gov/consumers/share-insurance-coverage).

## A Coverage Check Worth Fifteen Minutes

Work through this once, then again whenever something changes.

1. List every place you hold deposits, by brand name.
2. Look up each brand's FDIC certificate number and merge any that match.
3. Within each charter, sort accounts by titling: single, joint, retirement, trust or payable-on-death.
4. Total each group using the balance you expect at the end of the term, interest included.
5. Compare each total to its ceiling and write down every uninsured slice.

Repeat it when you add an owner, add or remove a beneficiary, open an account at an unfamiliar brand, receive a lump sum, or learn that one of your banks was acquired. Those five events cause most of the coverage gaps people discover too late — and each one takes minutes to check while it is still a choice rather than a claim.
