The bank will not discuss the account with you until it has a certified death certificate. That is usually the first thing a survivor learns, somewhere between the funeral and the stack of statements on the kitchen table. The second thing is that the account's destination was settled long ago, by a line in the bank's records the owner may have filled in once and never looked at again.

A will does not control a deposit account that carries survivorship rights or a named beneficiary. Those balances pass under the titling on the account records, and that titling overrides whatever the will says about the money.

## Who the money already belongs to

| How the account is titled | Who receives it | What the bank asks for | Probate |
|---|---|---|---|
| Joint with right of survivorship | The surviving owner or owners | Certified death certificate | No |
| Payable on death, or in trust for, with a named beneficiary | The named beneficiaries | Certified death certificate, beneficiary ID | No |
| Sole name, no beneficiary named | The estate, then heirs under the will or state law | Letters testamentary, or a small-estate affidavit where the state allows one | Yes |

Two wrinkles sit under that first row. A joint account is not automatically a survivorship account. The [CFPB](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-happens-if-i-have-a-joint-bank-account-with-someone-who-died-en-1101/) describes the usual case, where the money passes to the surviving owner or equally to the rest of the owners, and it also notes the alternative: some joint accounts are held as tenants in common, and there the deceased owner's share passes to their heirs under the will or state law rather than to the co-owner. The account agreement is where that distinction is recorded.

The second wrinkle is on the POD line. A beneficiary who died before the owner generally does not inherit, and if no named beneficiary survives, the balance falls back into the estate, which is the outcome the designation existed to avoid.

## The six-month clock on insurance

This is the part almost nobody knows is running.

Under [12 CFR 330.3(j)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/12/330.3), the death of a deposit owner does not affect the insurance coverage of the deposit for six months following the death unless the account is restructured in that time. The [FDIC](https://www.fdic.gov/resources/deposit-insurance) insures the accounts as if the owner were still alive. Credit unions have the mirror-image rule: [12 CFR 745.2](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/12/745.2) gives [NCUA](https://ncua.gov/consumers/share-insurance-coverage) share insurance the same six-month treatment, in nearly identical language.

The window exists because a death can silently collapse coverage. The FDIC's own [example](https://www.fdic.gov/financial-institution-employees-guide-deposit-insurance/death-account-owner) for bank staff runs like this. A married couple hold a $500,000 joint money market deposit account, fully insured because each owner's half falls under the $250,000 limit. One spouse also holds a $100,000 CD in her sole name. Both are covered while both are living.

When the husband dies, the joint account becomes the wife's alone. Add the CD and she now has $600,000 in the single-account category at one bank, where the limit is $250,000. During the grace period all $600,000 stays insured. After it, $350,000 is not.

Nothing about the money changed. The ownership category did.

Two limits on the grace period are worth knowing. The FDIC will not apply it in the rare case where doing so would cause a reduction in coverage rather than preserve it. And there is no grace period at all upon the death of a *beneficiary*, so coverage tied to that beneficiary can drop immediately with no six-month cushion. A POD account naming three children that has quietly become a POD account naming two is a coverage change already in effect.

## Whether the CD keeps running

A certificate of deposit does not mature because its owner died. The term runs, interest accrues, and an auto-renewal instruction set years earlier will still fire on the maturity date unless somebody stops it.

What changes is that the early withdrawal penalty becomes optional. [Federal Reserve](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy.htm) [Regulation D](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/12/204.2) does not order banks to charge anything. It defines what counts as a time deposit — a deposit the customer has no right to withdraw from within six days of deposit unless it carries an early withdrawal penalty of at least seven days' simple interest on amounts taken out in those first six days — and then lists circumstances in which such a deposit may be paid before maturity without that penalty being imposed. Payment upon the death of any owner of the time deposit funds is on that list, alongside payment where an owner is determined to be legally incompetent by a court or other administrative body of competent jurisdiction.

Read the verb. The regulation says such a deposit *may* be paid without the penalty. It permits the bank to waive; it does not order it. And the familiar penalty on a five-year CD, the one quoted as six or twelve months of interest, is not a federal requirement at all. It is a term of the bank's own contract, which means the bank is free to waive it and equally free not to. Some institutions waive on death as stated policy, some waive on request, some decline. The account disclosure is where the answer lives.

A brokered CD changes the question, because there is nobody to ask. The certificate sits in a brokerage account, the issuing bank never opened anything for the decedent, and there is no branch relationship in which a waiver request would land. The broader differences between the two products, which have nothing to do with death, are covered in [brokered CDs versus bank CDs](/2026/08/27/brokered-cds-vs-bank-cds-the-differences-that-matter/). For an estate, what remains is two possible exits, and it is worth establishing which one applies before the executor starts making promises to beneficiaries.

The first exit is a sale. As the [SEC](https://www.investor.gov/introduction-investing/general-resources/news-alerts/alerts-bulletins/investor-bulletins/brokered-cds-investor-bulletin) puts it, brokered CDs can generally be sold at any time through a secondary market — but the same bulletin warns that depending on market conditions, some brokered CDs may not have a secondary market at all. Where there is a buyer, the price is whatever the market offers on the day, and if rates have risen since the purchase that means selling at a discount and recovering less than the original deposit. Where there is no buyer, the estate holds the CD to maturity whether or not that fits the timetable for settling everything else. The same bulletin adds that brokered CDs generally do not have early withdrawal penalties like CDs purchased directly from a bank, which is a separate matter from liquidity: there is no penalty to negotiate because there is no early redemption to request.

The second exit is a survivor's option. Some brokered CDs and agency notes carry one, sometimes called a death put, which lets the estate return the security to the issuer at par plus accrued interest instead of selling it into the market. It is not a standard feature, and the conditions attached to it — including any limits on how much can be put back and any minimum holding period — are set in the offering documents for that specific CD. The prospectus, or a call to the brokerage holding it, is the only reliable check.

Either exit has a prerequisite that surprises people. The position has to be retitled into the name of the estate or the inheriting account before it can be sold or put back, and that is the brokerage's transfer paperwork, which moves on the brokerage's schedule rather than the executor's.

## What the survivor actually does

Sequence matters here, because several of these steps unlock the ones that follow.

1. **Order certified death certificates, and order more than seem necessary.** Each institution keeps the certified copy it receives, so a photocopy will not move the process forward. Banks, brokerages, insurers, and pension administrators each hold on to one, and a second order later costs another fee and another wait.
2. **Notify the bank in writing and get the account flagged.** That stops new activity on sole-name accounts and starts the institution's own internal process, which usually takes longer than survivors expect.
3. **Claim the POD accounts first.** This is the fastest money in the process and it does not wait on the estate, which matters when funeral costs land before letters testamentary do.
4. **Handle the Social Security payment for the month of death.** Entitlement stops before the month of death does: under [20 CFR 404.311](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/20/404.311), entitlement to old-age benefits ends with the month before the month you die. Since the agency pays a month behind, the deposit that lands after a July death is the July payment, and it has to go back. Where it arrived by direct deposit, tell the bank, which under [31 CFR 210.10](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/31/210.10) must return any benefit payments it receives once it becomes aware of the death. Do not cash a check for that month or later. Reporting the death itself is usually already handled, since [SSA](https://www.ssa.gov/personal-record/when-someone-dies) says funeral homes generally report it.
5. **Cancel the auto-renewal on any CD before its maturity date.** A CD that rolls into a new term starts a new penalty period and a new maturity, and the argument for a death-based waiver gets harder once the deposit is technically one that was opened after the owner died.
6. **Restructure balances inside the six months.** This is the deadline nobody is reminded of. The bank will not write to say the grace period is running out, and it is the step people skip because nothing appears to be wrong: the statements still arrive, the balance is unchanged, and the coverage drops on a date that was fixed the day of the death.

## The tax split nobody expects

Interest does not stop at the date of death, and it does not all belong to the same taxpayer.

Interest the bank actually paid or credited before the death goes on the decedent's final Form 1040. Interest that had accrued but had not been credited does not. [IRS Publication 559](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p559) is explicit that for a cash-method taxpayer, only those items actually or constructively received before death are included on the final return, and it treats amounts the decedent had a right to receive but had not collected as income in respect of a decedent — taxable to whoever ends up receiving it, the estate on Form 1041 or the beneficiary directly, and keeping its original character in the recipient's hands.

That distinction does real work on a CD that pays at maturity. Years of accrued interest can sit on the decedent's side of the date line and still belong on the recipient's return rather than on the final 1040. Anyone who uses "earned through the date of death" as the test instead of "paid or credited before death" will put income on the wrong return, and the final 1040 is the one nobody wants to amend two years later.

The practical friction is paperwork. Banks routinely issue one Form 1099-INT for the whole year under the decedent's Social Security number, including interest that was credited months after the death. The publication's answer is to request a corrected form from the payer, and where that fails, to report the full amount on Schedule B and subtract the portion belonging to another recipient on a line identified as a nominee distribution.

Only the interest raises a tax question at all. Deposit principal a beneficiary receives is not income, and inheriting a $40,000 CD does not create $40,000 of taxable anything.

The six-month insurance clock starts on the date of death rather than the date the bank learns of it. By the time an estate is organized enough to think about deposit insurance limits, a good part of that window has usually gone already.
