The maturity date on the certificate reads February 2029. It was opened for five years, $20,000, at a rate that looked reasonable at the time. A 12-month CD at a competitive online bank now pays more than double it, and the branch's answer to the obvious question was one line: closing it early costs 365 days of interest. What that comes to in dollars was not on the statement. Whether it comes out of the interest or out of the $20,000 was not mentioned at all.

Both answers were settled in writing before the account was ever opened. Federal deposit rules require the bank to state, in the account disclosure, "how it is calculated, and the conditions for its assessment." The figure was fixed on the day the CD was funded, and a change in rates does not move it.

Three things follow from how that penalty is written. It is counted in days of interest rather than in interest actually earned, so a CD broken early in its term can return less than was deposited; the IRS form that reports it names both possibilities, "interest or principal forfeited." The interest already credited stays taxable in full, and the penalty is subtracted separately, on Schedule 1 (Form 1040), line 18, which sits in the adjustments section rather than among itemized deductions. And the decision to break is never the rate gap alone. It is the rate gap over the remaining term, measured against a penalty that does not shrink as maturity gets closer.

## What the disclosure has to say, and when

Regulation DD, the [Consumer Financial Protection Bureau](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/)'s rule implementing the Truth in Savings Act, fixes the timing at [12 CFR 1030.4(a)(1)](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1030/4/): "A depository institution shall provide account disclosures to a consumer before an account is opened or a service is provided, whichever is earlier." The same section requires them on request, so a CD's penalty terms can be read before any application begins.

The content list at 1030.4(b) opens with "Account disclosures shall include the following, as applicable," and the time account items sit at paragraph (b)(6):

- **(b)(6)(i) Time requirements.** "The maturity date."
- **(b)(6)(ii) Early withdrawal penalties.** "A statement that a penalty will or may be imposed for early withdrawal, how it is calculated, and the conditions for its assessment."
- **(b)(6)(iii) Withdrawal of interest prior to maturity.** "If compounding occurs during the term and interest may be withdrawn prior to maturity, a statement that the annual percentage yield assumes interest remains on deposit until maturity and that a withdrawal will reduce earnings."
- **(b)(6)(iv) Renewal policies.** "A statement of whether or not the account will renew automatically at maturity. If it will, a statement of whether or not a grace period will be provided and, if so, the length of that period must be stated."

"How it is calculated" is the operative phrase, and the [official interpretations](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1030/Interp-4/) make clear the word "penalty" need not appear: "The term 'penalty' may but need not be used to describe the loss of interest that consumers may incur for early withdrawal of funds from time accounts." The interpretations then list three forms the thing can take:

1. "Monetary penalties, such as '$10.00' or 'seven days' interest plus accrued but uncredited interest.'"
2. "Adverse changes to terms such as a lowering of the interest rate, annual percentage yield, or compounding frequency for funds remaining on deposit."
3. "Reclamation of bonuses."

The second is the one savers miss, because it does not look like a charge. A partial withdrawal that drops the rate on the balance left behind is a penalty under this rule, disclosed in the same clause. The third is why an opening bonus on a CD is exposed the moment the CD is broken, covered in [Savings Account Bonuses: The Fine Print and the Tax Form](/2026/09/19/savings-account-bonuses-the-fine-print-and-the-tax-form/).

One thing on the list is not a Reg DD penalty at all: "Penalties imposed by the Internal Revenue Code for certain withdrawals from IRAs or similar pension or savings plans are not early withdrawal penalties for purposes of this part." A CD inside a retirement account can carry both, the bank's and the tax code's, and the disclosure only describes the first. The retirement side is in [IRA CDs: Insurance, Rollovers, and the RMD Deadline](/2026/09/12/ira-cds-insurance-rollovers-and-the-rmd-deadline/).

Round units are allowed: "Penalties may be stated in months, whether institutions assess the penalty using the actual number of days during the period or using another method such as a number of days that occurs in any actual sequence of the total calendar months involved." So "six months' interest" is not necessarily 180 days, and the disclosure is where the bank's method is spelled out.

[Appendix B to Part 1030](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-12/appendix-Appendix%20B%20to%20Part%201030) gives the model sentence, brackets included: "We [will/may] impose a penalty if you withdraw [any/all] of the [deposited funds/principal] before the maturity date." Each bracket is the bank's choice. The last one is the subject of the next section.

The advertisement is a much weaker source. [12 CFR 1030.8(c)(6)(ii)](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1030/8/) requires an ad for a time account to carry only "A statement that a penalty will or may be imposed for early withdrawal." No amount, no method, no conditions. The rate table settles nothing here. Other lines in the same disclosure worth reading before signing are in [Six Lines in a Bank Disclosure That Decide What You Earn](/2026/08/24/six-lines-in-a-bank-disclosure-that-decide-what-you-earn/).

## When the penalty reaches principal

The [FDIC](https://www.fdic.gov/national-rates-and-rate-caps)'s consumer page on [shopping for a certificate of deposit](https://www.fdic.gov/consumer-resource-center/2023-11/shopping-certificate-deposit) puts the general case plainly: "The terms of most fixed-rate CDs will allow the depositor to pay a fee to redeem the deposit prior to maturity." It also flags the exception that catches people who assumed every CD has an exit price: "Most market-linked CDs do not allow for an early redemption."

Where the fee lands depends on arithmetic the disclosure has already fixed. Take $20,000 in a 60-month CD at 1.36%, the FDIC national average for that term as of August 17, 2026, with a penalty of 365 days of interest. Break it at the 90-day mark:

- Daily interest: $20,000 × 1.36% ÷ 365 = **$0.7452**
- Penalty: $0.7452 × 365 = **$272.00**
- Interest earned in 90 days: $0.7452 × 90 = **$67.07**
- Shortfall: $272.00 − $67.07 = **$204.93**

The closing check is about $19,795, roughly $205 below the deposit. Nothing went wrong and no rule was broken. A penalty measured in a year of interest simply exceeds a quarter of a year of interest, and the difference has only one place to come from.

The general shape: the penalty crosses into principal whenever the penalty period is longer than the time the money has actually been on deposit. A 365-day penalty eats principal for the whole first year of the term. A 90-day penalty does it for the first three months. A [no-penalty CD](/2026/08/28/no-penalty-bump-up-and-add-on-cds-when-the-variants-pay/) avoids the question entirely, usually at a lower rate.

There is a federal floor underneath, from the [Federal Reserve](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy.htm)'s Regulation D. [12 CFR 204.2(c)(1)(i)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-12/section-204.2) defines a time deposit as one the depositor may not withdraw from within six days of deposit "unless the deposit is subject to an early withdrawal penalty of at least seven days' simple interest on amounts withdrawn within the first six days after deposit," and the same seven-day minimum applies again to amounts withdrawn within six days after each partial withdrawal. Money parked in a CD on Monday and pulled back on Wednesday comes back short by rule, not by choice.

A second, quieter route to the same place is closing the account between interest credit dates. Section 1030.4(b)(2)(ii) requires the warning: "If consumers will forfeit interest if they close the account before accrued interest is credited, a statement that interest will not be paid in such cases." The interpretation leaves the trigger to the contract, so what counts as closing is whatever the deposit agreement says it is: "An institution may, subject to state or other law, provide in its deposit contracts the actions by consumers that will be treated as closing the account and that will result in the forfeiture of accrued but uncredited interest."

## Interest that already left the account

The penalty is a fixed number of days of interest. What absorbs it is whatever interest is still sitting inside the CD. That makes the payout schedule, which looks like a convenience question when the account is opened, part of the penalty calculation.

Reg DD requires the schedule to be disclosed at 1030.4(b)(2)(i), "The frequency with which interest is compounded and credited," and requires the warning at (b)(6)(iii) that an APY quoted on a compounding CD "assumes interest remains on deposit until maturity and that a withdrawal will reduce earnings."

Two versions of one CD, $25,000 for three years at a hypothetical 3% APY with a 180-day penalty, broken in month 10:

| Interest handling | Interest inside the CD at month 10 | Penalty | Where the penalty lands |
|---|---|---|---|
| Compounded, paid at maturity | about $625 | $369.86 | Entirely out of accrued interest |
| Paid monthly to a checking account | at most about $62 | $369.86 | At least about $308 out of principal |

The saver in the second row is not $308 poorer than the first. That money was already paid out month by month. But the closing balance is below the deposit, and the statement says so. Choosing monthly payouts is a decision about liquidity, not about yield, and it changes the shape of an early exit.

For long CDs the schedule also changes which tax form arrives. The [Instructions for Forms 1099-INT and 1099-OID](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i1099int) require Form 1099-OID from "an issuer of a certificate of deposit (CD) made, purchased, or renewed after 1970 if the CD has OID and a term of more than 1 year." A multi-year CD that holds all its interest until maturity produces original issue discount, reported and taxed as it accrues rather than when the money is handed over. The penalty has its own box on that form too.

## What the penalty is worth on the tax return

Two separate numbers go on the return, and netting them yourself is the common mistake. The 1099-INT instructions are explicit about the second one, Box 2: "Enter interest or principal forfeited because of an early withdrawal of time deposits, such as an early withdrawal from a certificate of deposit (CD), that is deductible from gross income by the recipient. Do not reduce the amount reported in box 1 by the amount of the forfeiture."

So Box 1 still shows the full interest credited during the year, including interest the bank took straight back. The [recipient instructions printed on the form](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/f1099int.pdf) give the other side: "Box 2. Shows interest or principal forfeited because of early withdrawal of time savings. You may deduct this amount to figure your adjusted gross income on your income tax return. See the Instructions for Form 1040 to see where to take the deduction."

Where to take it is [Schedule 1 (Form 1040)](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/f1040s1.pdf), Part II, Adjustments to Income, line 18, printed on the 2025 form as "Penalty on early withdrawal of savings." The practical consequence is in the phrase the form itself uses, "to figure your adjusted gross income": adjustments come off before the standard deduction, so the penalty does not require itemizing to be worth anything. For a CD reported as original issue discount, the same amount appears in Box 3 of Form 1099-OID, described in nearly the same words as "interest or principal forfeited because of an early withdrawal of time deposits, such as an early withdrawal from a CD, that is deductible from gross income by the recipient."

What that saves depends on the marginal rate. On the $272.00 penalty from the example above:

| Hypothetical marginal rate | Tax saved by the deduction | Net cost of the penalty |
|---|---|---|
| 12% | $32.64 | $239.36 |
| 22% | $59.84 | $212.16 |
| 24% | $65.28 | $206.72 |

Two cautions. The bank decides what goes in the box, and the instructions point filers to Rev. Ruls. 75-20 and 75-21 for determining the deductible forfeiture, so a reclaimed bonus or an unusual penalty structure may not be reported the way a depositor expects. And the deduction lands in the year of the withdrawal, while the replacement interest it was spent to capture may arrive in the next one. The broader arithmetic of taxed interest is in [The Tax Bite on Savings Interest: What Your Real Yield Is](/2026/08/26/the-tax-bite-on-savings-interest-what-your-real-yield-is/).

## Running the break-even when rates have risen

The inputs all come from the disclosure: the penalty in days, the current rate, the maturity date. Against them goes one outside number, the rate available now.

Take $40,000 in a CD at 1.5% APY with a 270-day penalty, and a replacement paying a hypothetical 4%. Daily interest is $40,000 × 1.5% ÷ 365 = $1.6438, so the penalty is $443.84 whenever the CD is broken. The extra interest, by contrast, depends entirely on how much term is left:

| Remaining term | Extra interest at 4% vs 1.5% | Penalty | Net |
|---|---|---|---|
| 18 months | $1,500.00 | $443.84 | +$1,056.16 |
| 12 months | $1,000.00 | $443.84 | +$556.16 |
| 5 months | $416.67 | $443.84 | −$27.17 |

The bottom row is the warning. Five months from maturity, a 2.5-point improvement in rate still loses to the penalty, and waiting those five months captures the whole improvement for nothing.

Now add the tax layer, which is where intuition tends to go wrong. At a hypothetical 22% marginal rate, the penalty costs $346.20 after the deduction, and the $1,500 of extra interest is worth $1,170.00 after tax. The net is $823.80, which is exactly the pre-tax $1,056.16 multiplied by 0.78. Both sides scale by the same factor, so the deduction lowers what the exit costs without changing whether the exit is worth making.

That equivalence breaks in specific cases worth checking: a replacement that is taxed differently, such as Treasury interest exempt from state tax, or a year in which the deduction lands in a higher bracket than the interest it bought. Otherwise the pre-tax comparison is the decision, and the deduction is a rebate on the cost.

Whether a wide enough gap exists at all is a market question. [FDIC national average](https://www.fdic.gov/national-rates-and-rate-caps) CD rates as of August 17, 2026 were 1.71% at 12 months and 1.36% at 60 months, the highest figure on the curve sitting at one year and every longer term paying less. Averages that flat mean the gap usually comes from moving to a competitive institution rather than from the rate environment, and a longer replacement term is not being paid for. The full arithmetic, including partial withdrawals, is worked through in [When Breaking a CD Actually Pays: Run the Break-Even](/2026/08/25/when-breaking-a-cd-actually-pays-run-the-break-even/).

## The exits that cost nothing

Before paying a penalty, check whether waiting removes it. Reg DD builds in two notices designed for exactly this moment.

For an auto-renewing CD longer than one month, [12 CFR 1030.5(b)](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1030/5/) requires pre-maturity disclosures: "The disclosures shall be mailed or delivered at least 30 calendar days before maturity of the existing account." There is an alternative timing rule tied to the grace period: "the disclosures may be mailed or delivered at least 20 calendar days before the end of the grace period on the existing account, provided a grace period of at least five calendar days is allowed." For maturities longer than a year, that notice carries the full set of 1030.4(b) disclosures for the new term.

For a CD longer than one year that does not renew on its own, 1030.5(c) asks for less but still guarantees a warning: "institutions shall disclose to consumers the maturity date and whether interest will be paid after maturity," mailed or delivered "at least 10 calendar days before maturity of the existing account."

Both notices exist because the grace period, disclosed under 1030.4(b)(6)(iv), is the one window in which the money moves for nothing. A saver four months from maturity who breaks the CD pays a penalty for the privilege of being four months early.

Terms cannot be rewritten underneath a locked CD without warning either. Section 1030.5(a)(1) requires advance notice "of any change in a term required to be disclosed under § 1030.4(b) of this part if the change may reduce the annual percentage yield or adversely affect the consumer," at least 30 calendar days ahead. The exceptions are narrow, and one applies here: no notice is required for "Changes in any term for time accounts with maturities of one month or less." Very short CDs are the exception to the stability everything else assumes. What happens to a locked CD when the institution itself changes hands is covered in [When Your Bank Is Acquired, Not Failed: What Happens to Your CD](/2026/09/03/when-your-bank-is-acquired-not-failed-what-happens-to-your-cd/).

## Common questions

**Can the penalty be larger than the interest the CD earned?** Yes, and the rules assume it. The model clause in Appendix B lets a bank write the penalty against "[deposited funds/principal]," and Box 2 of Form 1099-INT reports "interest or principal forfeited." Any penalty period longer than the time the money has been on deposit takes the difference out of principal.

**Do I report interest the bank took back?** Yes. The 1099-INT instructions tell the bank "Do not reduce the amount reported in box 1 by the amount of the forfeiture." The full interest goes on the return as income, and the penalty goes on Schedule 1, line 18, as a separate deduction.

**Is the deduction available without itemizing?** Line 18 sits in Part II of Schedule 1, Adjustments to Income, not on Schedule A, and the recipient instructions on Form 1099-INT say the amount is deducted "to figure your adjusted gross income." How it interacts with the rest of a particular return is a question for a tax professional.

**Can the bank raise the penalty on a CD I already hold?** A change to a disclosed term that adversely affects the consumer requires 30 calendar days' advance notice under 1030.5(a)(1). The exception is time accounts with maturities of one month or less, where any term can change without notice.

**What if I only need part of the money?** Check whether the disclosure permits partial withdrawal, and read what happens to the balance left behind. A lower rate on the remainder counts as an early withdrawal penalty under the official interpretations, and Regulation D re-applies its seven-day minimum to amounts withdrawn within six days after each partial withdrawal.

The penalty on a CD is one of the few bank charges written out in full before the money moves, in a document the institution has to hand over on request. Read it while the rate still looks reasonable, not on the day it stops looking that way.
