FDIC insurance covers deposits at an insured bank, up to $250,000 per depositor, per insured bank, per ownership category. That one sentence is the entire guarantee. Everything else is detail about where its edges fall.

The edges are where the useful information sits. A CD at an insured bank is covered. A bond fund bought through that same bank's brokerage arm is not. Money held in an app that partners with a bank may or may not be, depending on how the arrangement is documented. Deposits at a bank that fails are insured; investments are not, regardless of which lobby you bought them in.

What follows is a product-by-product table of what is inside the guarantee and what is outside it, the three cases where the answer is not obvious from the account screen, and a verification routine that takes about a minute per bank. Where the answer depends on your specific account titling, I point you to the FDIC's own estimator instead of a rule of thumb.

## The scope of deposit insurance

Coverage is automatic. Open a deposit account at an FDIC-insured bank and the insurance attaches — there is no form to file, and the depositor pays nothing for it.

The standard maximum is $250,000 per depositor, per insured bank, per ownership category. Each phrase constrains the others.

- **Per depositor** means you, the person — not each account you open.
- **Per insured bank** means the charter. One company can run several consumer brands on a single charter, and the FDIC's BankFind tool shows which names belong to which insured institution.
- **Per ownership category** means how the account is titled. Single, joint, certain retirement accounts, and revocable trust accounts are insured separately from one another at the same bank.

Credit unions work the same way through a different insurer: the National Credit Union Administration insures share accounts at federally insured credit unions to the same $250,000 standard limit.

## Covered and not covered

| What you hold | Insured by the FDIC? |
|---|---|
| Savings account | Yes — a deposit |
| Checking account | Yes — a deposit |
| Money market deposit account | Yes — a deposit |
| Certificate of deposit held at the bank | Yes — a deposit |
| Bank deposits held inside an IRA | Yes — certain retirement category |
| Stocks, bonds, mutual funds | No — investments |
| Money market *mutual* funds | No — investments |
| Annuities and life insurance policies | No |
| Crypto assets | No |
| Contents of a safe deposit box | No — not a deposit |

The pattern behind the table: if the money is something you deposited and the bank owes it back to you at face value, it is a deposit. If its value moves with a market, it is not, and buying it inside a bank branch does not change that.

### The money market naming collision

Two different products share most of a name. A money market deposit account is a deposit at the bank, insured like savings. A money market mutual fund is a security bought through a brokerage; it is not a deposit and carries no FDIC coverage. The account agreement will call one a deposit account and the other a fund, and the fund version comes with a prospectus. If you are unsure which one you hold, that prospectus is the tell.

## Brokered CDs, app balances, and credit unions

Three situations need one extra step before you can call the money insured.

**A CD bought through a brokerage.** The CD is issued by a bank, and the confirmation or disclosure names that bank. It is that bank's $250,000 limit that applies to you, and any deposits you already hold directly at the same bank in the same ownership category are added in before the limit is tested.

**Money in an app that is not itself a bank.** Deposit insurance responds to the failure of an insured bank. Read the app's terms to see which insured bank or banks hold the funds and how the account is titled. That document, not the marketing page, is what answers the question.

**Accounts at a credit union.** Look for NCUA share insurance rather than FDIC coverage — same standard limit, different agency, different lookup tool.

## A one-minute verification routine

1. Find the legal name of the institution. It appears in the deposit agreement and usually in the fine print of the account page. The brand you signed up with is not always the charter.
2. Look that legal name up in the FDIC's BankFind tool, or the NCUA's research tool for a credit union. Confirm the institution is insured, and note which other brands share the charter.
3. Add up everything you hold at that charter in the same ownership category — checking, savings, money market, CDs, and interest that has already posted.
4. If the total is anywhere near $250,000, run the accounts through the FDIC's EDIE estimator, which applies the current rules to your exact titling.

Run this once per bank, not once per account.

## Coverage and yield as separate questions

Insurance does not scale with the rate. A savings account paying very little and a CD paying more are equally insured if both are deposits at the same insured bank; the higher rate buys no extra protection.

What the rate changes is the interest. FDIC national average rates as of August 17, 2026 were 0.38% APY for savings, 0.07% APY for interest checking, 0.63% APY for money market accounts, and 1.71% APY for a 12 month CD. Those are national averages published by the FDIC, not offers from any particular bank.

Take $10,000 held for one full year at those averages:

- Savings at 0.38% APY: $10,000 × 0.0038 = **$38.00**, ending balance $10,038.00.
- A 12 month CD at 1.71% APY: $10,000 × 0.0171 = **$171.00**, ending balance $10,171.00.
- Difference over the year: $171.00 − $38.00 = **$133.00**.

The same $10,000 at the 0.63% money market average earns $10,000 × 0.0063 = **$63.00**, and at the 0.07% interest checking average it earns $10,000 × 0.0007 = **$7.00** — a gap of $63.00 − $7.00 = **$56.00** between two products that are both fully insured deposits.

APY already includes the effect of compounding while a stated interest rate does not, so compare APY against APY and nothing else. The averages themselves move with two forces you can watch rather than predict: the Federal Reserve's policy rate, which sets the cost of money in the system, and competition among institutions for deposit balances. Neither tells you what any single bank will pay next month, and this post does not try to guess.

The CD's cost is access. CDs generally carry an early withdrawal penalty, stated in the disclosure and often quoted as a number of months of interest. Say a disclosure states three months of interest on that $10,000 CD. Using simple monthly proration of the $171.00 annual figure, one month is $171.00 ÷ 12 = $14.25, so the penalty is $14.25 × 3 = **$42.75**. Your own disclosure states both the penalty length and the bank's calculation method, and it is worth reading before the money goes in.

One mechanical note on savings accounts: Regulation D's six-transfer limit was suspended in 2020, but many banks still set their own transfer limits. That is now a question for your account agreement rather than a federal rule.

## The mechanics of a bank closing

All of this matters at exactly one moment. When a bank's chartering authority closes it, the FDIC steps in as receiver. Insured deposits are paid out or transferred to another insured bank, and the FDIC publishes a page for each closed institution with the specifics for its customers.

Balances above the insured limit are not covered. They become claims against the receivership, and whether any of that money comes back depends on what the receivership recovers. That is the line the $250,000 figure is actually drawing.

## Your next five minutes

- Write down every institution where you hold deposits, using legal names.
- Check each one in BankFind, or the NCUA tool for credit unions, and note any brands sharing a charter.
- Total your balances per charter and per ownership category — not per account.
- For any total within sight of $250,000, run EDIE and keep the result with your records.

The guarantee itself is simple. Applying it to your accounts is a few minutes of clerical work, and that clerical work is the only part that can go wrong.
