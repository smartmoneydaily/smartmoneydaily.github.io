On November 10 the IRS put out a reminder, [IR-2026-135](https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/irs-reminder-permanent-charitable-contribution-deduction-begins-for-returns-filed-next-year-for-taxpayers-who-do-not-itemize), about a deduction people who take the standard deduction haven't had since 2021. For cash gifts made in 2026, you can deduct up to $1,000 even if you don't itemize, or up to $2,000 on a joint return. You claim it on the 2026 return you file in 2027. The old version covered only 2021 and stopped at $300, or $600 on a joint return. The 2025 tax law, which the IRS now calls the Working Families Tax Cuts, raised the cap and dropped the 2021-only wording from [section 170(p)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/170) of the tax code, so it applies every year from 2026 on.

If the whole deduction falls in the 22% bracket, a single filer's $1,000 gift cuts federal income tax by $220. A married couple whose $2,000 deduction falls in the 12% bracket saves $240. The rules on what counts are narrower than "anything you gave to a good cause," though, and the paperwork rule has no small-gift exception. With about six weeks left in 2026, those rules decide which December gifts end up on the return.

## Who gets it, and what it's worth

You qualify if you don't itemize. Usually that means you take the standard deduction. The basic amount for 2026 is $16,100 for single filers and married people filing separately, $32,200 for joint filers and $24,150 for heads of household, according to the IRS's [2026 inflation adjustments](https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/irs-releases-tax-inflation-adjustments-for-tax-year-2026-including-amendments-from-the-one-big-beautiful-bill). You claim the charity deduction in addition to it. The IRS's one-page [Publication 6196](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/p6196.pdf) adds that you don't have to be taking the standard deduction to use it. It names nonresidents, and married people filing separately whose spouse itemizes, as examples of people who don't itemize but can't take the standard deduction either.

The cap is per return. Section 170(p) limits the deduction to $1,000, or $2,000 on a joint return, so married people filing separately get up to $1,000 on each return that doesn't itemize, and a single person who gives $3,000 can deduct at most $1,000. Like other cash gifts, these also generally can't exceed 60% of your contribution base (AGI figured without any net operating loss carryback), a limit that only matters at very low incomes.

[Section 63(b)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/63) lists this deduction among the amounts subtracted from adjusted gross income to reach taxable income, in the same list as the standard deduction. So it lowers taxable income but not AGI. It won't change the income used to figure [how much of your Social Security is taxed](/2026/10/11/does-savings-interest-make-your-social-security-taxable/) or your premium tax credit.

At 2026 rates, the deduction works out like this, assuming it doesn't change any other deduction or credit on your return. The brackets come from the same IRS release: for single filers, 12% applies to taxable income over $12,400 and 22% over $50,400; for joint filers, 22% applies to taxable income over $100,800.

| Filer | Bracket the deduction falls in | Cash given in 2026 | Deduction | Federal tax saved |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Single | 12% | $1,000 | $1,000 | $120 |
| Single | 22% | $1,000 | $1,000 | $220 |
| Single | 22% | $2,500 | $1,000 | $220 |
| Married filing jointly | 12% | $2,000 | $2,000 | $240 |
| Married filing jointly | 22% | $2,000 | $2,000 | $440 |

If the deduction takes your taxable income across a bracket line, part of the saving is figured at each rate.

## Cash means money, not the coat you dropped off

The deduction covers cash contributions only. [Publication 526](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p526) defines those broadly: "payments made by cash, check, electronic funds transfer, online payment service, debit card, credit card, payroll deduction, or a transfer of a gift card redeemable for cash." What doesn't count is property. The IRS release says plainly that noncash contributions don't qualify, so a bag of clothes, a used laptop or shares of stock get nothing under this rule. Your time doesn't count either; Publication 526 says you can't deduct the value of your time or services as a volunteer.

Gifts that come with something in return are generally only partly deductible. If you pay more than fair market value for a charity dinner or show, you can deduct only the amount above the value of what you received, and Publication 526 says you must pay that excess intending it as a gift. Not using the ticket doesn't change the math; handing it back to the charity for resale does, since then the whole payment counts. Say a $150 gala ticket comes with a dinner the charity values at $60. The deductible part is $90.

## Which charities count

The statute limits the deduction to gifts to organizations described in section 170(b)(1)(A), a list that includes churches, schools, hospitals, government units and publicly supported charities, and then carves out two kinds that would otherwise qualify. Gifts to supporting organizations under section 509(a)(3) don't count, and neither do gifts "for the establishment of a new, or maintenance of an existing, donor advised fund." If you've been moving year-end money into a donor-advised fund, those deposits stay outside this deduction.

To check a charity, Publication 6196 points to the IRS Tax Exempt Organization Search on IRS.gov. Pick "Pub. 78 Data" from the first menu, search by name or EIN, and look at the deductibility code. Codes such as 50% (60% for cash contributions), PC, POF or FED generally mean the organization can receive deductible gifts. The publication also warns that some eligible groups, including certain churches, government bodies and organizations under a group exemption, may not show up on their own in the search, so a missing listing doesn't settle it.

Gifts to people don't count at all, even when the cause is sympathetic. Publication 526 says you can't deduct contributions earmarked for a particular individual or family, even if they pass through a qualified organization. A fundraiser for one family's medical bills falls on that side of the line. A gift to a charity's general disaster relief fund doesn't.

## The record rule has no minimum

[Section 170(f)(17)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/170) says no deduction is allowed for "any contribution of a cash, check, or other monetary gift" unless you keep a bank record or a written communication from the charity showing its name, the date and the amount. Publication 526 adds "regardless of the amount." Dollar bills dropped in a bell ringer's kettle leave no bank record and usually no receipt, so they don't count unless the charity gives you a written record of the gift.

If you give from an account, the record usually already exists. A canceled check, a bank or credit union statement, a credit card statement, an electronic funds transfer receipt, or a scanned image of both sides of a canceled check from your bank's website all work, as long as they show the charity's name, the date and the amount.

Gifts of $250 or more need more. You need a contemporaneous written acknowledgment from the charity that states the amount and whether you got any goods or services in return. If you did, it has to describe them and give a good-faith estimate of their value, or say that the only benefit was an intangible religious one. "Contemporaneous" means you have it by the earlier of the date you file or the return's due date, including extensions. Publication 526 notes that charities typically send these by January 31. Separate gifts aren't added together for the $250 test, so $25 a week to your church stays a string of $25 gifts. Payroll giving has its own paperwork at any amount: a pay stub, W-2 or other employer document showing the date and amount, plus a pledge card or similar document prepared by or for the charity that names it and says it gives nothing in return for payroll gifts. Each paycheck's deduction counts as a separate contribution for the $250 test, and with those payroll records a single paycheck of $250 or more doesn't need a separate acknowledgment.

## December 31 is the line, and how you pay sets the date

Only gifts made in 2026 count toward the 2026 return. Publication 526's timing rules are worth knowing before the last week of December:

- A check counts on the date you mail it, as long as it clears.
- A credit card gift counts in the year you make the charge, even if you pay the bill in 2027.
- A gift by text message counts in the year you send the text, if it's charged to your phone bill.
- A pay-by-phone account gift counts on the date the financial institution pays it.

So a card gift on the evening of December 31 counts for 2026. A check you write on December 31 and mail on January 2 doesn't.

## Itemizers got a floor instead

Starting in 2026, people who itemize lose part of their charitable deduction to a new floor. Section 170(b)(1)(I) allows their charitable deduction "only to the extent that the aggregate of such contributions exceeds 0.5 percent" of their contribution base. With a contribution base of $150,000, the first $750 of the year's gifts doesn't count.

The non-itemizer deduction is figured without that floor; section 170(p) says so directly. A couple who take the standard deduction and give $2,000 deduct $2,000. A couple who itemize with that $150,000 contribution base and give $2,000 deduct $1,250 of it. That doesn't make itemizing a mistake. Compare your itemized total, after the floor, with the standard deduction plus the non-itemizer charity deduction. For that couple, assuming no extra standard deduction for age or blindness, it's itemized deductions against $34,200, not $32,200.

## How I'd pay for it out of savings

The cap limits the deduction, not the giving. Above $1,000 (or $2,000 jointly), extra cash gifts don't add to this deduction, so I wouldn't count on a tax break for anything past that line.

I'd also give from an account rather than a wallet. For anything under $250, the bank or card statement does the job, and that's one less piece of paper to keep. For gifts of $250 or more, I'd keep the charity's acknowledgment email in the same folder as the 1099-INT forms for my savings accounts, since both get used at the same time next spring.

If you leave your withholding and estimated payments as they are, the tax saving shows up as a smaller balance due or a bigger refund when you file in 2027, not in December. If I put a December gift on a credit card for the timing, I'd pay the card off in full. Rates on card accounts charged interest [averaged 22.36% in the third quarter](/2026/11/10/rates-on-card-accounts-charged-interest-averaged-2236-in-q3-and-revolving-credit-rose-in-september/). Carrying a $1,000 gift on the card for six months at that rate costs about $112 in interest on simple monthly math, nearly all of a $120 saving.

Since the rule is permanent, each year gets its own $1,000 or $2,000. For a household that never itemizes, moving 2027's gifts into 2026 can cost something in two ways: the combined total can go over the cap, and if your 2026 bracket is lower than your 2027 bracket, the same gift saves less in 2026. Bunching two years of gifts into one is a tool for people close to the itemizing line, and even then it's worth running the numbers both ways.

If you're 70½ or older and give from an IRA, a qualified charitable distribution sent straight from the IRA trustee (not from an ongoing SEP or SIMPLE IRA) works differently. Publication 526 says it can be nontaxable, and if it is, you can't also deduct it. The [2027 retirement limits post](/2026/11/16/the-ira-limit-stays-at-7500-for-2027-while-the-401k-limit-rises-1000-to-25500/) has next year's cap.

The next date on my calendar for this is January 31, 2027, the point by which charities typically send year-end acknowledgments. If one for a $250-plus gift hasn't shown up by then, I'd ask the charity for it before filing, not after.
