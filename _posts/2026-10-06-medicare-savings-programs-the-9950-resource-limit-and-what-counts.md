A retiree living mostly on a monthly Social Security check, with about $8,000 spread across checking and savings, sits down before [Medicare Open Enrollment](https://www.medicare.gov/basics/get-started-with-medicare/get-more-coverage/joining-a-plan) to review Medicare costs and assumes that savings balance rules out any help with the Part B premium. Under the 2026 federal figures, the ones Medicare.gov lists now, it does not, at least not by itself. The resource limit for the three Medicare Savings Programs that pay Part B premiums is $9,950 for an individual and $14,910 for a married couple, according to [Medicare.gov's Medicare Savings Programs page](https://www.medicare.gov/basics/costs/help/medicare-savings-programs), and a person whose counted resources are below that line, and whose income is below a separate monthly limit, can qualify to have the state pay the [$202.90 monthly Part B premium](https://www.medicare.gov/basics/costs/medicare-costs).

Two details change the picture for many households. First, not everything you own counts. Medicare lists money in checking, savings and retirement accounts, plus stocks and bonds, as counted resources, but says states don't count your home, one car, a burial plot, up to $1,500 you have set aside for burial expenses, furniture, or other household and personal items. Second, the federal figures are not the last word. Medicare says you may still qualify in your state even if your income or resources are higher than the federal limits, because some states don't count certain types or amounts of income or resources.

In practice: add up only what counts, compare it with $9,950 or $14,910, and if you are close or even somewhat over, apply through your state anyway. Medicare's instruction on this is direct: "Even if you don't think you qualify, you should still apply." Getting into any of the three Part B programs also brings [Extra Help](https://www.medicare.gov/basics/costs/help/drug-costs) with prescription drug costs automatically, which has its own, higher resource limit of $18,090 for an individual and $36,100 for a married couple.

## What a Medicare Savings Program pays for

A Medicare Savings Program, or MSP, is help from your state with Medicare costs. The Medicare page puts it simply: these programs help pay Part A and Part B premiums, and for some people they "might also pay your Part A and Part B deductibles, coinsurance, and copayments." You don't apply through Medicare. You apply through your state, and the state decides which program, if any, you qualify for.

There are four programs, and they differ in what they pay and whom they serve.

| Program | What it helps pay for | Who it is for |
|---|---|---|
| Qualified Medicare Beneficiary (QMB) | Part A premiums (if you don't have premium-free Part A); Part B premiums, deductibles, coinsurance and copayments for services and items Medicare covers | People under the QMB income and resource limits |
| Specified Low-Income Medicare Beneficiary (SLMB) | Part B premiums only | People with both Part A and Part B under the SLMB limits |
| Qualifying Individual (QI) | Part B premiums | People with both Part A and Part B who don't qualify for any other Medicaid coverage or benefits |
| Qualified Disabled & Working Individual (QDWI) | Part A premiums only | People with a disability who are working and lost Social Security disability benefits and premium-free Part A because they returned to work |

For most retirees reviewing their budget, the line that matters is the Part B premium. Medicare's costs page lists the 2026 standard Part B premium as $202.90 each month, "or higher depending on your income," and notes that the amount can change each year. QMB, SLMB and QI all help with that premium. QDWI does not; it is a Part A program for a specific group of working people with disabilities.

QMB goes further than the other two. If you qualify for QMB, Medicare says providers "aren't allowed to bill you for services and items Medicare covers, including deductibles, coinsurance, and copayments." The page adds that you may get a bill for a small Medicaid copayment if one applies, and it tells QMB enrollees to show both their Medicare card and their Medicaid card (or QMB card) each time they get care. People in Original Medicare can also show a Medicare Summary Notice, the claims statement, because it shows they are in the QMB program.

That billing protection is the reason the QMB line in the table carries so much weight. A Part B premium is a known monthly cost. Total spending on deductibles, coinsurance and copayments depends on the care you end up receiving, so for someone with a fixed income it can be the harder part of the budget to predict.

## The 2026 federal income and resource limits

Each program has two tests: a monthly income limit and a resource limit. Here are the 2026 federal figures as Medicare publishes them.

| Program | Monthly income limit, individual | Monthly income limit, married couple | Resource limit, individual | Resource limit, married couple |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| QMB | $1,350 | $1,824 | $9,950 | $14,910 |
| SLMB | $1,616 | $2,184 | $9,950 | $14,910 |
| QI | $1,816 | $2,455 | $9,950 | $14,910 |
| QDWI | $5,405 | $7,299 | $4,000 | $6,000 |

Medicare notes under each table that income limits are slightly higher in Alaska and Hawaii, and it says the limits go up each year.

In the resource columns, QMB, SLMB and QI share the same limit. A single person needs counted resources below $9,950 for any of the three, and a married couple needs them below $14,910. What separates the three programs is income. The SLMB monthly income limit for an individual is $266 higher than the QMB limit ($1,616 minus $1,350), and the QI limit is another $200 higher ($1,816 minus $1,616). For married couples the steps are $360 (from $1,824 to $2,184) and $271 (from $2,184 to $2,455).

Put another way, the federal tables let an individual's monthly income run $466 above the QMB limit and still fall under the QI limit, and a couple's run $631 above the QMB limit. A person can be over the line for the most generous program and still be in range for one that pays the Part B premium.

The resource limit works differently. Because it is the same across QMB, SLMB and QI, a household above $9,950 or $14,910 in counted resources is over the federal resource limit for all three at once. That makes the resource question worth getting right: it decides whether the income tiers matter at all under the federal figures.

QDWI stands apart, with much higher income limits and much lower resource limits: $4,000 for an individual and $6,000 for a couple. It serves a narrow group, and most of this article is about the three programs that help with Part B.

## What counts toward the $9,950 limit

The phrase "resource limit" is easy to misread as a cap on what is in the bank. It is a cap on counted resources, and Medicare's page spells out which assets fall on each side of the line in a help panel titled "What counts in resource limits?"

| Counted as a resource | Not counted by states |
|---|---|
| Money in a checking account | Your home |
| Money in a savings account | One car |
| Money in a retirement account | Burial plot |
| Stocks | Up to $1,500 for burial expenses, if you have put that money aside |
| Bonds | Furniture |
| | Other household and personal items |

Several things follow from that list.

The $9,950 figure is a total, not a per-account limit. A person with $5,000 in checking and $5,000 in savings has $10,000 in counted resources, even though neither account alone is near the limit. Splitting money across accounts or banks does not change the sum.

The largest asset many retirees own, their home, is on the not-counted side. So is one car. A homeowner with a paid-off house and a car can still have counted resources well under $9,950, because neither of those appears in the total.

The burial exclusion has a condition attached. Medicare's wording is "Up to $1,500 for burial expenses if you have put that money aside." The words "put that money aside" matter. Medicare's page does not say how a state wants that money held or documented, so that is a question for your state Medicaid office when you apply. A burial plot is listed separately, so the plot itself is not counted either.

Everyday possessions are excluded. Furniture and "other household and personal items" are not counted, so a sofa or a set of dishes never enters the total.

The same counted and not-counted lists appear on Medicare's Extra Help page for the drug-cost program, so the resource definitions line up across the two forms of help even though the dollar limits differ.

## Retirement accounts, stocks and bonds

Retirement accounts are on the counted side of Medicare's list, and that is easy to overlook. Many savers think of an IRA or a workplace plan as money that is locked away until later, and so not really "available." Medicare's list does not make that distinction. It puts "money in a checking, savings, or retirement account" in the same line.

For a household whose savings are mostly in a retirement account, that can be the deciding fact. Someone with $2,000 in checking and a $9,000 retirement account has $11,000 in counted resources under that list, which is over $9,950, even though the checking balance alone looks small.

Stocks and bonds are on the counted list as well. That covers holdings people sometimes forget when they tally up savings: a few shares inherited years ago, or savings bonds in a drawer. If they are stocks or bonds, Medicare's list counts them.

What the Medicare page does not spell out is how a state values each of these assets, or whether it treats any specific type of account differently. It says only that some states don't count certain types or specific amounts of resources. That is a real gap in what the federal page can tell you, and it is one reason the page sends people to their state. If most of your counted resources sit in a retirement account, ask the state Medicaid office how it treats that account before deciding whether to apply. Better still, apply and let the state make the determination, which is what Medicare recommends.

## Worked example: a single retiree near the line

Take a single person in 2026 with these assets:

- Checking account: $3,400
- Savings account: $4,800
- IRA: $2,900
- Money set aside for burial expenses: $1,500
- A home and one car

Apply Medicare's list. The checking, savings and IRA balances count. The burial money, up to $1,500, does not count if it has been set aside, and the home and car do not count.

Counted resources: $3,400 + $4,800 + $2,900 = $11,100.

Federal resource limit for QMB, SLMB and QI: $9,950.

Difference: $11,100 − $9,950 = $1,150 over the federal limit.

Without the IRA, the count would be $3,400 + $4,800 = $8,200, which is $1,750 under the limit. The retirement account is what puts this person over the federal figure.

This example is about how the counting works, not a suggestion to move or spend money. Three facts from Medicare's pages matter more for this person than the $1,150 gap.

First, the state may not use the federal limit. Medicare says you may still qualify in your state even if your resources are higher than the federal limits. Contacting the state tells you which rules it uses, and applying gets you an official determination.

Second, the Extra Help resource limit is higher. For an individual it is $18,090, and $11,100 is $6,990 below it ($18,090 − $11,100). If the person's income also fits the Extra Help income limit, help with drug costs may still be available even if the state denies an MSP.

Third, the value of the premium help is large relative to the gap. At $202.90 a month, the standard Part B premium comes to $202.90 × 12 = $2,434.80 for a full year. That is the amount at stake in the MSP decision for one person paying the standard premium, before counting any deductibles or coinsurance that QMB would also cover.

The point of the arithmetic is to show where the line actually falls. A person who had looked only at a bank balance of $8,200 might have assumed either that they were safely under or that "having savings" disqualified them. Neither is a reliable shortcut. The total of counted resources, compared with the limit your state uses, is what decides the question.

## Worked example: a married couple and the Part B math

Now a married couple, both on Medicare, with:

- Joint checking: $2,600
- Joint savings: $7,400
- Bonds: $3,000
- A home, one car and household furniture

Counted resources: $2,600 + $7,400 + $3,000 = $13,000.

Federal resource limit for a married couple: $14,910.

Difference: $14,910 − $13,000 = $1,910 under the limit.

So the couple passes the federal resource test. The next question is income. Suppose the state counts the couple's income at $2,100 a month. Compare that with the federal married-couple income limits:

- QMB: $1,824. The couple is $2,100 − $1,824 = $276 over.
- SLMB: $2,184. The couple is $2,184 − $2,100 = $84 under.
- QI: $2,455. The couple is also under this one.

Under the federal figures, then, this couple would fit SLMB, which pays Part B premiums only. The state decides which program applies, and its rules on what income counts can change the result, but the federal tables point to SLMB.

The value of that help: two standard Part B premiums cost 2 × $202.90 = $405.80 a month, or $405.80 × 12 = $4,869.60 a year at the 2026 rate. Both members of the couple would also get Extra Help automatically, because SLMB is one of the programs Medicare names as bringing Extra Help with it.

The couple's margins are narrow. They are $1,910 under the resource limit and only $84 under the SLMB income limit. A small change in income could move them from one tier to another, or out of range under the federal figures. Medicare says the limits go up each year, so the margins are worth rechecking annually, and QI in particular requires a new application every year.

## When state rules are more generous than the federal limits

The figures in the tables above are federal limits. Medicare's page is explicit that a state's rules can be more generous: "You may still qualify for these programs in your state even if your income or resources are higher than the federal limits listed. Some states don't count certain types or specific amounts of income or resources when deciding who qualifies."

A shorter version, "You may qualify in other states for these programs even if your income or resources are higher than the federal limits listed," appears under each of the four program tables, followed by "Contact your state to find out." The statement has three practical consequences for someone weighing whether to apply.

A household over $9,950 or $14,910 is not necessarily out. The single retiree in the first example was $1,150 over the federal limit. In a state that does not count a certain type of resource, or counts only part of it, the result could be different. Medicare's page does not list each state's rules, and this article does not either. Contact your state Medicaid office to ask which resource exclusions apply.

The income test can also differ by state. The same sentence covers "certain types or specific amounts of income." A state may leave some income out of the count, which can move a household from one program tier to another.

The state decides. Medicare says that when you apply, "your state determines which program(s) you qualify for." Running the federal numbers is a good way to understand where you stand, but it is not a substitute for the state's decision.

Put together, these points support the instruction Medicare gives near the top of its page: even if you don't think you qualify, you should still apply. A household that assumes its savings disqualify it, and never applies, gives up the chance to have the state check that assumption against rules that may be more favorable than the federal ones.

## Extra Help: the higher $18,090 limit and the automatic link

Extra Help is Medicare's program for people with limited income and resources that lowers prescription drug costs. It is separate from the Medicare Savings Programs, with its own limits, but the two are connected.

The connection runs one way automatically. The Extra Help page lists three groups who get it without a separate application: people with full-benefit Medicaid coverage, people who get help from their state paying Part B premiums through a Medicare Savings Program, and people who get Supplemental Security Income payments from Social Security. The MSP page confirms it under each Part B program: QMB, SLMB and QI enrollees "also get Extra Help paying for your prescription drugs," and they pay no more than $12.65 in 2026 for each drug covered by their Medicare drug plan.

The Extra Help limits are higher than the MSP limits, especially on the resource side.

| 2026 limit | QMB, SLMB and QI | Extra Help |
|---|---|---|
| Resource limit, individual | $9,950 | $18,090 |
| Resource limit, married couple | $14,910 | $36,100 |
| Income limit, individual | $1,350 to $1,816 a month, by program | $23,940 |
| Income limit, married couple | $1,824 to $2,455 a month, by program | $32,460 |

One caution on the income rows: Medicare labels the MSP income limits as monthly, but its Extra Help table lists $23,940 and $32,460 with no monthly label, so those two figures should not be read against a monthly check the way the MSP limits are.

The resource gap is wide. An individual can have $18,090 − $9,950 = $8,140 more in counted resources under the Extra Help limit than under the federal MSP limit. For a married couple the difference is $36,100 − $14,910 = $21,190.

So a household over the MSP resource limit may still be under the Extra Help limit, as the single retiree in the first example was. And a household that qualifies for QMB, SLMB or QI doesn't need to clear the Extra Help test separately, because help from the state with Part B premiums brings Extra Help with it.

To put the $12.65 cap in concrete terms: a person with four drugs on their plan's covered list would pay no more than $12.65 for each, so at most 4 × $12.65 = $50.60 for one fill of all four, under the 2026 cap Medicare states for MSP enrollees.

The Extra Help page adds a few rules that matter to people whose income or resources change:

- Even if your income changes in the middle of the year, you keep getting Extra Help through Dec. 31.
- If you don't qualify now, you can reapply any time if your income and resources change.
- You can apply for Extra Help and Medicare Savings Programs at the same time. The page says Social Security will send information to your state to start an MSP application unless you tell it not to on the Extra Help application.
- You can contact your local State Health Insurance Assistance Program (SHIP) for free help applying.

The third point is useful for anyone unsure where to start. An Extra Help application can open the door to an MSP application as well, so one filing can also start the state's review of MSP eligibility.

## QI reapplication and the first-come rule

Of the three programs that pay Part B premiums, QI has the highest income limits, and Medicare's page attaches three conditions to it.

You must apply every year to stay in the QI program. If the annual application is missed, the QI help can stop.

States approve QI applications on a first-come, first-served basis, with priority given to people who got QI benefits the previous year. Medicare's page does not give a deadline or say how quickly a state's QI slots fill, so the practical takeaway is simply that timing can matter, and a current QI enrollee should not let the renewal slide.

QI is only available for people who don't qualify for any other Medicaid coverage or benefits. The page adds that such a person "may qualify for help from another Medicare Savings Program." In other words, the state may determine that you qualify for QMB or SLMB instead.

For a household whose monthly income is above the federal SLMB limit ($1,616 for an individual, $2,184 for a couple) but not above the QI limit ($1,816 or $2,455), and that meets the other conditions, those rules are the reason to keep track of the annual application. Under a first-come rule, the order in which applications reach the state can matter.

## Applying through your state around Open Enrollment

The calendar is a natural trigger for this review. The Medicare Open Enrollment Period runs Oct. 15 to Dec. 7. During it you can join, drop or switch Medicare Advantage plans, switch between Original Medicare and Medicare Advantage, and join, drop or switch a Medicare drug plan if you're in Original Medicare. Coverage changes take effect Jan. 1 of the next year, and the plan must get your request to join by Dec. 7. For 2026's window, that means changes made by Dec. 7, 2026, start Jan. 1, 2027.

Medicare Savings Programs are not part of that window. They are run by your state, and Medicare's instruction is simply "Contact your state to apply." Nothing on Medicare's MSP page ties the application to the Oct. 15 to Dec. 7 dates. Open Enrollment is a good reminder to check, not a deadline for the state program.

The two do interact. Medicare's enrollment page lists getting Medicaid, or getting Extra Help to pay drug costs, among the situations that can give you a Special Enrollment Period for joining or switching plans. And the Extra Help page says that if you qualify for Extra Help, Medicare will enroll you in a Medicare drug plan if you don't already have one, and you can pick a different plan if you want.

A practical sequence for someone reviewing costs this fall:

1. List every account and holding that Medicare's page counts: checking, savings, retirement accounts, stocks and bonds. Use current balances.
2. Add them up and compare the total with $9,950 for an individual or $14,910 for a married couple. Then compare it with the Extra Help limits of $18,090 and $36,100.
3. Note what you have that does not count: your home, one car, a burial plot, up to $1,500 set aside for burial, furniture and household items.
4. Look at monthly income against the QMB, SLMB and QI limits, keeping in mind the state decides what income counts.
5. Contact your state Medicaid office to apply, even if you are over the federal figures. Medicare says to apply even if you don't think you qualify.
6. If you are in QI now, put the annual reapplication on your calendar.
7. Use Open Enrollment for the separate job of comparing health and drug plans for Jan. 1.

Having statements for each counted account at hand makes step 1 faster and gives the state the same numbers you used. A local SHIP can help with the Extra Help application at no cost, according to Medicare's Extra Help page.

## Common questions

**Does money in a retirement account count toward the Medicare Savings Program resource limit?**

Medicare's list of counted resources includes "money in a checking, savings, or retirement account," so under the federal definition it does. That surprises many people who think of retirement money as unavailable until later. Medicare's page also says some states don't count certain types or specific amounts of resources. Ask your state Medicaid office how it treats your account, and apply to get a determination rather than assuming.

**Is my house or car counted against the $9,950 limit?**

No, not under the list Medicare publishes. States don't count your home or one car, and they also don't count a burial plot, up to $1,500 set aside for burial expenses, furniture, or other household and personal items. That means a homeowner can have significant property and still be under the federal resource limit if their counted assets are small. The counted side is money in checking, savings and retirement accounts, plus stocks and bonds.

**What if my savings are a little over $9,950 or $14,910?**

You may still qualify. Medicare says you may qualify in your state even if your income or resources are higher than the federal limits, because some states don't count certain types or amounts of resources. Medicare's advice is to apply even if you don't think you qualify. Separately, the Extra Help resource limits are higher, at $18,090 for an individual and $36,100 for a married couple, so help with drug costs may be within reach even if an MSP is not.

**Do I have to wait for Open Enrollment to apply for a Medicare Savings Program?**

No. Medicare Savings Programs are run by states, and Medicare's instruction is to contact your state to apply; its page does not limit the application to the Oct. 15 to Dec. 7 window. Open Enrollment is for joining, dropping or switching Medicare Advantage and drug plans. Extra Help can also be reapplied for any time your income and resources change.

**If I qualify for QMB, SLMB or QI, do I also need to apply for Extra Help?**

Medicare lists people who get help from their state paying Part B premiums through a Medicare Savings Program among those who get Extra Help automatically. The MSP page says QMB, SLMB and QI enrollees also get Extra Help and pay no more than $12.65 in 2026 for each drug covered by their Medicare drug plan. Medicare's Extra Help page says you'll get a notice in the mail about your Extra Help. If your costs at the pharmacy don't match, the same page says to contact your drug plan, which may ask for proof that you get Extra Help.

**Will the limits change for next year?**

Medicare's page says the MSP limits go up each year, and its Extra Help page says those limits may go up each year. The figures above are the 2026 limits Medicare publishes; the next year's numbers were not on the pages reviewed for this article. The Part B premium can also change each year. The $12.65 per-drug figure is a 2026 amount, and Medicare's Extra Help page already lists different per-prescription amounts for 2027. Check Medicare.gov and your state Medicaid office for the current figures before you apply.
