Your balance does not shrink when prices rise. The number on the statement stays exactly where it was, which is why this loss has to be calculated rather than observed: it shows up in what the money buys, not in what the statement says. An account paying less than the rate prices climb is losing ground every month, including the months the interest line reads positive.

This page hands you the arithmetic instead of the worry. You get the formula for real return, the FDIC national average deposit rates as a baseline to measure your own account against, a worked example carried to the cent, and a short list of the things you can actually change. It also marks the line where deposit insurance stops, because insurance and inflation are different risks and only one of them is covered.

There are no forecasts here. Where prices or rates go next is not something this page will pretend to know, so the example below uses a clearly labeled assumption that you can replace with the official published figure when you run the numbers on your own balance.

## Nominal yield vs. real return

The rate on your account is the nominal return. What that return is worth after prices move is the real return, and one line of arithmetic connects them:

**real return = (1 + nominal rate) ÷ (1 + inflation rate) − 1**

Subtracting one rate from the other gets you close at small numbers, but it is a different calculation, and the division is the one that gives the right figure.

Two details keep the comparison honest. APY already includes the effect of compounding while a stated interest rate does not, so compare APY against APY and never an APY against a bare rate. And the inflation number belongs to official published data: the Bureau of Labor Statistics publishes the Consumer Price Index on a monthly schedule, and that release is where the denominator should come from.

## The national average baseline

FDIC national average rates as of August 17, 2026 are below. These are averages across reporting institutions and product tiers — not offers, and not rates you can request by name. They are useful for one thing: telling you whether your own account sits near the middle or well under it. Your actual rate is on your statement or in your bank's rate disclosure.

The last column applies the real-return formula using an assumed 3.00% rise in prices over one year. That 3.00% is an assumption chosen to make the arithmetic legible. It is not a measurement and not a prediction; substitute the published CPI change when you do this for yourself.

| Account (national average, Aug 17, 2026) | APY | One year on $10,000 | Real return at 3.00% assumed inflation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Interest checking | 0.07% | $7.00 | −2.84% |
| Savings | 0.38% | $38.00 | −2.54% |
| Money market | 0.63% | $63.00 | −2.30% |
| 12 month CD | 1.71% | $171.00 | −1.25% |
| 60 month CD | 1.36% | $136.00 | −1.59% |

Interest column: balance × APY, one year, no deposits or withdrawals. Real return column: (1 + APY) ÷ 1.03 − 1, rounded to two decimals.

Every line is negative under that assumption. The spread between the worst and best rows — 1.59 percentage points of real return between interest checking and a 12 month CD — is the part of the outcome your account choice controls.

## A worked example on $20,000

Take $20,000 held for exactly one year, no deposits, no withdrawals, prices assumed to rise 3.00%.

**Left in savings at the national average of 0.38% APY:**

- Interest: $20,000.00 × 0.0038 = $76.00
- Ending balance: $20,000.00 + $76.00 = $20,076.00

**In a 12 month CD at the national average of 1.71% APY:**

- Interest: $20,000.00 × 0.0171 = $342.00
- Ending balance: $20,000.00 + $342.00 = $20,342.00

**What the same basket of goods costs a year later, under the 3.00% assumption:**

- $20,000.00 × 1.03 = $20,600.00

The shortfall, measured in end-of-year dollars:

- Savings: $20,600.00 − $20,076.00 = $524.00 short
- 12 month CD: $20,600.00 − $20,342.00 = $258.00 short

The gap between the two outcomes is $524.00 − $258.00 = $266.00, which matches the difference in interest earned: $342.00 − $76.00 = $266.00. Both accounts lose purchasing power under this assumption. One loses about half as much. That is the honest shape of the choice at these averages — a deposit account slows the erosion rather than reversing it.

## Levers you control

- **The rate you accept.** Compare the APY on your statement against the averages above. If yours is far below, the fix is a different account, and the only figures that count are the ones printed in the rate disclosure of the account you are considering.
- **Fee drag.** A maintenance fee is subtracted from the same balance interest is added to. On $10,000 at 0.38%, a year of interest is $38.00; a $5 monthly fee costs $5.00 × 12 = $60.00, leaving $38.00 − $60.00 = −$22.00 for the year before prices are considered at all. The fee schedule in your account agreement settles this.
- **Term structure.** A CD fixes its rate for the term. That protects the yield if rates fall and locks you out if they rise, so the term should match money you are certain you will not need.
- **Taxes.** Interest is ordinary income, reported to you and the IRS on Form 1099-INT. The after-tax yield is what belongs in the formula: 0.38% at a 22% marginal rate leaves 0.38 × 0.78 = 0.2964%, and that smaller number is what goes in the numerator.
- **Placement.** Money you will spend within weeks does not belong in a term product. Money you will not touch for a year gives up the CD-versus-savings spread every month it sits in checking.

## Coverage limits and what insurance is for

FDIC deposit insurance covers $250,000 per depositor, per insured bank, per ownership category. Credit union accounts are insured by the NCUA at the same standard limit. Ownership categories — single, joint, certain retirement accounts, revocable trusts — are insured separately, so one person can be covered above $250,000 at a single bank across different categories. Trust account rules were revised effective April 1, 2024; if a trust holds your deposits, run the numbers through the FDIC's EDIE estimator rather than any formula you remember.

Insurance answers one question: what happens if the bank fails. Deposits are covered, investments are not. It does not answer the question this page is about, because no amount of coverage protects the purchasing power of the covered dollars. Both statements hold at once. The balance is safe, and under the arithmetic above it is shrinking in real terms.

## Term length and the shape of the averages

The averages as of August 17, 2026 do not rise steadily with term. The 12 month average is 1.71%, the 24 month average is 1.57%, and the 36 month average of 1.34% sits below the 6 month average of 1.41%. Longer does not automatically pay more, which means a long CD chosen for yield alone can hand you a lower rate and less access at the same time. Read the term-by-term rate sheet before assuming the five year line is the best one.

Deposit pricing responds to the Federal Reserve's policy rate and to competition among institutions for deposits. Those are mechanisms, not a schedule, and nobody can tell you what the next rate sheet says. What you can check today: your current APY, the early-withdrawal penalty stated in any CD disclosure you hold (commonly quoted as a number of months of interest), and whether the term you are considering matches money you can leave alone.

## A short checklist

1. Find your current APY on your statement or rate disclosure. Compare it to the savings average of 0.38% and the money market average of 0.63% as of August 17, 2026.
2. Run the real-return formula with the latest published CPI change in place of the 3.00% used here.
3. Check the fee schedule. A maintenance fee larger than a year of interest makes the rate question moot.
4. For money with a known, distant spending date, price a CD term against the savings rate, and read the early-withdrawal penalty before committing.
5. Confirm your balances sit inside the insured limits for the ownership categories you use.

None of this stops prices from rising. It does put a number on the gap, and a number is what makes the account choice decidable.
