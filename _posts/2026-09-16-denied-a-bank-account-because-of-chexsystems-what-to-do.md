![Denied a Bank Account Because of ChexSystems: What to Do](/assets/pin-images/2026-09-16-denied-a-bank-account-because-of-chexsystems-what-to-do.png)

The application took about four minutes: name, address, Social Security number, a checking account to link for the first deposit. The screen that came back showed no account number. It said the account could not be opened, and a notice followed that pointed to information from a consumer reporting company and listed that company's name, phone number, and mailing address.

The applicant's credit score is fine, which is what makes this confusing. The problem sits in a different file, one built from deposit account history, and the entry behind it may trace back to an old checking account that closed with a negative balance.

Here is what can be done, in order. Find the screening company named in the denial notice, because the bank has to name it. Request the free copy of that report the denial entitles you to, and use your annual free disclosure to check the other companies. Compare every line against your own records, and dispute anything wrong in writing, both with the screening company and with the bank or credit union that supplied it. If the entry is accurate, the realistic routes are settling what the old institution says is owed, applying somewhere with a different screening policy, or starting with a lower-risk account. None of those steps requires paying anyone.

## Screening companies and what banks send them

The [CFPB](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/)'s answer to [why people are denied checking accounts](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/why-was-i-denied-a-checking-account-en-1113/) starts with the file rather than the applicant: "You may have been unable to open a checking account because a checking account reporting company has negative information in its files about your checking history." Then it names the supply chain: "Banks and credit unions supply this type of information to checking account reporting companies, such as Chex Systems and Early Warning Services."

A savings application runs through the same pipe. An [archived 2017 CFPB blog post](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/archive/blog/denied-bank-account-heres-what-you-should-know/) said so directly: "When you go to apply for a new checking or savings account, the bank or credit union will usually request a checking account report." The CFPB's [2025 List of Consumer Reporting Companies](https://files.consumerfinance.gov/f/documents/cfpb_consumer-reporting-companies_list_2025.pdf), current as of January 2025, says institutions use deposit account and payments screening "to help decide whether to offer you a checking or savings account."

What lands in the file is narrower than people fear. The CFPB's [consumer guide to checking account denials](https://files.consumerfinance.gov/f/documents/cfpb_adult-fin-ed_consumer-guide-to-being-denied-a-checking-account.pdf) says you may have a negative rating if you had a checking account before and you:

- "Have an unpaid negative balance on that account, such as from an overdraft, that you have not repaid and the account was closed by the bank or credit union (this is called an 'involuntary closure')."
- "Were suspected of fraud related to a checking account."
- "Had a joint account with someone else who had these types of problems."

The third item blindsides people. A record can follow someone who never overdrew anything, because a former co-owner did.

The guide also describes the company that ends up holding the entry: "a checking account reporting company, a type of consumer reporting agency." That phrase carries the legal weight. The CFPB's page on [disputing a checking account report](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/how-do-i-dispute-an-error-on-my-checking-account-consumer-report-en-2029/) says "Checking account reporting companies must comply with the federal Fair Credit Reporting Act (FCRA)," and the CFPB's [model summary of FCRA rights](https://files.consumerfinance.gov/f/documents/bcfp_consumer-rights-summary_2018-09.pdf) lists "specialty agencies (such as agencies that sell information about check writing histories, medical records, and rental history records)" among consumer reporting agencies. The notice, the free copies, and the dispute duties below all follow from that.

It also explains the good credit score. The CFPB's page on [getting a copy of a checking account report](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/how-do-i-get-a-copy-of-my-checking-account-consumer-report-en-2035/) says "It's possible to have a great credit score and a not-so-good checking account report."

The 2025 list names six companies in its deposit account and payments screening section: Certegy Payment Solutions, Chex Systems, CrossCheck, Early Warning Services, Global Payments Check Services, and TeleCheck Services.

## The adverse action notice

The CFPB states the requirement in one sentence: "The bank must provide you with an 'adverse action' notice that includes the name and contact information of the checking account reporting agency from which the bank got the report." The model FCRA summary says anyone who uses a consumer report to deny an application "or to take another adverse action against you – must tell you, and must give you the name, address, and phone number of the agency that provided the information."

The trigger is broad. The CFPB denial guide: "If you are denied an account based in any part on information from a checking account reporting company, the bank or credit union must give you a notice that includes the name of that company that provided the negative information." Based in any part. The report does not have to be the only reason.

Why it matters: six companies sit on the CFPB's list, and a bank may use only one. Request a report from the wrong company and it may come back clean without telling you anything. The notice is the only document that identifies which file was pulled. That is why the guide's first step is to "Make sure that the bank or credit union provides you with the information about the checking account reporting company that provided the negative account history that was used to deny you an account." If the decline arrived only as a website message naming no company, ask the bank in writing for the notice before doing anything else.

Remember that the company supplies information and the bank applies its own rules to it. The guide says "Each bank or credit union has its own policies about the way the information in your banking history report impacts your ability to open an account." Banks layering house rules over the legal floor is a familiar pattern, the same reason [a savings withdrawal limit can survive after the Fed repealed it](/2026/09/10/the-savings-withdrawal-limit-was-repealed-your-bank-kept-it-anyway/).

## Two free copies, two different rights

A denial opens one right to a free report. A separate annual right exists whether or not anyone has turned you down.

**The copy tied to the denial.** The 2017 CFPB post: "You also have the right to request a free report if you have received an 'adverse action' notice." The model FCRA summary lists free file disclosures when "a person has taken adverse action against you because of information in your credit report," and when "your file contains inaccurate information as a result of fraud." Ask the company named in the notice.

**The annual file disclosure.** This comes from Regulation V, the CFPB's rule implementing the FCRA. [12 CFR 1022.130(a)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-12/section-1022.130) defines it: "Annual file disclosure means a file disclosure that is provided to a consumer, upon consumer request and without charge, once in any twelve month period, in compliance with section 612(a) of the FCRA, 15 U.S.C. 1681j(a)."

The duty to make that easy falls on a defined group. Section 1022.130(i) gives a nationwide specialty consumer reporting agency "the meaning provided in section 603(w) of the FCRA," and [12 CFR 1022.137(a)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-12/section-1022.137) sets the requirement: "Any nationwide specialty consumer reporting agency shall have a streamlined process for accepting and processing consumer requests for annual file disclosures."

The practical detail: the process must "Enable consumers to request annual file disclosures by a toll-free telephone number," and that number must be "clearly and prominently posted on any Web site owned or maintained by the nationwide specialty consumer reporting agency that is related to consumer reporting."

The CFPB's consumer pages put it plainly: "Nationwide checking account reporting companies must provide you with one free annual report per year, upon request." And: "You have to request the reports individually from each reporting company." The 2025 list's Chex Systems entry says it "will provide one free report every 12 months if you request it," and that "A free score will be provided upon request too."

The working plan: use the denial right with the company named in the notice, and the annual right with the other screening companies. If one institution reported a closure, the question to settle before the next application is whether the same entry sits anywhere else.

## Reading the report line by line

The CFPB guide says the report "should indicate which banks or credit unions have previously provided information to the checking account reporting companies." Every line has two parties behind it: the company holding the file and the institution that furnished the entry. If a score comes with the report, the entries underneath it are what can be checked and disputed.

Work through each entry with whatever records exist: old statements, closing letters, emails, a payoff receipt. The fields Regulation V lets you dispute directly with a furnisher make a good checklist. [12 CFR 1022.43(a)(3)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-12/section-1022.43) covers "the current payment status, high balance, date a payment was made, the amount of a payment made, or the date an account was opened or closed." Section 1022.43(a)(1) covers disputes over "whether there is or has been identity theft or fraud against the consumer, whether there is individual or joint liability on an account."

Four hypothetical entries, with illustrative dollar figures:

| What the report shows | What your records show | What it points to |
|---|---|---|
| Involuntary closure, $400 owed, at a bank you never used | No account there, ever | Possible identity theft; dispute with the company, check credit reports |
| Involuntary closure, $400 owed, your old account | Final statement shows $400 negative | Accurate as reported; see the section on accurate entries |
| Involuntary closure, $400 owed, your old account | Payoff receipt for $400 | Payment status is wrong; dispute with the bank, attach the receipt |
| Suspected fraud on a joint account | The activity was the co-owner's | Liability is a disputable field; explain and document |

For the first row, the CFPB guide suggests checking your credit reports as well, "especially if it appears that the inaccurate information is the result of identity theft."

## Disputing an entry in writing

The CFPB is specific about the channel: "If you find an error in your checking account report, contact the company to dispute it in writing." And about the targets: "To dispute inaccurate information on your checking account consumer report, you should file a dispute with the checking account reporting company that compiled the report. You should also file a dispute with the bank, credit union, or company that provided the information to the checking account reporting company."

Both parties carry duties. The CFPB's denial page says "All checking account reporting companies must investigate consumers' disputes of inaccurate information on their reports and correct any inaccurate information." And per the CFPB guide, "Banks and credit unions that report information to checking account reporting companies also have an obligation to investigate and correct disputed information."

The furnisher's duty has a written rulebook that doubles as a letter template. Under 12 CFR 1022.43(d), "A dispute notice must include":

1. "Sufficient information to identify the account or other relationship that is in dispute, such as an account number and the name, address, and telephone number of the consumer, if applicable";
2. "The specific information that the consumer is disputing and an explanation of the basis for the dispute"; and
3. "All supporting documentation or other information reasonably required by the furnisher to substantiate the basis of the dispute."

Examples listed in the rule: "a copy of the relevant portion of the consumer report that contains the allegedly inaccurate information; a police report; a fraud or identity theft affidavit; a court order; or account statements." The same contents make a sound letter to the screening company, and the CFPB's dispute page links to sample letters. Send it to the dispute address shown on the report or specified by the institution; 1022.43(c) ties the duty to investigate to where the notice is sent.

Once a proper notice arrives, 1022.43(e) requires the furnisher to "Conduct a reasonable investigation," review what you sent, and, if the information was inaccurate, "promptly notify each consumer reporting agency to which the furnisher provided inaccurate information." That is why the bank dispute matters: a correction at the source reaches every company the bank reported to.

Three limits in the same section:

- **Another furnisher's entry.** The duty does not cover "Information provided to a consumer reporting agency by another furnisher." Dispute a closure with the bank that reported it.
- **Repeat disputes.** A dispute can be treated as frivolous if it "is substantially the same as a dispute previously submitted," unless it includes information "that had not previously been provided to the furnisher." A second letter needs something new.
- **Credit repair letters.** A furnisher need not investigate a dispute it reasonably believes was prepared by a credit repair organization or submitted "on a form supplied to the consumer by" one. A letter in your own words, with your own documents, avoids that exception.

Keep originals, send copies, and log what went where and when.

## When the entry is accurate

A dispute is for errors. The model FCRA summary is blunt: "a consumer reporting agency may continue to report information it has verified as accurate."

If the record is a real involuntary closure with a real unpaid balance, the leverage shifts to the next institution's policy. The CFPB denial guide says "Some banks and credit unions require you to pay these old charges and fees before you are allowed to open a new account."

Paying the old balance can do two things. It satisfies that requirement where it exists. And if the report still shows the balance as owed afterward, that is a payment status question, one of the fields 1022.43(a)(3) lets you dispute with the furnisher, receipt attached.

It cannot make the closure itself inaccurate, and nothing in the CFPB or CFR text quoted here requires an accurate entry to be deleted because the balance was paid. The CFPB guide says the closure information can stay on the report "even if, in the case of overdraft, you later pay back the overdrawn balance."

Before paying, ask the original institution in writing for the amount it says is owed and how it will report the account once paid, and keep the receipt.

## Other routes while the record stands

**A different institution.** The CFPB guide: "Financial institutions policies about how and when they use prior negative account history vary widely. So if you get turned away at one place, try another."

**A lower-risk account first.** The guide continues: "Because these products are considered less risky, many financial institutions may be less reliant on checking account reports when making a decision about a potential customer. As a result, you may be able to qualify for one of these products even if you were denied for another product recently." The CFPB's [guide to selecting a lower-risk account](https://files.consumerfinance.gov/f/documents/201602_cfpb_consumer-guide-to-selecting-a-lower-risk-account.pdf) gives examples, "such as a checkless checking account or a prepaid account," warns of "limited features, such as no check-writing and no online bill payment capabilities," and notes that "Some banks may not offer these lower-risk products, or may not market them prominently." Ask for one by description.

For someone whose goal is a high-yield savings account, a lower-risk checking account can become the linked funding account most online savings products ask for, which turns reapplying into a second step rather than a dead end.

**Credit unions.** Every CFPB passage quoted here says "banks and credit unions." A credit union is another institution with its own policy, not a way around screening, and its deposits carry [NCUA](https://ncua.gov/consumers/share-insurance-coverage) share insurance rather than [FDIC](https://www.fdic.gov/resources/deposit-insurance) insurance; the [differences are covered here](/2026/08/31/credit-union-share-insurance-how-ncua-coverage-compares/).

**A prepaid card, with a caution.** The CFPB's denial page says "You might also consider getting a prepaid card," and the lower-risk guide notes that "Prepaid accounts are available through a number of non-bank providers as well." That handles spending. Before parking savings with any app or non-bank provider, confirm where the money sits; [a partner bank arrangement is not the same as the app being insured](/2026/09/08/when-fdic-insured-means-a-partner-bank-not-your-app/), and any bank can be [checked against FDIC records before a transfer](/2026/09/09/how-to-verify-a-bank-is-actually-fdic-insured-before-you-transfer/).

Waiting has a price. Hypothetically, $10,000 earning 4% produces about $400 a year, roughly $33 a month; in an account paying no interest, the same money earns nothing, so every month the record sits unchallenged has a cost.

**A complaint.** The third tip in the CFPB's 2017 post was titled "If you have a problem, you can submit a complaint."

## From the denial letter to reapplying

| Step | What to do | What it rests on |
|---|---|---|
| Day of the decline | Find the company named in the notice; if none, ask the bank in writing | CFPB: notice includes the reporting agency's name and contact information |
| Right after | Request the free copy from that company | CFPB: free report after an adverse action notice |
| Same week | Request annual disclosures from the other screening companies, one by one | 12 CFR 1022.130(a) and 1022.137 |
| When reports arrive | Match every line to statements, letters, receipts | CFPB guide: report shows which institutions furnished data |
| If a line is wrong | Written dispute to the company and the furnishing institution, with documents | CFPB dispute page; 12 CFR 1022.43(d) and (e) |
| If a line is accurate | Get the amount owed in writing, pay if it makes sense, keep the receipt | CFPB guide: some institutions require old charges paid |
| Meanwhile | Open a lower-risk account or apply where the policy differs | CFPB lower-risk account guide |

The rules around this record are written almost entirely as duties on other parties: the bank names the source, the company hands over the report, both investigate a dispute and correct what is wrong. The applicant's part is making each of them do it, in writing, with the documents attached.
