The $250,000 figure gets repeated so often that most people treat it as a hard ceiling on what any one bank can safely hold for them. It isn't. The full sentence matters: [FDIC](https://www.fdic.gov/national-rates-and-rate-caps) coverage is $250,000 per depositor, per insured bank, per ownership category. That last clause is the part doing the real work, and it is the part almost nobody reads closely.

Ownership categories are not a loophole. They are the FDIC's method of answering a specific question during a bank failure: who owned this money, and who was supposed to benefit from it? Accounts that answer that question differently get counted separately. A household that understands the categories can hold well past $250,000 at a single institution with every dollar insured, without opening accounts at five different banks or chasing paperwork tricks.

Below: how the four categories most households use are calculated, a worked example with the arithmetic spelled out, the moves that feel like they should add coverage but don't, and how to verify your own numbers in the FDIC's own tools.

## Coverage Per Category, Not Per Account

Within a single ownership category at a single bank, every deposit you hold gets added together and measured against one $250,000 limit. Product type is irrelevant to that math. A savings account, a money market account, and three CDs held in your name alone are not five separate limits — they are one balance, tested once.

Move to a different ownership category and the counter resets. That is the entire mechanism. Coverage includes principal plus interest accrued through the date a bank fails, so a balance sitting just under the line can drift over it as interest posts.

Deposits are what's covered. Mutual funds, stocks, bonds, annuities, and crypto bought through a bank or its affiliated brokerage carry no FDIC insurance regardless of where you purchased them. Credit unions are outside the FDIC system entirely; they are insured by the NCUA, which applies a parallel $250,000 structure through a different fund and a different agency.

The FDIC recognizes several ownership categories, including ones for businesses, government entities, and employee benefit plans. Four cover almost every household situation.

## The Four Categories a Household Actually Uses

### Accounts in one name

Any deposit you own alone, with no co-owner and no beneficiary named, sits in the single ownership category. Everything you hold this way at one bank combines into a single $250,000 limit. Two people in the same household each get their own single-account limit at the same bank, because the category is per depositor.

### Joint accounts

A joint account gets its own limit, separate from either owner's single accounts. Each co-owner is insured up to $250,000 for their share of all joint accounts at that bank. Two owners with equal rights therefore support up to $500,000 in the joint category.

The conditions are strict and worth checking. Every co-owner must be a living person — not a business, not a trust. Each must have equal withdrawal rights. The bank's records must actually show the joint ownership. Absent a written statement otherwise, the FDIC presumes shares are equal, so a $500,000 joint balance splits $250,000 / $250,000 regardless of who deposited what.

### Trust accounts

This is the category that changed most recently, and old guidance still circulates. Since the FDIC's trust rule took effect on April 1, 2024, revocable trust deposits (payable-on-death accounts, in-trust-for accounts, and living trust accounts) and irrevocable trust deposits are combined into one trust accounts category.

The calculation is now simple: $250,000 per owner, per eligible beneficiary, capped at five beneficiaries. One owner naming three children is covered up to $750,000. Two owners naming those same three children are covered up to $1,500,000. The maximum any single owner can reach in this category at one bank is $1,250,000, and naming a sixth beneficiary adds nothing. Eligible beneficiaries are living people, charities, and nonprofit organizations.

### Certain retirement accounts

Traditional IRAs, Roth IRAs, SEP and SIMPLE IRAs, and self-directed plan deposits combine into one $250,000 limit per owner at each bank — separate from that person's single accounts. Coverage applies to deposit products inside the account, such as an IRA CD or IRA savings account, not to securities in a brokerage IRA. Workplace plans where you don't direct the investments fall into a different category.

| Category | Who qualifies | How coverage is calculated |
|---|---|---|
| Single | One owner, no co-owner, no beneficiary | $250,000 per owner, all such deposits combined |
| Joint | Two or more living people, equal withdrawal rights | $250,000 per co-owner, shares presumed equal |
| Trust | POD, ITF, living trust, or irrevocable trust deposits | $250,000 per owner per beneficiary, five beneficiaries maximum |
| Certain retirement | IRAs and self-directed plan deposits | $250,000 per owner, all such deposits combined |

## A Worked Example: $865,000 at One Bank

Priya and Marcus hold everything at one FDIC-insured bank:

- Priya, savings in her name only: $180,000
- Marcus, savings in his name only: $95,000
- Marcus, a 12-month CD in his name only: $60,000
- Joint money market account: $400,000
- Priya's IRA CD: $130,000

Now sort by category rather than by account.

**Single accounts.** Priya's $180,000 is tested against her own $250,000 limit — insured, with $70,000 of room left. Marcus's savings and CD combine: $95,000 + $60,000 = $155,000 against his separate $250,000 limit. Insured, $95,000 of room. Note what happened there: two different products, one limit, because the ownership category is identical.

**Joint account.** $400,000 with two equal owners splits into $200,000 per owner. Each share is under $250,000, so the account is fully insured, with $100,000 of unused capacity.

**Retirement.** Priya's $130,000 IRA CD is tested on its own. It does not touch her $180,000 single-account balance, so she is well inside the limit.

All $865,000 is insured. Their unused capacity at this one bank adds up to $70,000 + $95,000 + $100,000 + $120,000 = $385,000, meaning they could hold $1,250,000 here — $250,000 + $250,000 + $500,000 + $250,000 — before a single dollar became uninsured, with no trust paperwork at all.

Now break it. Suppose Marcus closes the joint account and moves the $400,000 into his own name for convenience. His single category becomes $95,000 + $60,000 + $400,000 = $555,000. Insured: $250,000. Uninsured: $305,000. Same household, same bank, same total money, one signature line different.

Going the other direction: if the couple names their two children as payable-on-death beneficiaries on that joint money market account, the account leaves the joint category and enters the trust category. Coverage becomes 2 owners × 2 beneficiaries × $250,000 = $1,000,000 for that one account. Worth understanding clearly — the account moved, it did not multiply. The joint category no longer covers it at all.

## What Does Not Expand Coverage

- **Opening more accounts in the same category.** Four savings accounts in your sole name share one $250,000 limit.
- **Mixing product types.** A CD, an MMA, and a savings account in the same category still aggregate.
- **Using different branches.** One charter, one set of limits, no matter how many buildings.
- **Using different brand names of the same bank.** Several online brands can operate under one FDIC certificate. Their deposits combine.
- **Adding a beneficiary to a joint account and expecting both categories.** The account changes category; it does not occupy two.
- **Naming the same beneficiary on multiple accounts.** Trust coverage counts unique beneficiaries per owner across all trust deposits at the bank, not per account.
- **Naming an ineligible beneficiary.** A pet, or an entity that is not a person, charity, or nonprofit, does not create coverage.
- **Naming more than five beneficiaries.** The sixth and beyond add nothing.

## Beneficiary Mistakes That Shrink Coverage

The most expensive errors are quiet ones. A beneficiary designation made verbally and never entered into the bank's records does not exist for insurance purposes — the FDIC works from the deposit account records. A beneficiary who has died and was never replaced reduces the count. A trust amended by an attorney but never reported to the bank leaves those records stale.

There is also a timing rule worth knowing. When two insured banks merge, deposits you held at the acquired bank stay separately insured for six months after the merger, and CDs stay separately insured until they mature after that window. If you deliberately split money across two banks and those banks combine, that grace period is your window to rebalance, not a permanent state.

## Verifying With BankFind and EDIE

Two free FDIC tools settle these questions without a phone call.

**[BankFind Suite](https://banks.data.fdic.gov/bankfind-suite/bankfind)** (banks.data.fdic.gov/bankfind-suite) confirms an institution is FDIC-insured and shows its certificate number along with the trade names operating under that charter. Run this before assuming two brands are two banks — if they share a certificate, you have one limit, not two.

**EDIE**, the Electronic Deposit Insurance Estimator (edie.fdic.gov), takes your actual account list — ownership, balances, beneficiaries — and returns the per-category result with any uninsured amount identified. It produces a printable report, and it is worth redoing after any change in ownership, beneficiaries, or a large deposit. Credit union members need the NCUA's own estimator instead.

## Yield and Coverage as Separate Questions

Structure decides whether the money is protected. Rates decide what it earns. Answer them independently.

As of August 17, 2026, the FDIC national average was 0.38% APY for savings, 0.63% APY for money market accounts, and 1.71% APY for 12-month CDs, with 6-month CDs at 1.41% APY and 60-month CDs at 1.36% APY. These are national averages across thousands of institutions, and online banks commonly pay well above them. Deposit rates move with [Federal Reserve](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy.htm) policy and with how hard banks are competing for balances — no one can tell you where they go next.

Two practical notes. CDs generally charge an early-withdrawal penalty, so a CD used to fill coverage capacity should hold money you won't need before maturity. And compare APY to APY, since APY reflects compounding and a stated interest rate does not.

## A Coverage Check You Can Finish Today

List every deposit account you hold, grouped by bank. Under each bank, sort the accounts by ownership category rather than by product. Add the balances within each category and compare to $250,000, remembering that posted interest pushes the total up over time. Run BankFind to confirm which brands share a charter. Run EDIE on anything close to a limit.

For most households the fix is not moving to another bank. It is a beneficiary form that was never filed, a joint account that quietly became a single one, or a second brand that turned out to be the same charter — small corrections that change the insured number substantially.
