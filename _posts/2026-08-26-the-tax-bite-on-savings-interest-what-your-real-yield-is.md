The APY printed on a savings account is a pre-tax number. Interest from a bank account is ordinary income, taxed at the same rate as the last dollar of your paycheck, and that gap between the advertised rate and what you keep is wider than most people assume when they compare accounts.

None of this makes a high-yield account a bad place for cash. It changes which comparisons are meaningful. Two accounts paying the same APY can leave you with different amounts, and a Treasury paying slightly less than a bank account can leave you with more.

## What the bank reports, and when

Banks send a Form 1099-INT when an account pays $10 or more of interest during the year. That threshold is a reporting rule for the bank, not a taxability rule for you. Interest below $10 is still income, and it still belongs on the return even though no form arrives to remind you.

A few details on the form matter more than the headline number:

- **Box 1** is ordinary interest from the account.
- **Box 2** is the early withdrawal penalty, if you broke a CD during the year.
- **Box 3** is interest on U.S. Savings Bonds and Treasury obligations, which is treated differently by your state.
- **Box 4** is federal income tax already withheld, which is usually zero unless backup withholding was triggered.

The penalty in Box 2 is worth noticing. It is an adjustment to income rather than an itemized deduction, so it reduces taxable income even for filers taking the standard deduction. If you ran the break-even on cashing a CD early and decided the math worked, the after-tax version of that math is slightly friendlier than the pre-tax version.

## After-tax yield, worked

The arithmetic is short. For a taxable account:

After-tax yield = APY × (1 − your marginal rate)

At a 4% APY, that produces meaningfully different outcomes depending on the bracket:

| Marginal rate | Kept on $25,000 at 4% | Effective yield |
|---|---|---|
| 12% | $880 | about 3.5% |
| 22% | $780 | about 3.1% |
| 24% | $760 | about 3% |
| 32% | $680 | under 2.8% |

State income tax stacks on top of that where it applies, which is why the same account is worth noticeably more to a saver in a state with no income tax than to one paying nine percent at the state level.

Two more layers apply to higher incomes. The net investment income tax adds 3.8% on investment income above $200,000 of modified adjusted gross income for single filers and $250,000 for joint filers. Interest also feeds adjusted gross income, which is the input for a long list of phase-outs, so a large interest year can quietly raise the cost of unrelated parts of the return.

## Where the money sits changes the answer

Not all interest is taxed the same way, and the difference is usually about jurisdiction rather than rate.

**Bank interest** — savings accounts, [money market account](/2026/08/22/money-market-account-vs-money-market-fund-whats-insured/)s, CDs — is fully taxable at both the federal and state level.

**[U.S. Treasury](https://www.treasurydirect.gov/) interest** — T-bills, notes, and the Treasury holdings inside a government money market fund — is taxable federally but exempt from state and local income tax. In a high-tax state, that exemption can be worth more than a modest APY advantage at a bank. Comparing a bank APY against a Treasury yield without adjusting for it compares two numbers that are not measuring the same thing.

**Tax-advantaged accounts** — an IRA, a 401(k), an HSA — change the timing or remove the tax entirely, depending on the account type. Cash held inside them does not generate a 1099-INT.

The practical version: when a bank account and a Treasury are within a few tenths of a percent of each other and you live somewhere with meaningful state income tax, the ranking may reverse once state tax is applied.

## Backup withholding

If a bank does not have a certified taxpayer identification number for the account, or the IRS notifies the bank that the number on file does not match its records, the bank must withhold 24% of the interest and send it to the IRS. This shows up in Box 4 and reduces what lands in the account.

It is recoverable — withheld tax is credited against the year's liability when you file — but it is an interest-free loan to the government in the meantime. The fix is administrative: return the W-9 the bank asks for, and correct the name or number on file if the IRS flagged a mismatch. This tends to surface on accounts opened quickly online, on accounts inherited or retitled, and after a legal name change.

## Timing on multi-year CDs

A common surprise involves CDs longer than a year that pay all their interest at maturity. Interest generally becomes taxable in the year it is credited to the account, not the year you can finally touch it. A three-year CD crediting interest annually produces a 1099-INT each year, even though no money has been withdrawn.

The practical effect is a tax bill in years when the CD produced no cash. For a large enough position, that is worth planning around before the money is locked up rather than after.

## The withholding gap

Wages arrive with tax already taken out. Interest does not. A year in which cash balances grew and rates were high can therefore produce a liability with nothing set aside against it, and if the shortfall is large enough the IRS adds an underpayment penalty on top.

The usual protection is a safe harbor: paying in, through withholding or estimated payments, at least 90% of the current year's tax or 100% of the prior year's tax, with that second figure rising to 110% for filers whose adjusted gross income exceeded $150,000. Raising payroll withholding is often simpler than filing quarterly estimates, since withholding is treated as paid evenly across the year regardless of when it actually happened.

## What to do with the numbers

- Compare accounts on after-tax yield, not on APY, whenever the accounts are taxed differently.
- Apply the state-tax adjustment before ranking a bank account against a Treasury.
- Check Box 2 in a year you broke a CD, and make sure the penalty is claimed.
- Return the W-9 promptly on a new account, and treat any withholding in Box 4 as a paperwork problem to solve rather than a cost to accept.
- Report interest even when no form arrives.
- Check the safe harbor after a year of unusually large interest, before the return is due.

The comparison most people run — this bank pays more than that bank — is the right shape. It just needs the tax adjustment applied to both sides before the winner is declared.

This is general information, not tax advice for your situation. Brackets, thresholds, and state rules change, and a tax professional can confirm how they apply to you.
