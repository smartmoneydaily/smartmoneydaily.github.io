The certificate matures on a Thursday. The envelope arrived three weeks earlier and ran to a single page: the maturity date, a line saying the account renews for another twelve months, and, in the space where the new rate should have been, a sentence explaining that the rate has not been set yet, followed by a date and a telephone number. A new twelve-month CD at the same bank, tagged for new deposits, can advertise a rate well above what the renewal will pay.

No rule is being broken and nothing is hidden. Federal deposit rules decide what a bank has to tell a depositor and when it has to say it. They do not decide what it pays. Regulation DD states its own ambition at [12 CFR 1030.1(b)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-12/section-1030.1): "The purpose of this part is to enable consumers to make informed decisions about accounts at depository institutions. This part requires depository institutions to provide disclosures so that consumers can make meaningful comparisons among depository institutions." Comparison is the whole of the remedy. Nothing in the rule requires the renewal rate to match the rate the same bank is advertising further down the page.

The [Consumer Financial Protection Bureau](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/) says as much in plainer words on its consumer page about [CD rollovers](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/ask-cfpb/what-is-a-certificate-of-deposit-cd-rollover-or-renewal-en-923/): "The interest rate for the new CD is not guaranteed to be the same as the interest rate for your current CD." Higher or lower, and the depositor finds out by asking.

What the rules do supply is a deadline and a door. The deadline is a pre-maturity notice with a fixed mailing date. The door is the grace period, defined at [12 CFR 1030.2(m)](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1030/2/) as "a period following the maturity of an automatically renewing time account during which the consumer may withdraw funds without being assessed a penalty." Whether that door exists at all, and how wide it opens, is the bank's choice, subject to disclosure. The bank need not pay interest for the days the money sits inside it. Once it shuts, the deposit is committed to a term it was never priced for, with an early withdrawal penalty as the only exit.

## The notice that has to arrive before maturity

The timing rule is in [12 CFR 1030.5(b)](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1030/5/), and it covers only part of the CD universe: "For time accounts with a maturity longer than one month that renew automatically at maturity, institutions shall provide the disclosures described below before maturity." Two mailing deadlines follow, and the bank picks one.

The default runs from maturity. "The disclosures shall be mailed or delivered at least 30 calendar days before maturity of the existing account." The alternative is tied to the grace period: "the disclosures may be mailed or delivered at least 20 calendar days before the end of the grace period on the existing account, provided a grace period of at least five calendar days is allowed."

The second option trades a shorter warning for a longer window. Its clock runs backward from the end of the grace period rather than from maturity, so a longer grace period buys the bank a later mailing. The [official interpretations](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1030/Interp-5/) work one case: "Under the alternative timing rule, an institution offering a 10-day grace period would have to provide the disclosures at least 10 days prior to the scheduled maturity date." The same subtraction fills in the rest:

| Grace period offered | Notice due under the alternative rule |
|---|---|
| 5 calendar days | 15 days before maturity |
| 10 calendar days | 10 days before maturity |
| 15 calendar days | 5 days before maturity |
| 20 calendar days | the maturity date itself |

A depositor who assumes the envelope always comes a month early is assuming the default. Under the alternative, notice a week before maturity is compliant, and behind a long grace period it can land on the day the CD comes due.

What the notice must contain splits by term. For maturities longer than one year, 1030.5(b)(1) requires "account disclosures set forth in § 1030.4(b) of this part for the new account, along with the date the existing account matures," which means the whole opening package: rate, compounding, fees, the early withdrawal penalty, the renewal policy. At one year or less but longer than one month, 1030.5(b)(2) permits a far shorter notice instead, limited to three items. "The date the existing account matures and the new maturity date if the account is renewed." The rate and annual percentage yield "if they are known." And "any difference in the terms of the new account as compared to the terms required to be disclosed under § 1030.4(b) of this part for the existing account."

Any certificate of twelve months or less sits in that bracket, so the renewal notice on a one-year CD can run to three lines. It is not required to restate the penalty, the compounding method, or anything else that has not changed.

Delivery is governed by [12 CFR 1030.3(a)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-12/section-1030.3), which allows electronic form "subject to compliance with the consumer consent and other applicable provisions of the Electronic Signatures in Global and National Commerce Act." For an account opened online, the notice that starts the clock may be a message in a portal, and it satisfies the rule whether or not anyone signs in to read it.

One category gets no notice at all. Section 1030.5(c) covers non-renewing accounts, but only long ones: "For time accounts with a maturity longer than one year that do not renew automatically at maturity, institutions shall disclose to consumers the maturity date and whether interest will be paid after maturity," at least ten calendar days ahead. A six-month CD that does not renew falls outside both 1030.5(b) and 1030.5(c). Nothing has to be mailed, and the commentary on 1030.7(a)(1) confirms the earnings can stop: institutions "are not required to pay interest after time accounts mature," including "following the maturity of nonrollover time accounts." Other terms buried in the same opening package are catalogued in [Six Lines in a Bank Disclosure That Decide What You Earn](/2026/08/24/six-lines-in-a-bank-disclosure-that-decide-what-you-earn/).

## The rate the notice is allowed to leave blank

Both versions of the notice share one escape hatch, and the rule spells it out. From 1030.5(b)(1): "If the interest rate and annual percentage yield that will be paid for the new account are unknown when disclosures are provided, the institution shall state that those rates have not yet been determined, the date when they will be determined, and a telephone number consumers may call to obtain the interest rate and the annual percentage yield that will be paid for the new account."

[Appendix B to Part 1030](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-12/appendix-Appendix%20B%20to%20Part%201030) prints the sentence banks use. Model Clause B-3(a), for automatically renewable accounts of one year or less but longer than one month, offers the rate line in two forms: "The interest rate for the renewed account will be ____% with an annual percentage yield of ____%," or, failing that, "The interest rate and annual percentage yield have not yet been determined. They will be available on (date). Please call (phone number) to learn the interest rate and annual percentage yield for your new account."

The commentary is relaxed about how precisely that date gets pinned down. Acceptable answers include "a specific date, such as 'October 28,'" or "a date that is easily determinable, such as 'the Tuesday before the maturity date stated on this notice' or 'as of the maturity date stated on this notice.'" That last option lets the notice promise the renewal rate on the maturity date, the day the renewal happens.

That leaves the phone call, which has a rule of its own. Under [12 CFR 1030.3(e)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-12/section-1030.3), "in an oral response to a consumer's inquiry about interest rates payable on its accounts, the depository institution shall state the annual percentage yield. The interest rate may be stated in addition to the annual percentage yield. No other rate may be stated." A figure quoted over the phone is an APY unless the representative separately labels an interest rate, which makes it directly comparable to any other APY. Nothing else, including a promotional rate on some unrelated product, is a permitted answer to the question.

Written quotes carry a documented looseness. Section 1030.4(a)(2)(ii)(A) lets an institution responding to a request "specify an interest rate and annual percentage yield that were offered within the most recent seven calendar days; state that the rate and yield are accurate as of an identified date; and provide a telephone number consumers may call to obtain current rate information." A rate sheet handed across a counter can be a week stale by permission.

## Grace period mechanics

The grace period is a creature of contract that federal rules describe rather than require. Section 1030.4(b)(6)(iv), the renewal policies item in the opening disclosures, demands "a statement of whether or not the account will renew automatically at maturity. If it will, a statement of whether or not a grace period will be provided and, if so, the length of that period must be stated." The model clauses in Appendix B, at B-1(h)(iv), give both answers: "You will have [____ calendar/business] days after the maturity date to withdraw funds without penalty," or "There is no grace period following the maturity of this account to withdraw funds without penalty."

The second clause exists because the second outcome is allowed. A CD can renew with no window at all, and a disclosure saying so is compliant.

The common ten-day figure traces back to a footnote in the [Federal Reserve](https://www.federalreserve.gov/monetarypolicy.htm)'s Regulation D. [12 CFR 204.2](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-12/section-204.2) defines a time deposit around a mandatory penalty of "at least seven days' simple interest on amounts withdrawn within the first six days after deposit," and footnote 1 lists the cases where an institution may pay out early without imposing it. Item (f) on that list: "Where a time deposit is withdrawn within ten days after a specified maturity date even though the deposit contract provided for automatic renewal at the maturity date."

That clears the federal obstacle to a ten-day exit from a freshly renewed term. It does not create a right to one. The depositor's window is whatever the account disclosure states, and the regulation's only contribution is to make the longer choices possible.

Two other items on the same list have nothing to do with rate shopping. A deposit may be paid without penalty "upon the death of any owner of the time deposit funds." The same goes for the portion of a deposit that lost federal insurance when two insured banks merged, "for a period of one year from the date of the merger." The deposit insurance side of that scenario is in [When Your Bank Is Acquired, Not Failed: What Happens to Your CD](/2026/09/03/when-your-bank-is-acquired-not-failed-what-happens-to-your-cd/).

Credit unions run on a parallel track. Regulation DD "applies to depository institutions except for credit unions" under 1030.1(c), and share certificates follow the [National Credit Union Administration](https://ncua.gov/consumers/share-insurance-coverage)'s Truth in Savings rule at [12 CFR 707.5](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-12/section-707.5), which repeats the structure almost word for word with "term share account" in place of time account and "dividend rate" in place of interest rate. The thirty days, the twenty-day alternative and the five-day floor on the grace period all carry over.

## Interest during the days between two terms

The grace period suspends the penalty. It does not guarantee earnings. Comment 5 to 1030.7(a)(1) in the [official interpretations](https://www.consumerfinance.gov/rules-policy/regulations/1030/Interp-7/) says so directly: "Institutions are not required to pay interest after time accounts mature." The first example given is this exact situation, "during a grace period offered for an automatically renewable time account, if consumers decide during that period not to renew the account."

The disclosure rules do not close the gap either. The interpretation of the renewal policies item states that institutions "offering a grace period on time accounts that automatically renew need not state whether interest will be paid if the funds are withdrawn during the grace period." A bank is free to pay the new term's rate for those days, to pay nothing, or to say nothing about which it does.

On $25,000 moving to a replacement paying 4%, eight unpaid days come to $21.92 of interest that exists in neither account. Against a rate gap measured in hundreds of dollars that is a rounding error. It turns into something larger when the window is treated as a free look: every day left inside a renewed CD paying almost nothing costs the difference.

Confirm the numbers before moving, in this order: the renewal APY, the length of the grace period, and whether interest accrues inside it.

## The cost of noticing late

Miss the window and the money sits inside a new term at the new rate, with the old exit price attached. The penalty is disclosed under 1030.4(b)(6)(ii), which requires "a statement that a penalty will or may be imposed for early withdrawal, how it is calculated, and the conditions for its assessment." Because it is usually written in days of interest, it is computed on the renewed balance at the renewed rate, which shrinks the penalty as the rate falls.

Take $25,000 in a twelve-month CD that renews at 1% when a competitive alternative pays 4%, with a 180-day penalty. Daily interest on the renewed CD is $25,000 × 1% ÷ 365 = $0.6849, so the penalty is $123.29 whenever it is triggered. What moves is the prize:

| When the depositor acts | Extra interest at 4% over the remaining term | Penalty | Net |
|---|---|---|---|
| Inside the grace period | $750.00 | none | +$750.00 |
| Day 30 of the new term | $688.36 | $123.29 | +$565.07 |
| Day 180 | $380.14 | $123.29 | +$256.85 |
| Day 300 | $133.56 | $123.29 | +$10.27 |
| Day 330 | $71.92 | $123.29 | −$51.37 |

Acting inside the grace period is worth roughly $185 more than acting a month later, which is the real price of an envelope that went unopened. A late exit still usually beats no exit: at day 180 the switch clears $256.85 after the penalty. The point where waiting wins arrives late: the extra interest stops covering the $123.29 penalty once roughly 60 days are left.

The low renewal rate is what makes the penalty survivable. Had the CD renewed at 4% and the alternative paid 7%, the same 180-day penalty would come to $493.15, four times larger, because the penalty is denominated in the account's own interest. Cheap CDs are cheap to leave. The general version of this calculation, including partial withdrawals, is in [When Breaking a CD Actually Pays: Run the Break-Even](/2026/08/25/when-breaking-a-cd-actually-pays-run-the-break-even/), and what the penalty is worth on the tax return is in [CD Early Withdrawal Penalties: The Disclosure and the Deduction](/2026/09/22/cd-early-withdrawal-penalties-the-disclosure-and-the-deduction/).

A ladder removes the deadline problem instead of managing it. A maturity lands on the calendar every few months, so the renewal decision becomes a recurring appointment rather than an annual ambush. The arithmetic of building one while short terms pay best is in [Building a CD Ladder When 12-Month CDs Pay the Most](/2026/08/19/building-a-cd-ladder-when-12-month-cds-pay-the-most/).

## Benchmarks for the renewal rate

The free public benchmark is the [FDIC](https://www.fdic.gov/national-rates-and-rate-caps)'s national rate table, which as of August 17, 2026 put the twelve-month CD national average at 1.71% and the sixty-month average at 1.36%, with savings accounts at 0.38%. Against numbers like those a renewal quote lands as above or below the middle of the market.

The [FDIC](https://www.fdic.gov/national-rates-and-rate-caps)'s own page attaches three caveats to that comparison.

The figure is a deposit-weighted average, defined as "the average of rates paid by all insured depository institutions and credit unions for which data is available, with rates weighted by each institution's share of domestic deposits." Institutions holding the most money count the most, which pulls the average toward the largest branch networks rather than toward the best available offer. Beating the average is a low bar, not a target.

The tiers matter too. CD figures "represent an average of the $10,000 and $100,000 product tiers," so a small certificate is being held up against pricing set for larger ones.

And the number is old before it posts. The table "will be published every 3rd Monday of each month," while the inputs are "based on information available on the last business day of the prior month end." What appears on screen can be three weeks behind the market, which matters most when rates move quickly. Why posted rates lag harder in one direction than the other is covered in [Why Your Savings Rate Drops Faster Than It Rises](/2026/09/01/deposit-betas-why-savings-rates-fall-faster-than-they-rise/).

The same page carries a second figure that is not a consumer rate at all. The national rate cap, listed at 5.65% for twelve-month CDs on the August 17, 2026 table, is a supervisory ceiling. It is "calculated as the higher of: (1) the national rate plus 75 basis points; or (2) 120 percent of the current yield on similar maturity U.S. Department of the Treasury obligations plus 75 basis points," and it limits what a less than well capitalized institution may offer. It is neither a forecast nor an average, and no one is promising to pay it.

For a like-for-like check on the term itself, Treasury yields published by the government cover the same maturities, and the tax treatment differs enough to reorder the ranking. That comparison is worked through in [Treasury Bills vs CDs: The Tax Break That Changes the Comparison](/2026/09/23/treasury-bills-vs-cds-the-tax-break-that-changes-the-comparison/).

## What else can change at renewal

The rate is the visible term. It is not the only one that resets.

Under 1030.5(b)(2)(ii)(C), the short notice has to flag "any difference in the terms of the new account as compared to the terms required to be disclosed under § 1030.4(b) of this part for the existing account." That list takes in the early withdrawal penalty, the compounding and crediting schedule, minimum balance requirements, and fees. A renewal can carry a longer penalty than the term that just ended, and the differences line is the only place it appears.

The commentary separates two kinds of change at rollover. If the institution initiates it, the pre-maturity notice rules apply. If the consumer initiates it, "the account opening disclosure requirements of § 1030.4(b) apply," and the worked example is a depositor who receives a notice on a one-year CD and asks to roll into a six-month one: the institution "must provide either account opening disclosures including the new maturity date or, if all other terms previously disclosed in the prematurity notice remain the same, only the new maturity date."

Mid-term changes are handled elsewhere. Section 1030.5(a)(1) requires 30 calendar days' advance notice "of any change in a term required to be disclosed under § 1030.4(b) of this part if the change may reduce the annual percentage yield or adversely affect the consumer." One exception covers the shortest CDs, at 1030.5(a)(2)(iii): no notice for "changes in any term for time accounts with maturities of one month or less." A 28-day CD on automatic renewal sits outside both the change-in-terms notice and the pre-maturity notice, since 1030.5(b) starts above one month. It can reprice indefinitely in silence.

Deposit insurance can turn on a renewal as well. [12 CFR 330.4(b)](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-12/section-330.4) continues separate coverage for six months after one insured institution assumes another's deposits, then adds a rule specific to CDs: time deposits maturing inside that window "and which are renewed at the same dollar amount (either with or without accrued interest having been added to the principal amount) and for the same term as the original deposit" stay separately insured "until the first maturity date after the six-month period." Those "renewed on any other basis, or that are not renewed and thereby become demand deposits, are separately insured only until the end of the six-month period." Changing the term or the amount at renewal, which is the ordinary response to a bad rate, is what ends the extra coverage.

Interest earned in the old term is taxed whether or not it leaves the bank. [IRS Publication 550](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p550) sets the general standard: "You constructively receive income when it is credited to your account or made available to you. You do not need to have physical possession of it." It is blunter about renewals in its discussion of original issue discount: "Renewable certificates. If you renew a CD at maturity, it is treated as a redemption and a purchase of a new certificate. This is true regardless of the terms of renewal." Rolling interest into the next term defers nothing. Why a long CD can produce a tax bill years before the money is reachable is in [Why You Owe Tax on a 5-Year CD You Cannot Touch Yet](/2026/09/25/why-you-owe-tax-on-a-5-year-cd-you-cannot-touch-yet/).

## Common questions

**Does the bank have to tell me the renewal rate before the CD renews?** Not necessarily. Section 1030.5(b)(1) permits a notice stating that "those rates have not yet been determined, the date when they will be determined, and a telephone number consumers may call," and the commentary accepts a determination date as late as "the maturity date stated on this notice." The obligation is to give a way to find out, not to quote a number in advance.

**What happens if the grace period closes before I act?** The deposit is committed to the new term at the new rate, and withdrawing triggers the early withdrawal penalty disclosed under 1030.4(b)(6)(ii). Because most penalties are expressed in days of interest on the account's own rate, a penalty calculated on a low renewal rate is small in dollars, and the break-even frequently still favors moving.

**Is a grace period required by law?** No. Section 1030.4(b)(6)(iv) requires the bank to state whether one is provided and how long it runs, and Appendix B supplies a model clause for accounts that offer none. Regulation D's footnote 1(f) permits, without requiring, a penalty-free withdrawal "within ten days after a specified maturity date" on a contract that renews automatically.

**Does the money earn interest during the grace period?** The commentary says interest is not required after maturity, and the disclosure rules add that a bank offering a grace period "need not state whether interest will be paid if the funds are withdrawn during the grace period." It is a question for the institution, and the answer is not standard across banks.

**Can a renewal change the penalty or the fees, not just the rate?** Yes. For maturities of one year or less, 1030.5(b)(2)(ii)(C) requires the notice to disclose "any difference in the terms of the new account" measured against the 1030.4(b) disclosures for the old one. Above a year, 1030.5(b)(1) requires the full set of account disclosures for the new account, penalty included.

The bank picks the mailing schedule, the rate inside the notice may be blank by design, and the window it announces can be five days long. The one date in the sequence that does not depend on an envelope is the maturity printed on the opening disclosure.
