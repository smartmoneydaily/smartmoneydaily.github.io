The Form 1099-OID arrives in the last week of January. Box 1 shows a figure in four digits. The CD it refers to was opened in 2024, matures in 2029, and has paid out nothing at all — no transfer, no check, no line on a checking statement. The balance shown in online banking cannot be reduced by a dollar without triggering the early withdrawal clause. The amount in Box 1 is taxable income for the year that just ended, and the tax on it is due in April, in cash the CD does not provide.

The form is correct. What decides this is not whether the money can be reached but how long the interest is deferred, and the dividing line sits at one year. [IRS Publication 550](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p550) puts it in two sentences under *Certificates of deposit and other deferred interest accounts*: "You generally must include this interest in your income when you actually receive it or are entitled to receive it without paying a substantial penalty. The same is true for accounts that mature in 1 year or less and pay interest in a single payment at maturity." Then the pivot: "If interest is deferred for more than 1 year, see Original Issue Discount (OID), later."

Three things follow. A multi-year CD that holds all of its interest until maturity is treated as a discounted debt instrument, producing income every year of its term whether or not anything is paid out. That income arrives on Form 1099-OID rather than Form 1099-INT, and the two forms report different things in nearly identical boxes. And the tax has to be funded from somewhere else, because the only account that earned the money is the one that will not release it.

The alternative is a CD that pays interest out monthly. It earns less in total, since nothing compounds inside the account, but every dollar of tax it creates arrives alongside cash to pay it with. Which of the two is worth more after five years is arithmetic, and the answer is closer than the yield difference suggests.

## The rule that decides which form arrives

Two separate rules are at work, and they meet at the crediting schedule.

The first is constructive receipt, stated in Publication 550 as a general principle before it is applied to deposits: "You constructively receive income when it is credited to your account or made available to you. You do not need to have physical possession of it. For example, you are considered to receive interest, dividends, or other earnings on any deposit or account in a bank, savings and loan association, or similar financial institution ... when they are credited to your account and subject to your withdrawal."

The words "subject to your withdrawal" are doing less work than they appear to, because the same passage lists four obstacles that do not break constructive receipt. Income is still constructively received even if the depositor must:

- "Make withdrawals in multiples of even amounts,"
- "Give a notice to withdraw before making the withdrawal,"
- "Withdraw all or part of the account to withdraw the earnings, or"
- "Pay a penalty on early withdrawals, unless the interest you are to receive on an early withdrawal or redemption is substantially less than the interest payable at maturity."

The fourth bullet is the hinge for every CD. A penalty alone does not postpone the tax. The exception only opens where the interest actually payable on an early exit is "substantially less than the interest payable at maturity," which is a different and much narrower question than whether a penalty exists. A CD that credits interest annually, lets the depositor draw that credited interest out, and charges a penalty only against principal is producing taxable income each year it credits.

The second rule is the one-year deferral line already quoted. Interest "paid at fixed intervals of 1 year or less during the term of the account" is ordinary interest income, reported by the bank in Box 1 of Form 1099-INT. The [Instructions for Forms 1099-INT and 1099-OID](https://www.irs.gov/instructions/i1099int) describe that box broadly enough to cover the whole category: "Include amounts of $10 or more, whether or not designated as interest, that are paid or credited to the person's account by savings and loan associations, mutual savings banks ... credit unions, or similar organizations."

"Paid or credited" is the operative pair. Crediting is enough. Nothing has to leave the bank.

Interest deferred past a year leaves Form 1099-INT entirely and becomes original issue discount. That is not a special rule invented for deposits — it is the same regime that governs zero coupon bonds, applied to a bank product that behaves the same way.

## Original issue discount on a bank deposit

Publication 550 opens its CD section with a six-word sentence that explains everything downstream of it: "A CD is a debt instrument."

The definition follows from that. "OID is a form of interest. You generally include OID in your income as it accrues over the term of the debt instrument, whether or not you receive any payments from the issuer." Structurally, "OID is the difference between the stated redemption price at maturity and the issue price" — so a $40,000 deposit that will return $48,666 in five years was bought at a discount to what it pays back. The presumption is stated directly: "All debt instruments that pay no interest before maturity are presumed to be issued at a discount."

The application to deposits is explicit: "If you buy a CD with a maturity of more than 1 year, you must include in income each year a part of the total interest due and report it in the same manner as other OID." And the same treatment reaches a list of products that are not always called CDs:

- Time deposits
- Bonus plans
- Savings certificates
- Deferred income certificates
- Bonus savings certificates
- Growth savings certificates

Marketing names on a rate sheet do not change the classification. What matters is the term and whether interest is deferred past a year.

The bank's filing duty is spelled out in the 1099 instructions, which require Form 1099-OID from "an issuer of a certificate of deposit (CD) made, purchased, or renewed after 1970 if the CD has OID and a term of more than 1 year." The same instructions set the reporting floor at "the total daily portions of original issue discount (OID) for a holder is at least $10" — daily portions, because OID accrues by the day rather than on a crediting date, which is why the figure on a 1099-OID for the first partial year of a CD often matches no line on any statement.

Publication 550 works a deposit example in full, under *Time deposit open account arrangement*: "you and your bank enter into an arrangement under which you agree to deposit $100 each month for a period of 5 years. Interest will be compounded twice a year at 7 1/2%, but payable only at the end of the 5-year period. You must include a part of the interest in your income as OID each year. Each year the bank must give you a Form 1099-OID to show you the amount you must include in your income for the year." The defining feature is stated one sentence earlier — "there is no actual or constructive receipt of interest until the fixed maturity date is reached" — and that is exactly the condition that makes the money untouchable and the tax annual at the same time.

The OID rules carry a de minimis escape hatch, and the numbers show why it almost never reaches a bank CD. Publication 550: "You can treat the discount as zero if it is less than one-fourth of 1% (0.0025) of the stated redemption price at maturity multiplied by the number of full years from the date of original issue to maturity." For a five-year CD redeeming at $48,666.11, that threshold is 0.0025 × $48,666.11 × 5, or **$608.33**. The deferred interest is $8,666.11, more than fourteen times it.

## Five years of tax on a balance that has not moved

Take $40,000 in a five-year CD at a hypothetical 4%, compounded annually, with everything payable at maturity. Assume a 22% marginal rate throughout, and set aside state tax.

| Year | Interest accrued | Reported as income | Tax at 22% | Cash available from the CD |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | $1,600.00 | $1,600.00 | $352.00 | $0 |
| 2 | $1,664.00 | $1,664.00 | $366.08 | $0 |
| 3 | $1,730.56 | $1,730.56 | $380.72 | $0 |
| 4 | $1,799.78 | $1,799.78 | $395.95 | $0 |
| 5 | $1,871.77 | $1,871.77 | $411.79 | $48,666.11 |
| **Total** | **$8,666.11** | **$8,666.11** | **$1,906.54** | — |

The fourth column is the whole problem. By the time the CD matures, **$1,494.75** of tax has already been paid on it — the first four years added together — out of money that came from somewhere other than the CD. The account itself produced no cash at all until the final row.

Now the same $40,000 for five years at a stated 4% paid out monthly, $133.33 a month, nothing compounding inside the account:

| Year | Interest paid out | Reported as income | Tax at 22% | Cash received during the year |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | $1,600.00 | $1,600.00 | $352.00 | $1,600.00 |
| 2 | $1,600.00 | $1,600.00 | $352.00 | $1,600.00 |
| 3 | $1,600.00 | $1,600.00 | $352.00 | $1,600.00 |
| 4 | $1,600.00 | $1,600.00 | $352.00 | $1,600.00 |
| 5 | $1,600.00 | $1,600.00 | $352.00 | $1,600.00 |
| **Total** | **$8,000.00** | **$8,000.00** | **$1,760.00** | **$8,000.00** |

Both CDs are taxed every year of their term. Only one of them hands over the cash to pay the bill.

| | Compounding, paid at maturity | Paid out monthly |
|---|---|---|
| Interest over five years | $8,666.11 | $8,000.00 |
| Total tax at 22% | $1,906.54 | $1,760.00 |
| After-tax interest | $6,759.57 | $6,240.00 |
| Tax funded from outside the CD | $1,494.75 | $0 |

The compounding CD wins by **$519.57** over five years, about 1.3% of the original deposit, and the price of that win is $1,494.75 of tax paid out of pocket before a dollar can be withdrawn. The gap also understates the payout CD, whose $1,248.00 of after-tax cash each year is available to reinvest. The pre-tax yield difference is the part the rate sheet shows; the cash-flow difference is the part it does not, and reading what a disclosure actually promises is covered in [Six Lines in a Bank Disclosure That Decide What You Earn](/2026/08/24/six-lines-in-a-bank-disclosure-that-decide-what-you-earn/).

## The cash the tax has to come from

Nothing is withheld from a CD by default. A bank takes 24% off the top only when backup withholding has been triggered, which is a name-and-number problem rather than a feature of the account, covered in [Backup Withholding on Savings Interest: Why the Bank Took 24%](/2026/09/21/backup-withholding-on-savings-interest-why-the-bank-took-24/). Absent that, the full accrual is reported and none of it is prepaid. The obligation lands whole, once a year, on a return.

For a $40,000 CD that is a manageable number. Scale it up and the arithmetic changes character. On $250,000 at the same hypothetical 4%, the first year's accrual is $10,000 and the tax at 22% is $2,200, rising each year as the balance compounds — and at higher incomes the net investment income tax sits on top of it. Publication 550 describes that as "a 3.8% tax on the lesser of your net investment income or the amount of your modified adjusted gross income (MAGI) that is over a threshold amount based on your filing status," with thresholds of $250,000 for married filing jointly or qualifying surviving spouse, $125,000 for married filing separately, and $200,000 for single or head of household. A locked CD can push a return over one of those lines using money the taxpayer cannot spend.

Four ways to close the gap, in rough order of cost:

1. **Take the payout option at the counter.** The choice between compounding and monthly payment is usually offered at account opening and is far harder to change afterwards. It costs a slice of yield and removes the funding problem entirely.
2. **Ladder shorter terms.** A rung maturing every twelve months produces cash on a schedule, and the [FDIC national average](https://www.fdic.gov/national-rates-and-rate-caps) rates as of August 17, 2026 make the case on their own: 1.71% at 12 months against 1.36% at 60 months, with every term past a year paying less than the one-year figure. The construction is in [Building a CD Ladder When 12-Month CDs Pay the Most](/2026/08/19/building-a-cd-ladder-when-12-month-cds-pay-the-most/).
3. **Hold the tax money in a liquid account.** Parking each year's estimated tax in a savings account costs the spread between the two rates and nothing else.
4. **Break the CD.** The most expensive option, because the penalty is measured in days of interest and can reach principal. The disclosure rules and the offsetting deduction are in [CD Early Withdrawal Penalties: The Disclosure and the Deduction](/2026/09/22/cd-early-withdrawal-penalties-the-disclosure-and-the-deduction/).

Worth checking before any of these: some deposit agreements permit withdrawal of credited interest without touching principal, which moves the account closer to the payout column. Whether that is allowed, and what it does to the rate on the balance left behind, sits in the account disclosure rather than the rate table. Products built around liquidity rather than yield are compared in [No-Penalty, Bump-Up, and Add-On CDs: When the Variants Pay](/2026/08/28/no-penalty-bump-up-and-add-on-cds-when-the-variants-pay/).

## The one-year line

Everything above turns on a term longer than twelve months. On the other side of that line the treatment reverses, and the reversal is not always an improvement.

Publication 550 handles short accounts in the same paragraph as the deferral rule: "The same is true for accounts that mature in 1 year or less and pay interest in a single payment at maturity" — meaning the interest is income when received or made available, not as it accrues. The OID machinery is switched off for them by an explicit carve-out: "The OID accrual rules generally do not apply to short-term obligations (those with a fixed maturity date of 1 year or less from date of issue)." The list of exceptions to reporting OID as current income repeats it as item 3, "Short-term debt instruments (those with a fixed maturity date of not more than 1 year from the date of issue)."

The reporting follows the same split. The 1099 instructions direct issuers to "report OID on obligations with a term of 1 year or less on Form 1099-INT," so a 12-month CD paying everything at maturity generates one Form 1099-INT in the year it matures and nothing in the year it was opened.

That is simpler, and it concentrates. An 11-month CD opened in March pays all of its interest in one calendar year. A 60-month CD spreads the same total across five returns in roughly equal slices, which is usually the friendlier outcome for anyone near a bracket edge, and the less convenient one for anyone expecting to be in a lower bracket later. The choice of term is a tax-timing decision as much as a rate decision, and the underlying arithmetic of what a rate is worth after tax is worked through in [The Tax Bite on Savings Interest: What Your Real Yield Is](/2026/08/26/the-tax-bite-on-savings-interest-what-your-real-yield-is/).

## Renewal, early redemption, and the accrued amount

Two events end a CD early or start a new one, and both have their own line in Publication 550.

Automatic renewal is not a continuation. "If you renew a CD at maturity, it is treated as a redemption and a purchase of a new certificate. This is true regardless of the terms of renewal." The last sentence forecloses the argument that a rollover the depositor never touched is somehow the same deposit. The old CD is redeemed, with whatever that implies for the accrued interest, and a new instrument begins — new term, new rate, and a fresh OID clock if that term runs past a year. [What happens when](/2026/09/13/what-happens-when-you-deposit-more-than-10000-in-cash/) the institution behind the CD changes hands mid-term is a separate question, covered in [When Your Bank Is Acquired, Not Failed: What Happens to Your CD](/2026/09/03/when-your-bank-is-acquired-not-failed-what-happens-to-your-cd/).

Early redemption creates the mirror image of the annual accrual: income was reported in past years that will never be collected. Publication 550 provides the correction under *Redemption before maturity*: "If, before the maturity date, you redeem a deferred interest account for less than its stated redemption price at maturity, you can deduct OID that you previously included in income but did not receive."

The penalty itself is reported separately. On Form 1099-OID it lands in Box 3, described in the instructions as "interest or principal forfeited because of an early withdrawal of time deposits, such as an early withdrawal from a CD, that is deductible from gross income by the recipient," with the same warning that appears on the 1099-INT version: "Do not reduce the amounts in boxes 1 and 2 by the amount of the forfeiture." Publication 550 points to the same box: "If you forfeited interest or principal on the obligation because of an early withdrawal, the deductible amount will be shown in Form 1099-OID, box 3."

Note what Box 2 of Form 1099-OID is not. It holds "any qualified stated interest paid or credited on this obligation during the year" — actual periodic interest on an instrument that also carries OID. On Form 1099-INT, Box 2 is the early withdrawal penalty. Same box number, two meanings. Whether breaking the CD is worth it at all, before the tax layer, is run in [When Breaking a CD Actually Pays: Run the Break-Even](/2026/08/25/when-breaking-a-cd-actually-pays-run-the-break-even/).

## Frozen deposits, the narrow exception

One situation does leave interest on an unreachable deposit untaxed, and its limits matter precisely because they exclude the ordinary locked CD.

Publication 550: "Exclude from your gross income interest on frozen deposits. A deposit is frozen if, at the end of the year, you cannot withdraw any part of the deposit because:

- The financial institution is, or may become, bankrupt or insolvent, or
- The state in which the institution is located has placed limits on withdrawals because other financial institutions in the state are, or may become, bankrupt or insolvent."

Both triggers involve the institution's condition. Neither is satisfied by a contract the depositor signed. A five-year CD is illiquid by agreement, not frozen by insolvency, and the distinction is the difference between an exclusion and a tax bill.

The measurement rule closes the door further. The amount excluded is the credited interest minus "the net amount you withdrew from these deposits during the year" and "the amount you could have withdrawn at the end of the year (not reduced by any penalty for premature withdrawals of a time deposit)." The parenthesis is decisive: an amount that could have been withdrawn by paying a penalty counts as available, so the penalty does not create a freeze.

A Form 1099-INT that arrives for frozen deposits still goes on the return. Publication 550 sets out the mechanics: include the full amount on Schedule B (Form 1040), Part I, line 1, then "Below this subtotal, enter 'Frozen Deposits' and show the amount of interest that you are excluding. Subtract this amount from the subtotal and enter the result on Schedule B (Form 1040), Part I, line 2." Receiving that form is itself one of the conditions requiring Schedule B. What happens to a deposit and a CD contract when an institution actually fails is a separate chain of events, set out in [What Happens to Your Money When a Bank Fails](/2026/08/23/what-happens-to-your-money-when-a-bank-fails/).

## Where the numbers land on the return

The destination is ordinary. "Generally, you report all your taxable interest income on Form 1040 or 1040-SR, line 2b," and OID goes to the same line — Publication 550 says of Form 1099-OID that "In most cases, you must report the entire amount in Form 1099-OID, boxes 1, 2, and 8 as interest income."

Schedule B (Form 1040), Part I becomes mandatory in nine listed situations. Three of them are reachable from an ordinary deposit portfolio: "You had over $1,500 of taxable interest or ordinary dividends," "You received a Form 1099-INT for interest on frozen deposits," and "You are reporting OID in an amount less than the amount shown on Form 1099-OID." The instruction for listing payers is specific about brokered holdings: "If you received a Form 1099-INT or Form 1099-OID from a brokerage firm, list the brokerage firm as the payer."

That third trigger has its own convention. When less OID is reported than the form shows, the difference is displayed rather than silently omitted: "Show OID you do not have to report below a subtotal of the interest and OID listed. Identify the amount as 'OID Adjustment' and subtract it from the subtotal."

The early withdrawal penalty has a fixed destination that does not require itemizing. Publication 550: "You must include in income all interest shown in Form 1099-INT, box 1. You can deduct the penalty on Schedule 1 (Form 1040), line 18."

A missing form does not remove the obligation. "If you had OID for the year but did not receive a Form 1099-OID, you may have to figure the correct amount of OID to report on your return," with [Publication 1212](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p1212) supplying the method. The $10 daily-portions threshold means small accruals go unreported by the issuer, not untaxed.

## Common questions

**The bank sent a 1099-OID for a CD that has paid me nothing. Is that an error?** Not by itself. The 1099 instructions require the form from "an issuer of a certificate of deposit (CD) made, purchased, or renewed after 1970 if the CD has OID and a term of more than 1 year," and Publication 550 includes OID in income "as it accrues over the term of the debt instrument, whether or not you receive any payments from the issuer."

**Does a penalty on early withdrawal postpone the tax?** Generally no. The constructive receipt rule treats income as received even where the depositor must "Pay a penalty on early withdrawals," and carves out only the case where "the interest you are to receive on an early withdrawal or redemption is substantially less than the interest payable at maturity." A penalty is the norm on time deposits; the exception asks a narrower question, and how it applies to a specific contract is a question for a tax professional.

**Can I deduct interest I reported but never actually collected?** Publication 550 allows it in one defined circumstance: "If, before the maturity date, you redeem a deferred interest account for less than its stated redemption price at maturity, you can deduct OID that you previously included in income but did not receive." The early withdrawal penalty is separate and appears in Box 3 of Form 1099-OID.

**Is a locked CD the same as a frozen deposit for tax purposes?** No. A deposit is frozen only where the institution "is, or may become, bankrupt or insolvent" or a state has limited withdrawals for that reason. The exclusion calculation also counts money that could be withdrawn by paying a penalty as available, "not reduced by any penalty for premature withdrawals of a time deposit."

**Does a shorter CD avoid the problem?** It changes it. Under the short-term carve-out, "The OID accrual rules generally do not apply to short-term obligations (those with a fixed maturity date of 1 year or less from date of issue)," so a 12-month CD paying at maturity reports once, on Form 1099-INT, in the year it pays — no annual accrual, and the entire amount in a single tax year.

The 1099-OID that arrives for a CD with years left to run is not a mistake, and not a preview of a bill that waits for maturity. It is the deferral rule doing what it says, on a deposit whose term crossed twelve months. How much cash that costs was settled at the counter, in the box marked compound or pay out.
