The line on a bank's login screen that reads "[FDIC](https://www.fdic.gov/resources/deposit-insurance)-Insured — Backed by the full faith and credit of the U.S. Government" is there because a regulation puts it there, and the version now in force names three places it has to appear: the homepage, the login page, and the first screen of opening a deposit account. The [final rule](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2026-01-29/pdf/2026-01806.pdf) that settled the list was published in the Federal Register on January 29, 2026, took effect March 2, 2026, and gives banks until April 1, 2027 to comply. The DATES paragraph is short enough to quote whole: "Effective date: The amendments made in this rule are effective March 2, 2026. Compliance date: Compliance is required by April 1, 2027."

That April 2027 date is the fifth compliance deadline this part of the rule has carried. The original 2023 rulemaking "required full compliance by January 1, 2025," according to the FDIC's own [compliance-date letter](https://www.fdic.gov/news/inactive-financial-institution-letters/2025/compliance-date-extension-sections-3284-and-3285-amendments), and the deadline then moved to May 1, 2025, then to March 1, 2026, then to January 1, 2027, before the January 2026 final rule set April 1, 2027. The first extension came at banks' request for more time. The later ones had a different cause, which the preamble spells out: the digital signage provisions "continued to generate questions regarding implementation and had the potential to cause consumer confusion."

For a depositor, the practical content is in three parts. The sign itself has a required wording, a required color family and a required font family, so a look-alike is detectable. A second sign, for products that are not deposits, has its own required three-part message and its own placement standard. And a third element, a notice that has to appear for at least three seconds before a logged-in customer leaves the bank's app for a third party's investment product, marks the exact moment the insurance stops applying. Each is covered below, along with the deadline history and what to make of an app that shows no sign at all.

## What the sign has to say and how it has to look

The design requirement sits in the amended text of 12 CFR 328.5(b), reproduced in the [Federal Register rule](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2026-01-29/pdf/2026-01806.pdf): "In general, the 'FDIC' in the FDIC official digital sign shall be displayed in bold, navy blue or black, and the 'FDIC-Insured—Backed by the full faith and credit of the U.S. Government' shall be displayed in smaller type, in italic, and with navy blue or black lettering. The entire FDIC official digital sign shall be displayed in Source Sans Pro Web or similar font."

Two escape hatches follow in the same paragraph. Where the required colors would be illegible against the page's background, the sign "shall be displayed in white to contrast with the background, and must otherwise comply with the other format requirements." And the layout may wrap: the design is prescribed "for which wrapping may be permitted to address space constraints." A bank with a dark-mode app is therefore allowed a white sign, and a bank with a narrow phone layout is allowed to break the line, but neither is allowed to change the words, drop the italics, or switch to a display face that looks nothing like Source Sans.

What the rule does not do is pin the colors to a code. The preamble records that one commenter asked for "a range of acceptable hexadecimal color codes and minimum font sizes," and the FDIC declined: "The final rule requires that the text of the official digital sign be navy blue or black but does not mandate specific color codes." The stated reason is technical tolerance, "to accommodate technical limitations (e.g., space constraints, font availability, and color options for 'navy blue')." The agency adds that it "will continue to provide a standardized—but optional—digital official sign to IDIs via FDICconnect," which is why the sign looks identical across many unrelated banks: most of them use the file the FDIC hands out rather than typesetting their own.

The font and color rules exist so that a customer can tell an official sign from decorative text that borrows the initials. A bank with no FDIC insurance has no right to the sign at all.

## The three pages, and the pages that were dropped

Paragraph (c) of the amended section lists the pages: the digital channel "must clearly, continuously, and conspicuously display the FDIC official digital sign specified in paragraph (b) of this section on the following pages or screens: (1) Initial page or homepage of the website or application; (2) Login page; and (3) Page or screen where the consumer first initiates a deposit account opening."

The third item is where the 2026 rule departed most from the earlier version. The preamble explains that the 2023 rule required the sign on the "initial page or homepage of the website or application; landing or login pages; and pages where a customer may transact with deposits." Banks raised questions about both phrases; the preamble records that "landing page" "may be viewed as duplicative of 'login page,'" and that the transact-with-deposits language drew questions about which screens it reached. The FDIC's answer was to delete both phrases and replace the second with a single, identifiable screen. In the rule's words, the final rule "explicitly provides that the sign is required only on the first page or screen of the deposit account opening process."

The word "only" is doing real work there. A bank is not required to carry the sign on the account summary, on a transfer screen, or on the second step of an application. A customer who sees the sign at login and never again that session is looking at a bank that is following the rule.

Paragraph (e) of the amended section gives three examples of placement that satisfies the "clear, continuous, and conspicuous" standard: a homepage that "continuously displays the FDIC official digital sign near the top of the page and adjacent to the insured depository institution's name"; a mobile app login page "that displays the FDIC official digital sign immediately adjacent to the username and password fields"; and a web account-opening page that displays it "near the top or center of the page." The list is described as "not limited to, the following," so a bank may place the sign elsewhere on those pages.

The FDIC's [January 22, 2026 press release](https://www.fdic.gov/news/press-releases/2026/fdic-board-approves-final-rule-amend-official-signs-and-advertising) frames the change as narrowing: the rule focuses display requirements "on the screens and pages where signage would be most relevant for consumers."

## The second sign: not insured, not a deposit, may lose value

Most bank apps now sell things that are not deposits. A brokerage window, say, or an annuity page. The amended 328.5(d) requires that where a digital channel both takes deposits and "advertises or provides information about, or access to, one or more non-deposit products," it "must clearly, continuously, and conspicuously display signage indicating that the non-deposit products: are not insured by the FDIC; are not deposits; and may lose value."

The 2026 rule changed where that signage has to be. Paragraph (d)(1)(ii) states the standard: "This signage must be displayed on all pages or screens primarily dedicated to advertising or providing information about, or access to, one or more non-deposit products." "Primarily" is the operative word, and the preamble explains the line it draws. A bank's homepage, "typically geared toward general banking services, even if it includes limited graphics and links that allow customers to access pages with non-deposit products—would not meet this standard." By contrast, "pages on an IDI's digital deposit-taking channel where the primary focus of the content is marketing or providing information about non-deposit products (such as pages that are accessed by clicking 'Investing' or 'Wealth Management') will tend to be pages covered by this standard."

So the homepage can carry the FDIC sign and a small tile advertising investment accounts without the non-deposit warning. Tapping the tile leads to a page that has to carry it.

On wording, the rule requires the three ideas, not one fixed sentence. The preamble notes that the FDIC has previously said in Q&As that signage reading "Not FDIC Insured; No Bank Guarantee; May Lose Value" would meet the requirement. The preamble also addresses banks that already display such signage under other disclosure rules: if a bank "already continuously, clearly, and conspicuously displays non-deposit signage consistent with 12 CFR part 328, no additional changes would be required by the final rule."

On placement, the amended paragraph (e)(4) offers a concrete example: a page "promoting, for example, annuities available for purchase, with non-deposit signage appearing towards the bottom of the page in a manner that distinguishes the text of the non-deposit signage from the smallest text on the page using, for example, bold or larger text, or surrounding the signage with a text box." The preamble explains that the proposed version had asked for the signage near the promotional text or graphic itself, and the FDIC changed the example because "it is a common industry practice to include disclosures towards the bottom of a web page." The final standard, then, is that the warning may sit at the foot of the page, but it cannot be the smallest text there. If the "may lose value" line is set in the same size as the copyright notice, the page is not following the example the regulator wrote.

## The three-second notice before leaving the bank

The third element is the one a customer is most likely to notice and least likely to understand. Paragraph (d)(2) of the amended section covers a digital channel "that provides access to a non-deposit product from a non-bank third party's online interface." Such a channel "must provide a one-time per session notification to an insured depository institution customer who is logged into the insured depository institution's digital deposit-taking channel before the customer leaves the insured depository institution's digital deposit-taking channel to access the non-bank third party's non-deposit product."

The content is the same three-part message: the notice "must clearly and conspicuously indicate that the third party's non-deposit products: are not insured by the FDIC; are not deposits; and may lose value." The dismissal rule is specific. The requirement is satisfied if the notification either "is dismissed by an affirmative act of the bank customer, such as a click or swipe, after any period of time" or "automatically disappears after being displayed for a minimum of three seconds." A bank may do either, or both.

Three details from the preamble shape what this looks like in a real app. First, affiliates count as third parties. Commenters asked whether the notice applies when the investment platform is owned by the bank's own holding company, and the FDIC answered that the notice must appear when a logged-in customer moves from the bank's channel to an affiliate's website or application that offers non-deposit products. Second, the trigger is leaving, not looking: "The notification requirement applies only when a customer leaves the IDI's digital deposit-taking channel." A brokerage page hosted inside the bank's own app gets the static non-deposit sign from the previous section, not this pop-up. Third, the regulation's phrase is "one-time per session"; the rule does not require the notice to be repeated within the same login.

The pop-up marks a boundary: everything after it is on someone else's system, and the rule requires the bank to say so at the moment of crossing.

## Why the deadline moved four times

The compliance history is scattered across the Federal Register notices the rule cites and one Financial Institution Letter; the preamble collects it. The sequence, with the source for each date, is:

| Compliance date for the digital sign rules | Set by | Where it is recorded |
|---|---|---|
| January 1, 2025 | 2023 Final Rule, adopted December 20, 2023 | 89 FR 3504 (January 18, 2024) |
| May 1, 2025 | First extension, at banks' request for more time | 89 FR 84261 (October 22, 2024) |
| March 1, 2026 | Second extension, to let the FDIC propose changes | 90 FR 11659 (March 11, 2025); FIL-5-2025 (March 3, 2025) |
| January 1, 2027 | Third extension, during the rulemaking | 90 FR 54544 (November 28, 2025) |
| April 1, 2027 | Final rule of January 29, 2026 | 91 FR 3801 |

The first extension came because, as the preamble puts it, "some IDIs requested additional time to meet the new requirements." The second had a different cause. The FDIC "observed that the provisions governing signage requirements for digital deposit-taking channels and ATMs and like devices, 12 CFR 328.4 and 328.5, continued to generate questions regarding implementation and had the potential to cause consumer confusion," and the March 2025 extension "was intended to allow the FDIC to propose changes to these requirements." The FIL announcing that extension is blunter. Its explanation for postponing from May 1, 2025 to March 1, 2026 was that the digital sign requirements "continue to generate questions regarding implementation, and may result in consumer confusion."

The proposal came on August 21, 2025, the comment period closed October 20, 2025, and the FDIC "received a total of nine substantive comments from industry groups, a payments provider, a non-profit organization, and an individual." The final rule then picked April 1, 2027 rather than the proposed January 1, 2027 date, so that banks "have at least a full year to review the revised requirements and to implement any changes necessary."

One consequence is a gap between what a customer sees today and what the rule will require in April 2027. A bank that built its screens to the 2023 version, with the sign on every transaction page, may leave things as they are. A bank that has not yet built anything has until April 1, 2027. The FDIC decided not to grandfather early adopters wholesale: "The FDIC is not deeming IDIs currently in compliance with the regulation to be in compliance with this rule," though it adds that "for the most part" such banks would already comply, and the one place a 2023-compliant bank may still have work to do is the account-opening screen, because "this final rule expressly requires the display of the FDIC official digital sign on the deposit account opening page." So until the deadline, two banks that are both following the rules can look different from each other.

## Reading an app that has no sign

Here the rule starts to be useful to a depositor, not only to a compliance officer. The sign is required of insured depository institutions. It is not permitted to anyone else. The FDIC's [Part 328 questions and answers](https://www.fdic.gov/deposit-insurance/questions-and-answers-related-fdics-part-328-final-rule) address the question in the ATM context and take the position that display of the official digital sign by a non-bank third party is generally improper, because it would imply that the non-bank is itself FDIC-insured. A fintech app that holds customer money at a partner bank is a non-bank third party. What such an app may say is that deposits are held at a named insured bank, and the difference between that sentence and the official sign is the subject of [When 'FDIC Insured' Means a Partner Bank, Not Your App](https://smartmoneydaily.github.io/2026/09/08/when-fdic-insured-means-a-partner-bank-not-your-app/).

So the absence of the sign means one of three things. Either the app belongs to an insured bank that has not yet finished implementing a rule whose deadline is April 2027. Or the app belongs to a non-bank that is not permitted to display it and is instead routing deposits to an insured bank it should name. Or the app belongs to something that has no FDIC insurance behind it at all. The sign does not distinguish among the three; its presence is a positive signal from an insured bank, and its absence is a prompt to go and check. The checking procedure, using the FDIC's own institution lookup, is laid out in [How to Verify a Bank Is Actually FDIC-Insured Before You Transfer](https://smartmoneydaily.github.io/2026/09/09/how-to-verify-a-bank-is-actually-fdic-insured-before-you-transfer/).

ATMs are handled separately under amended 328.4, which requires the sign "on the initial screen" of a deposit-taking ATM and allows a machine placed into service on or before April 1, 2027 to display the physical sign instead. And nothing in Part 328 changes the insurance itself. The coverage limit and the ownership categories are set elsewhere. The sign says the institution is insured; it does not say a particular balance is fully covered.

## A screen-by-screen walk-through

Take a hypothetical insured bank with a mobile app that offers checking, savings, a CD, an in-app investing tab, and a link out to an affiliated brokerage. Under the amended rule, a customer moving through the app after the April 1, 2027 compliance date should see the following.

| Screen | What the rule requires | Source paragraph |
|---|---|---|
| App splash or home screen before login | FDIC official digital sign, navy blue or black, near the bank's name | 328.5(c)(1), example (e)(1) |
| Login screen | FDIC official digital sign, adjacent to the username and password fields | 328.5(c)(2), example (e)(2) |
| Account dashboard after login | No sign required | 328.5(c) lists three screens only |
| Transfer, bill pay, statements | No sign required | 328.5(c) |
| First screen of "Open a savings account" | FDIC official digital sign, near the top or center | 328.5(c)(3), example (e)(3) |
| Second and later screens of that application | No sign required | Preamble: "only on the first page or screen of the deposit account opening process" |
| A tile on the home screen advertising investing | No non-deposit signage required on the home screen | Preamble: homepage with limited links "would not meet this standard" |
| The "Investing" page inside the app | Not insured by the FDIC, not a deposit, may lose value, in text larger or bolder than the smallest text on the page | 328.5(d)(1), example (e)(4) |
| Tapping "Continue to brokerage" that leaves the app | One-time per session notice, dismissible by click or swipe or auto-dismissing after at least three seconds | 328.5(d)(2) |

The dashboard row surprises people: the page a customer spends the most time on is a page the rule does not touch. The "Investing" row is the protective one: if a page is primarily about products that can lose value, it has to say so in text that stands out from the fine print.

## What the sign settles and what it does not

Where a bank's app already shows the sign on the login screen, the sign means the institution is insured. It does not mean every product inside the app is a deposit, and the rule's own structure, with a second sign for non-deposit products and a third notice for leaving the channel, exists because insured banks sell uninsured things.

Where a bank's app shows no sign, the date matters. Before April 1, 2027, absence proves nothing either way about an insured bank. The check that settles the question is the FDIC's institution lookup, not the login page.

Where a fintech app shows the initials FDIC anywhere, the sentence around them carries the meaning. An insured bank displays the official sign in the official form. A non-bank is limited to describing the arrangement and naming the bank that holds the deposits, and the FDIC's guidance treats a non-bank's display of the official sign as generally improper.

### Does the sign have to be on every page of a bank's app?

No. The amended 12 CFR 328.5(c) lists three screens: the initial page or homepage, the login page, and the page where a consumer first initiates a deposit account opening. The preamble states that the sign "is required only on the first page or screen of the deposit account opening process." Account dashboards, transfer screens, and later steps of an application are not on the list.

### A bank's app uses dark mode and the sign is white. Is that allowed?

Yes. Paragraph (b) provides that where the sign "would be illegible if displayed in the colors listed in this paragraph (b), due to the color of the background, the FDIC official digital sign shall be displayed in white to contrast with the background, and must otherwise comply with the other format requirements." The wording, italics, and font family still apply.

### Why does a pop-up appear for a few seconds when a brokerage is opened from a bank app?

That is the one-time per session notification under 328.5(d)(2). A bank whose app provides access to a third party's non-deposit product must show a notice, before the customer leaves the bank's channel, stating that the third party's products are not insured by the FDIC, are not deposits, and may lose value. It may be dismissed by a click or swipe, or it may disappear on its own after at least three seconds. The rule treats the bank's own affiliates as third parties for this purpose, so the notice appears even when the brokerage shares the bank's name.

### If a bank's app shows no sign today, is the bank out of compliance?

Not necessarily. The compliance date for the amended sections 328.4 and 328.5 is April 1, 2027, and until then a bank may still be building its screens. Whether the institution is insured is a separate question; the FDIC's institution lookup answers it directly.
