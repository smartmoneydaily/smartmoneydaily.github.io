![The 2027 COLA Is 3.6%: Savings Interest, the $25,560 Earnings Limit and SSI](/assets/pin-images/2026-10-18-the-2027-cola-is-36-savings-interest-the-25560-earnings-limit-and-ssi.png)

A 64-year-old who left a full-time job last year and started Social Security early, a hypothetical case, expects to keep a part-time job through 2027 and earn about $31,560 in wages. She also has $120,000 in CDs and Treasury bills that she expects to pay roughly $4,800 of interest next year. When Social Security announced the 2027 cost-of-living adjustment on October 14, 2026, her question was which of those two kinds of income can reduce her checks under Social Security's earnings test. Only the wages can. The interest doesn't count toward the earnings limit at all, though it still matters for income tax and, later, for Medicare premiums.

Social Security benefits and SSI payments go up 3.6% for 2027. For people under full retirement age all year, the earnings test limit rises to $25,560, and SSA withholds $1 of benefits for every $2 of earnings above it. In the year a person reaches full retirement age, the limit is $67,920 for earnings in the months before reaching that age, with $1 withheld for every $3 above it. SSA's own guidance says interest, investment earnings and capital gains aren't counted. On the SSI side, the maximum federal payment for an individual rises to $1,029 a month, while the $2,000 resource limit stays where it has been since 1989. Interest earned on an SSI recipient's countable savings is excluded from income, but the balance itself is counted on the first of every month.

The figures here come from SSA's [October 14, 2026 press release](https://www.ssa.gov/news/en/press/releases/2026-10-14.html), the [2027 COLA fact sheet](https://www.ssa.gov/news/en/cola/factsheets/2027.html), the Office of the Chief Actuary's pages on the [COLA computation](https://www.ssa.gov/oact/cola/latestCOLA.html), the [earnings test exempt amounts](https://www.ssa.gov/oact/cola/rtea.html) and [2027 SSI payment amounts](https://www.ssa.gov/oact/cola/SSI.html), SSA's publication [How Work Affects Your Benefits](https://www.ssa.gov/pubs/EN-05-10069.pdf) and its [Receiving Benefits While Working](https://www.ssa.gov/benefits/retirement/planner/whileworking.html) page, and sections of SSA's Program Operations Manual System (POMS) linked below. Tax and Medicare points refer to IRS [Publication 915](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p915) and [POMS HI 01101.010](https://secure.ssa.gov/poms.nsf/lnx/0601101010). This is general information about how the rules work, not tax, legal or benefits advice for any person.

## The 2027 figures, next to 2026

SSA's fact sheet lists the adjustments together. These are the ones tied to work, savings and benefit amounts:

| Item | 2026 | 2027 |
|---|---|---|
| Earnings test limit, under full retirement age all year | $24,480 a year ($2,040 a month) | $25,560 a year ($2,130 a month) |
| Earnings test limit, year of reaching full retirement age | $65,160 a year ($5,430 a month) | $67,920 a year ($5,660 a month) |
| Maximum earnings subject to Social Security tax | $184,500 | $192,300 |
| Earnings for one quarter of coverage | $1,890 | $1,970 |
| Substantial gainful activity, non-blind | $1,690 a month | $1,770 a month |
| Substantial gainful activity, blind | $2,830 a month | $2,950 a month |
| SSI federal payment, individual | $994 a month | $1,029 a month |
| SSI federal payment, couple | $1,491 a month | $1,544 a month |
| SSI resource limit, individual | $2,000 | $2,000 |
| SSI resource limit, couple | $3,000 | $3,000 |

The press release says: "On average, Social Security retirement benefits will increase by $75 per month." The fact sheet's estimate for all retired workers is an average benefit payable in January 2027 of $2,095 before the COLA and $2,170 after it.

Not every number in the table moves with the COLA. The earnings test limits follow wages, not prices. The actuary's page on exempt amounts says: "These exempt amounts generally increase annually with increases in the national average wage index." That is why the lower limit rose about 4.4%, from $24,480 to $25,560, while benefits rose 3.6%. The SSI payment amounts do follow the COLA. The SSI resource limits are fixed dollar amounts set by statute, with no automatic adjustment for prices or wages. SSA's manual section on them, [POMS SI 01110.003](https://secure.ssa.gov/poms.nsf/lnx/0501110003), lists $2,000 and $3,000 as effective January 1, 1989, and notes: "The statutory limits have not changed since 1/1/89."

## How the 3.6% was figured

The COLA comes from a fixed formula in the Social Security Act. The actuary's page explains that a COLA is "equal to the percentage increase (if any) in the CPI-W from the average for the third quarter of the current year to the average for the third quarter of the last year in which a COLA became effective." CPI-W is the Consumer Price Index for Urban Wage Earners and Clerical Workers, which the Bureau of Labor Statistics calculates monthly.

The inputs SSA published:

| CPI-W | 2025 | 2026 |
|---|---|---|
| July | 316.349 | 327.104 |
| August | 317.306 | 328.481 |
| September | 318.139 | 330.654 |
| Third-quarter average | 317.265 | 328.746 |

The calculation is (328.746 − 317.265) ÷ 317.265 × 100, which comes to about 3.62. The rule is that "If there is an increase, it must be rounded to the nearest tenth of one percent," so the COLA is 3.6%.

## When the higher payments and the notices arrive

The increase starts with benefits for December 2026. The actuary's page says: "Social Security benefits will increase by 3.6 percent beginning with the December 2026 benefits, which are payable in January 2027." SSI runs on a different calendar. Federal SSI payment levels rise "effective for payments made for January 2027," and the same page explains: "Because the normal SSI payment date is the first of the month and January 1 is a holiday, the SSI payments for January are always made at the end of the previous December."

The press release puts dates on both: "Nearly 72 million Social Security beneficiaries will see a 3.6 percent cost-of-living adjustment (COLA) beginning in January 2027. Increased payments to about 7.3 million people receiving SSI will begin on December 31, 2026."

The notice showing each person's new amount comes in two ways. People with a personal my Social Security account can see it online starting in late November, and the release adds: "Individuals who do not have a personal account will need to create one and enroll in paperless notices by November 18 to see their COLA notice online." People who haven't enrolled in paperless notices get it by mail in December. SSA describes it as a one-page notice that "provides exact dates and dollar amounts of an individual's new benefit amount and any deductions."

Medicare premiums aren't part of this announcement. The release says: "For Medicare enrollees, the 2027 premium amount will be available via my Social Security Message Center starting in late November." For anyone who has Medicare premiums deducted from benefits, the net change in the January deposit depends on that figure.

## A 3.6% COLA next to what savings paid

The COLA measures how much a particular price index rose over a year. A savings account gets no such adjustment; its rate is whatever the bank sets. The [FDIC](https://www.fdic.gov/national-rates-and-rate-caps)'s monthly national rates give a sense of the gap. The FDIC defines the national rate as "the average of rates paid by all insured depository institutions and credit unions for which data is available, with rates weighted by each institution's share of domestic deposits." In the figures dated September 21, 2026, the national rate was 0.37% for savings accounts, 0.63% for money market accounts and 1.73% for 12-month CDs. The page notes that published rates "are based on information available on the last business day of the prior month end."

On $20,000, a year at 0.37% works out to about $74 of interest before tax. For comparison, 3.6% of the same $20,000 is $720. The two numbers aren't measured over the same period, since the COLA compares quarterly price averages a year apart and the FDIC figure is a snapshot of rates, and individual banks pay more or less than the average. The comparison only shows how far an average savings rate sat below the price increase that set the COLA. Interest on savings is also taxable income; which tax year it lands in is covered in [T-Bills and Short CDs Bought Now: Which Tax Year Gets the Interest](/2026/10/16/t-bills-and-short-cds-bought-now-which-tax-year-gets-the-interest/).

## Who the earnings test applies to

The earnings test applies only before full retirement age. SSA's publication says that if you were born January 2, 1960 or later, "your full retirement age for retirement insurance benefits is 67," and that once you're at that age or older, "you may keep all of your benefits, no matter how much you earn."

Below that age, two limits apply in 2027:

- **Under full retirement age for all of 2027:** $25,560. The fact sheet's note: "One dollar in benefits will be withheld for every $2 in earnings above the limit."
- **Reaching full retirement age during 2027:** $67,920, which "Applies only to earnings for months prior to attaining full retirement age. One dollar in benefits will be withheld for every $3 in earnings above the limit."

The actuary's page adds: "Earnings in or after the month you reach NRA do not count toward the retirement test." NRA, normal retirement age, is the actuaries' term for full retirement age.

Survivors are measured against the retirement age, not the survivor age. SSA's working page says: "If you receive survivors benefits, we use your full retirement age for retirement benefits when applying the annual earnings test (AET) for retirement or survivors benefits." People receiving disability benefits or SSI are under different rules altogether; the publication says that in those cases "you must report all earnings to us."

## What counts as earnings, and what doesn't

The publication's section titled "What income counts…and when do we count it?" says:

"If you work for someone else, only your wages count toward Social Security's earnings limits. If you're self-employed, we count only your net earnings from self-employment. For the earnings limits, we don't count income such as other government benefits, investment earnings, interest, pensions, annuities, and capital gains. However, we do count an employee's contribution to a pension or retirement plan if the contribution amount is included in the employee's gross wages."

The working page lists it the other way around: "We include bonuses, commissions, and vacation pay. We don't count pensions, annuities, investment income, interest, veterans benefits, or other government or military retirement benefits."

For someone living partly on savings before full retirement age, that has several practical effects:

- Interest on savings accounts, money market accounts, CDs and Treasury bills isn't counted, however large it is. A 1099-INT has no role in the earnings test.
- A CD that matures in 2027 and pays several years of interest at once doesn't count against the 2027 limit.
- Selling investments at a gain doesn't count, because capital gains are on the excluded list.
- Putting part of a paycheck into a workplace retirement plan doesn't necessarily lower the earnings SSA counts. Under the publication's last sentence above, a contribution that is included in gross wages still counts.

Timing follows the source of the money. For employees, the publication says, "If you work for wages, income counts when it's earned, not when it's paid," and it gives accumulated sick or vacation pay and bonuses as examples of pay earned in one year and paid the next. For the self-employed it's the reverse: "If you're self-employed, income counts when you receive it — not when you earn it," with an exception for income paid in a year after a person becomes entitled to benefits but earned before entitlement.

## Worked example: $4,800 of interest vs $4,800 of wages

Back to the 64-year-old from the opening. She was born in 1962, so her full retirement age is 67, which she reaches in 2029, and she's under it for all of 2027. Suppose her benefit after the COLA is $1,600 a month, a made-up round figure, or $19,200 for the year.

**Her wages.** $31,560 − $25,560 = $6,000 over the limit. At $1 for every $2, SSA withholds $3,000 of benefits for 2027.

**Her interest.** The $4,800 from CDs and Treasury bills doesn't enter the calculation. Withholding stays at $3,000.

**If the $4,800 had been wages instead.** Her earnings would be $36,360, which is $10,800 over the limit, and withholding would be $5,400. The same $4,800 costs $2,400 of benefits for the year when it comes from a job and nothing when it comes from interest.

How SSA collects the $3,000 matters for cash flow. In SSA's own examples, it withholds whole monthly checks from the start of the year until it has withheld at least the required amount, then pays the overage back later. In the publication's 2026 example, a person needing $800 withheld from $600 checks has January and February withheld, gets checks again starting in March, and is told: "In 2027, we would pay you the additional $400 we withheld in February 2026." Applied to her numbers, the January and February checks, $3,200 in all, would be withheld, payments would resume in March, and the extra $200 would be paid back in 2028.

In this example, two monthly payments are held at the start of the year. Dividing the expected withholding by the monthly benefit gives a rough count of how many checks would be held. SSA works from the earnings estimate the beneficiary gives it; the publication says that if you think your earnings "will be different from what you originally told us, let us know right away."

## Withheld months and the recalculation at full retirement age

At full retirement age, SSA recalculates the worker's monthly benefit to account for the months that were withheld. That is separate from the repayment of excess withholding in the examples above. The actuary's page says "any benefits withheld while you continue to work are not 'lost'. Once you reach NRA, your monthly benefit will be increased permanently to account for the months in which benefits were withheld." The publication gives an example: a person who claims at 62 with a $910 benefit and has 12 months withheld would be paid $975 a month starting at full retirement age of 67, "in today's dollars."

Two exceptions are worth knowing about before relying on that recalculation. The publication says: "Spouses and survivors who receive benefits because they have minor children or children with disabilities in their care, don't receive increased benefits at full retirement age if benefits were withheld because of work." It also says that if other family members get benefits on your record, "your earnings from work you do after you start getting retirement benefits could reduce their benefits, too."

## The year you reach full retirement age

The higher limit applies only to earnings in the months before full retirement age. A hypothetical person who reaches full retirement age in September 2027 and has a $2,200 monthly benefit earns $72,000 from January through August.

- Earnings counted: $72,000, the January-through-August wages. Pay from September on doesn't count.
- Over the limit: $72,000 − $67,920 = $4,080.
- Withheld at $1 for every $3: $1,360.

Following SSA's method, the January check of $2,200 would be withheld, February's would be paid, and the $840 difference would be paid back the next year. Any interest she earned on savings during those months again plays no part.

## The first-year monthly rule

Someone who retires partway through a year has often already earned more than the annual limit. The publication describes a special rule that applies to earnings for one year, "usually the first year of retirement": "Under this rule, you can get a full Social Security check for any whole month you're retired, regardless of your yearly earnings."

"Retired," for this rule, is measured by monthly wages. The 2026 publication says: "In 2026, a person younger than full retirement age for the entire year is considered retired if monthly earnings are $2,040 or less." The monthly figure isn't set separately; SSA's manual section on exempt amounts, [POMS RS 02501.025](https://secure.ssa.gov/poms.nsf/lnx/0302501025), says: "The monthly exempt amount is equal to 1/12 of the applicable annual exempt amount." For 2027, one-twelfth of $25,560 is $2,130, the monthly figure on the fact sheet.

Example: a 62-year-old earns $60,000 from January through June 2027, retires at the end of June, starts benefits in July and takes a part-time job paying $1,500 a month. Total 2027 wages are $69,000, far above $25,560. Because July through December wages are under $2,130 in each month, those months can be paid in full under the monthly rule. In the publication's matching 2026 example, the next sentence is: "Beginning in 2027, only the annual limit will apply." For this person, that would be 2028.

Self-employed people are judged by hours as well as dollars. "In general, if you work more than 45 hours a month in self-employment, you're not retired. If you work less than 15 hours a month, you're retired." Between 15 and 45 hours, the publication says, you won't be considered retired if the work requires a lot of skill or you're managing a sizable business.

## SSI: the payment rises, the $2,000 limit doesn't

For SSI, the COLA works on the payment and leaves the savings rules alone. The actuary's SSI page describes how the new amounts are figured: "In general, monthly amounts for the next year are determined by increasing the unrounded annual amounts for the current year by the COLA effective for January of the next year. The new unrounded amounts are then each divided by 12 and the resulting amounts are rounded down to the next lower multiple of $1."

For an individual, the unrounded 2026 annual amount of $11,929.46 becomes $12,358.92 for 2027. Divided by 12, that's $1,029.91, rounded down to $1,029 a month. For a couple, $17,892.21 becomes $18,536.33, or $1,544 a month. The page adds that "Some States supplement SSI benefits," so the total paid in some states is higher.

The resource limit is measured once a month. [POMS SI 01110.600](https://secure.ssa.gov/poms.nsf/lnx/0501110600) says: "We make all resources determinations as of the first moment of a calendar month." Something received during a month is evaluated under the income-counting rules for that month, and the same section says: "If the individual retains the item into the month following that of receipt, we evaluate it under the resource-counting rules."

A larger payment and an unchanged limit leave less room. With a $1,029 payment, the space between one month's full payment and the $2,000 individual limit is $971. Unless an exclusion applies, such as the early-payment rule described next, any part of a month's payment still in the account on the first of the next month generally counts toward that $2,000, along with the person's other countable resources. How an ABLE account changes this count is covered in [ABLE Accounts in 2026: Age 46, a $20,000 Limit and SSI's $100,000 Rule](/2026/10/17/able-accounts-in-2026-age-46-a-20000-limit-and-ssis-100000-rule/).

## The December 31 payment and the January 1 balance

Because January's SSI payment arrives on December 31, 2026, it may still be in a recipient's account at the first moment of January, the moment SSA uses for the January resource count. SSA's manual has a rule for this. [POMS SI 01140.200](https://secure.ssa.gov/poms.nsf/lnx/0501140200) explains that SSI payments "may be included in the account balance due to an early deposit of a benefit payment (EDB)," and when that is "material to eligibility," the instruction to SSA staff is to "Deduct the value of the SSI payment and any Federally-administered State supplement from the FOM account balance," using the exclusion reason "Early RSDI or SSI payment."

Example: a recipient has $1,500 in savings, and the $1,029 January payment lands on December 31. Her bank shows $2,529 on January 1. Counted at face value, that's over the $2,000 limit. Under the early-payment rule, the $1,029 is taken out of the January 1 figure, leaving $1,500. Whatever part of that payment is still unspent on February 1 is a different matter, because by then it has been held past the month it was for.

For SSI recipients, the same manual also covers other regular income, such as Social Security benefits or wages, that arrives early because of a weekend or holiday. [POMS SI 00810.030](https://secure.ssa.gov/poms.nsf/lnx/0500810030) says to treat direct deposits that post early "as income in the month of normal receipt," and that "Such funds are first subject to evaluation as resources in the month following the month of normal receipt."

## SSI and the interest on savings

SSI treats the interest and the balance differently. [POMS SI 00830.500](https://secure.ssa.gov/poms.nsf/lnx/0500830500) explains that under the Social Security Protection Act of 2004, interest or dividends earned on resources that aren't excluded under section 1613(a) of the Social Security Act, or that are excluded under other federal statutes, "are excluded income for SSI benefits payable on or after July 1, 2004." Its table puts the common case plainly: when the source of the interest is "a countable resource," then "the dividends or interest are excluded income."

Three details from the same section:

- **Bonuses count as interest.** "A cash gift or incentive payment to open an account (e.g., checking, savings, certificate of deposit) is considered interest."
- **Kept interest becomes part of the balance.** "However, the interest or dividends may be countable resources if retained into the following month."
- **Penalties don't reduce it.** "Account service fees or penalties for early withdrawal do not reduce the amount of interest or dividend income."

So interest on an SSI recipient's ordinary savings account doesn't lower the monthly payment, but it adds to the balance that is checked against $2,000 each month. On balances that small, at the average savings rates above, the amounts involved are a few dollars a year.

## Taxes and Medicare premiums move on their own schedules

The earnings test ignores interest, but the federal tax test for Social Security benefits doesn't. Publication 915 tells you to "compare the base amount (explained later) for your filing status with the total of: One-half of your benefits; plus All your other income, including tax-exempt interest." The base amount in the 2025 edition is $25,000 for single filers, heads of household and qualifying surviving spouses, and $32,000 for married couples filing jointly. A higher benefit raises the "one-half of your benefits" figure in that comparison, and savings interest is part of "all your other income." The full worksheet, with examples, is in [Does Savings Interest Make Your Social Security Taxable?](/2026/10/11/does-savings-interest-make-your-social-security-taxable/)

Withholding chosen on [Form W-4V](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/fw4v.pdf) is a rate, not a dollar amount. The form offers federal withholding from Social Security benefits "at the rate of (check one): 7% 10% 12% 22%," so if the chosen rate stays the same, the dollars withheld each month rise with the benefit after a COLA.

Medicare's income-related premium adjustment uses a different income figure. POMS HI 01101.010 defines modified adjusted gross income for this purpose as adjusted gross income plus "tax-exempt interest income (line 2a of IRS Form 1040)." Taxable interest is already in adjusted gross income. The same section says: "Generally, the information is from two years prior to the year for which the premium is being determined, but not more than three years prior. For example, 2026 premiums are generally based on 2024 tax return information." By that rule, 2027 premiums would generally be based on 2025 returns, so interest earned in 2025 is generally what feeds next year's premiums. The section's threshold table runs through 2026, when the threshold was $218,000 for married couples filing jointly and $109,000 for all other filing statuses. The 2027 premium amounts will reach enrollees through the my Social Security Message Center starting in late November, according to the press release.

A household whose income fell because of a life-changing event can ask for a lower adjustment. SSA's page on the request says: "Life-changing events include marriage, divorce, the death of a spouse, loss of income, and an employer settlement payment." The request is made on Form SSA-44.

## How savings interest counts, test by test

| Test | Does interest on savings count? | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Social Security earnings test (under full retirement age) | No. Only wages and net self-employment earnings count | How Work Affects Your Benefits |
| SSI income | Interest on countable resources is excluded income | POMS SI 00830.500 |
| SSI resources | The balance counts on the first of each month, including interest kept into the next month | POMS SI 01110.600; SI 00830.500 |
| Federal tax on Social Security benefits | Yes, including tax-exempt interest | IRS Publication 915 |
| Medicare income-related premium | Yes, through adjusted gross income plus tax-exempt interest | POMS HI 01101.010 |

In the opening example, the $4,800 of CD interest is left out of the earnings test but is included in the tax test on benefits and, for someone on Medicare, in the premium income figure.

## Dates between now and January

- **November 18, 2026:** the deadline SSA gives for creating a my Social Security account and enrolling in paperless notices to see the COLA notice online.
- **Late November 2026:** COLA notices and the 2027 Medicare premium amount start appearing in my Social Security accounts.
- **December 2026:** paper COLA notices are mailed to people not enrolled in paperless notices.
- **December 31, 2026:** January's SSI payment, the first one at the 2027 amount.
- **January 2027:** the first Social Security payment that includes the 3.6% increase, for December 2026 benefits.

For working beneficiaries under full retirement age, SSA bases withholding at the start of the year on the 2027 earnings estimate it has on record.

## Common questions

**Does interest from a CD that matures in 2027 count toward the earnings limit?**

No. SSA's publication says it doesn't count "investment earnings, interest, pensions, annuities, and capital gains." Only wages, or net earnings from self-employment, count. The interest is still reported for income tax.

**I'm 68 and still working. Does the earnings test apply?**

No. The fact sheet lists the limit "Beginning the month an individual attains full retirement age" as "None." For people born January 2, 1960 or later, full retirement age is 67.

**Does the COLA raise the SSI resource limit?**

No. The fact sheet shows $2,000 for an individual and $3,000 for a couple in both 2026 and 2027, and POMS SI 01110.003 notes that the limits "have not changed since 1/1/89."

**Does interest on an SSI recipient's savings reduce the SSI payment?**

Not when it's earned on a countable resource such as an ordinary savings account. POMS SI 00830.500 treats that interest as excluded income. If it's still in the account the next month, it counts as part of the balance.

**When will I see my exact new amount?**

Online in my Social Security starting in late November for people enrolled in paperless notices, and by mail in December for everyone else. The notice shows the new benefit and any deductions, such as the Medicare premium.

**Are benefits withheld under the earnings test gone for good?**

For the worker, generally no. SSA increases the monthly benefit at full retirement age to account for withheld months. Spouses and survivors who receive benefits because they have a minor child or a child with a disability in their care don't get that increase.

For 2027, SSA's figures are a 3.6% increase, a $25,560 earnings limit, a $1,029 maximum federal SSI payment and an unchanged $2,000 resource limit. For people with Medicare premiums deducted from their benefits, the net January deposit also depends on the 2027 premium amount, which SSA says will appear in the Message Center starting in late November.
