![New I Bonds Start at 5.51%, and Their 1.40% Fixed Rate Is the Highest Since 2006](/assets/pin-images/2026-11-03-new-i-bonds-start-at-551-and-their-140-fixed-rate-is-the-highest-since-2006.png)

I bonds issued from Nov. 1, 2026 through April 30, 2027 earn a 5.51% composite rate for their first six months. That's the number at the top of [TreasuryDirect's I bond rate page](https://www.treasurydirect.gov/savings-bonds/i-bonds/i-bonds-interest-rates/), but the one I'd look at first is smaller: the fixed rate, 1.40%. It stays with each bond for as long as the bond earns interest, up to 30 years. Treasury hasn't set a fixed rate that high since November 2006, when it was also 1.40%. The last higher one was 1.60%, in November 2002.

Bonds sold from May through October got a 0.90% fixed rate and started at 4.26%. Anyone who bought in those six months is half a point behind a November buyer on the part that never resets. The 5.51% is also the highest starting rate since bonds issued from November 2022 through April 2023 opened at 6.89%.

[EE bonds](https://www.treasurydirect.gov/savings-bonds/ee-bonds/) issued in the new window earn 2.90%, up from 2.40%.

## The 2.04% comes from March and September prices

The inflation half of the rate isn't a forecast. Treasury takes the non-seasonally adjusted Consumer Price Index for All Urban Consumers, the CPI-U, and measures how much it changed over six months. The Bureau of Labor Statistics put the index at [330.213 for March 2026 and 336.940 for September 2026](https://data.bls.gov/timeseries/CUUR0000SA0). That's a rise of 2.037%, or 2.04% rounded, which is the figure Treasury uses. Annualized, it's 4.08%.

Six months earlier the same measurement came out smaller. Treasury's [May 1 announcement](https://www.treasurydirect.gov/news/2026/release-05-01-rates/) put the change from September 2025 to March 2026 at 1.67%, with the index going from 324.8 to 330.213. So the inflation piece went from 1.67% to 2.04% between the two resets.

Treasury publishes the formula too: fixed rate + (2 × semiannual inflation) + (fixed rate × semiannual inflation). With this round's numbers, that's 0.0140 + 0.0408 + 0.0002856 = 0.0550856, which rounds to 5.51%.

I find it easier to read as two steps. Every six months the bond grows by the six-month inflation figure, then by half the fixed rate on top of that: 1.0204 × 1.007 comes to about 1.0275, the same 5.51% once it's stated as an annual rate. So the 1.40% sits on top of the inflation rate Treasury measures with the CPI-U, multiplied in rather than simply added, which is why the total is 5.51% and not 5.48%. It isn't a floor. If prices fell, the composite could drop below 1.40%, though not below zero. "We stop at zero," as TreasuryDirect puts it.

It's also a lagging number. It describes prices from March to September 2026, and a bond issued between November and April earns it for its first six months.

## When a bond you already own switches

Older I bonds don't all move to the new rate on Nov. 1. Each bond's rate changes every six months from its issue month, and the 2.04% applies starting at the next of those dates. Laid out from TreasuryDirect's month table:

| If your bond was issued in | It picks up the 2.04% on |
|---|---|
| May or November | Nov. 1, 2026 |
| June or December | Dec. 1, 2026 |
| July or January | Jan. 1, 2027 |
| August or February | Feb. 1, 2027 |
| September or March | March 1, 2027 |
| October or April | April 1, 2027 |

Your bond keeps its own fixed rate, and that goes into the same formula. Treasury's full chart for older bonds was still the May 2026 edition when I checked on Oct. 31, so the composites below are my own arithmetic, using the published formula with 2.04%. To test the method, I ran the formula with May's 1.67% for every issue period on that May chart and matched all 57 published rates. Treat these as expected figures, and check your TreasuryDirect account after your bond resets.

| Fixed rate | Issued | Expected composite |
|---|---|---|
| 0.00% | May 2020 to Oct. 2022 | 4.08% |
| 0.40% | Nov. 2022 to April 2023 | 4.49% |
| 0.90% | May to Oct. 2023, and Nov. 2025 to Oct. 2026 | 5.00% |
| 1.10% | May to Oct. 2025 | 5.20% |
| 1.20% | Nov. 2024 to April 2025 | 5.30% |
| 1.30% | Nov. 2023 to Oct. 2024 | 5.41% |

The slowest to catch up is a bond bought this October. It started at 4.26% on Oct. 1, keeps that rate through March and moves to an expected 5.00% on April 1, 2027. One bought in May 2026 already switched on Nov. 1.

## One year next to savings, a CD or a bill

This is where the choice usually gets made, so I'll stick to numbers that are already known. You can't cash an I bond for the first 12 months, and before five years you give up the last three months of interest. Cash a November bond in November 2027 and the forfeit takes months 10 through 12, so you keep nine months of interest.

The first six of those months run at 5.51%. On $10,000 that's about $275 ($10,000 × 5.51% ÷ 2). Months seven through nine earn whatever composite the bond gets on May 1, 2027, and that rate can't go below zero, so those months can add to the $275 but can't take anything off it. That makes about $275 the floor for a one-year hold, before federal tax.

The [FDIC](https://www.fdic.gov/national-rates-and-rate-caps)'s national averages as of October 19, 2026 were 0.38% for savings, 0.63% for money market accounts and 1.76% for a 12-month CD. On $10,000 for a year, that's about $38, $63 and $176, if the savings and money market rates held. All three sit below the I bond's floor.

Averages hide a lot, though. The FDIC weights them by each institution's share of domestic deposits, so the biggest institutions move them the most, and they don't tell you what the best accounts pay. The comparison that counts is your own account. If it pays more than about 2.76% APY and stays there, the part of the I bond's one-year return that's already set won't beat it. Whether the bond pulls ahead depends on the May 2027 rate, and nobody knows that yet.

A Treasury bill gives you a known figure instead. Treasury's daily rate for the 52-week bill was 4.36% on Oct. 30, stated as a coupon equivalent, and an auction result can differ. I compared bill rates with the bank averages in [the Nov. 1 post on the Fed's hold](/2026/11/01/the-feds-oct-28-hold-at-375-to-4-two-votes-for-a-hike-and-what-savers-earn-now/).

If I knew I'd need the money in about a year, I'd leave the I bond out. Its floor beats the national CD average, but a CD or a bill tells you the number in advance, and the 12-month lock doesn't bend because your plans changed.

## Five years and up

After five years there's no three-month forfeit, so cashing the bond costs nothing extra. Every six months along the way, its rate resets with the newest inflation figure, and the 1.40% stays in the formula each time. The FDIC's national average for a 60-month CD was 1.41% on Oct. 19. That's the CD's whole rate. On the I bond, 1.40% is the part that comes on top of inflation.

Taxes help in some places too. TreasuryDirect lists federal income tax on I bond interest but no state or local income tax, and you can report the interest each year or wait until you get the money, which happens when you cash the bond or when it matures at 30 years. Savings and CD interest don't get the state exemption, so in a state with an income tax that's worth adding to the comparison.

What you give up is access for the first year, and a cap on how much goes in.

## The $10,000 limit runs by calendar year

Each Social Security number can buy up to $10,000 in electronic I bonds per calendar year, according to TreasuryDirect's [limits page](https://www.treasurydirect.gov/savings-bonds/how-much-can-i-spend-own/), with a separate $10,000 for EE bonds. Paper I bonds aren't an extra route anymore. Since Jan. 1, 2025, I bonds have been sold only electronically, and buying paper ones with a tax refund is gone.

The calendar matters this round. The 1.40% fixed rate covers everything issued through April 30, 2027, and the limit resets on Jan. 1. Someone with the full $10,000 still open for 2026 could put $10,000 in during November or December and, with 2027's allowance, another $10,000 from January through April, all at 1.40% fixed. A spouse has a separate limit. So does each child, since bonds bought for a child count against the child's limit, and a gift counts toward the recipient's limit in the year they get it.

Interest starts on the first day of the issue month, so a bond issued late in November earns from Nov. 1. Buying one now takes an ID.me login, the only way into TreasuryDirect online since Oct. 28. [The Nov. 2 post](/2026/11/02/treasurydirect-cut-off-its-old-login-on-oct-28-idme-is-now-the-only-way-to-log-in/) covers the setup and what keeps running if you haven't linked yet.

## A 0.00% bond next to a 1.40% one

Bonds issued from May 2020 through October 2022 carry a 0.00% fixed rate. At the new inflation figure they should earn 4.08% at their next reset, against 5.51% for a new bond. The gap is 1.43 percentage points (1.40 × 1.0204), and because both bonds get the same inflation figure, it stays close to 1.4 points as long as inflation is positive.

Switching has costs, and TreasuryDirect's rules spell them out:

- A bond under five years old loses its last three months of interest when you cash it. The 0.00% bonds issued from May 2020 through October 2021 are already past five years. One issued in November 2021 turned five on Nov. 1, 2026, and the 2022 bonds reach five during 2027, each in its own issue month.
- If you've been deferring federal tax, all the interest the bond has built up is taxable in the year you cash it.
- The new purchase counts against that year's $10,000 limit, and the 12-month lock starts over.

If I held one of the 2022 bonds, the issue month is the first thing I'd look up. Cashing a few months before the fifth anniversary costs three months of interest, and waiting removes that cost. Bonds issued from November 2021 through April 2022 reach five years while the 1.40% is still on offer. For bonds issued from May through October 2022, the anniversary comes after this window closes on April 30, 2027, and nobody knows yet what fixed rate will be on offer then.

## EE bonds at 2.90%

New EE bonds earn a fixed 2.90% for their first 20 years, up from 2.40%. That rate alone doesn't double the money in 20 years. By my arithmetic, $10,000 earning 2.90% a year, compounded every six months, reaches about $17,786 at year 20 ($10,000 × 1.0145^40). TreasuryDirect guarantees an EE bond will double in value in 20 years, "even if we have to add money at 20 years to make that happen." Held the full 20 years, that works out to about 3.5% a year. Cash it sooner and you get the 2.90%, with the same 12-month lock and the same three-month forfeit before five years. [An earlier post on the EE guarantee](/2026/09/28/the-ee-bond-guarantee-how-a-low-stated-rate-still-doubles/) goes through that math.

Before year 20 there's no top-up. Cash a new EE bond at year 10 and it has earned its stated 2.90% a year, compounded every six months. A new I bond keeps its 1.40% fixed rate over those ten years, but its composite resets with inflation every six months, so which one comes out ahead depends on inflation nobody has seen yet. The longer comparison with a high-yield account is in [the earlier post on the 12-month lock](/2026/09/24/i-bonds-vs-a-high-yield-savings-account-the-12-month-lock/).

If I were deciding this month, I'd weigh the 1.40% more than the 5.51%. The 5.51% lasts six months. The 1.40% stays as long as you keep the bond, up to 30 years.

The rates, formula and month table above are from TreasuryDirect's I bond rate page, its EE bond and limits pages and Treasury's May 1 announcement. The index values are from the BLS CPI-U series, and the savings and CD averages are the FDIC's national rates as of Oct. 19. Treasury's next scheduled rate announcement is May 1, 2027. The inflation figure set then is based on the CPI-U change from September 2026 to March 2027, and the fixed rate is set separately. Bonds bought from now through April keep the 1.40% either way.
