The [FDIC](https://www.fdic.gov/resources/deposit-insurance) rule governing trust accounts changed on April 1, 2024, and the new version is considerably simpler than the one it replaced. It also caps coverage in a way the old rules did not for some depositors, which is worth knowing if a structure was built around the previous formula.

## What changed

Before the change, revocable trusts and irrevocable trusts were separate insurance categories with different rules, and revocable trust coverage above five beneficiaries required an analysis of the trust's actual allocations. That analysis was complex enough that many depositors got it wrong in both directions.

The 2024 rule merged both into a single category called trust accounts. It covers formal revocable living trusts, informal revocable arrangements such as payable-on-death accounts, and irrevocable trusts, all under one formula.

## The formula

Coverage is $250,000 per owner, per beneficiary, capped at five beneficiaries, at each insured bank.

One owner with one beneficiary is insured to $250,000. One owner with three beneficiaries reaches $750,000. One owner with five or more beneficiaries reaches $1,250,000, which is the maximum for a single owner under this category regardless of how many beneficiaries the trust actually names.

Two owners, such as a married couple with a joint revocable trust, double it. Five beneficiaries and two owners produces $2,500,000 at one bank.

| Owners | Beneficiaries | Maximum coverage |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1 | $250,000 |
| 1 | 3 | $750,000 |
| 1 | 5 or more | $1,250,000 |
| 2 | 3 | $1,500,000 |
| 2 | 5 or more | $2,500,000 |

The cap is the part that changed for large trusts. A revocable trust with eight beneficiaries and equal allocations could previously support coverage past $1.25 million per owner. Under the current rule it cannot, and a trust holding a large deposit balance at one bank may now have uninsured funds it did not have before.

## Who counts as a beneficiary

A beneficiary is a person, a charity, or a nonprofit organization named to receive funds when the owner dies. The naming has to appear in the bank's records for an informal arrangement, or in the trust document for a formal one.

Several things do not qualify. A beneficiary who only inherits after another beneficiary dies is a contingent beneficiary and does not add coverage while the primary is living. A pet, however specifically provided for, is not an eligible beneficiary. An estate named as beneficiary does not multiply coverage.

The trust category also requires that the account be titled to show the trust relationship. A POD account has to be recorded as payable on death with the beneficiaries named. A formal trust account should be titled in the name of the trust. An account holding trust money but titled in the owner's personal name is insured as a single account, not a trust account.

## What this means in practice

For most households, the change is an improvement. A married couple with two children reaches $1 million at one bank through a simple POD designation on a joint account, with no trust document and no legal fees.

For larger trusts, three checks are worth running:

1. **Count beneficiaries against the cap.** More than five adds nothing to insurance coverage, though it may be exactly right for estate purposes.
2. **Confirm the titling at the bank.** The FDIC insures based on how the account is recorded, not on what the trust document says. This is the most common failure.
3. **Recheck balances against the new maximum.** A structure sized under the old rules may exceed the current cap.

## A word on the estate side

Insurance coverage is one consideration and not the most important one. A POD designation passes the account outside a will, overriding whatever the will says about it. That is efficient when it matches the plan and a genuine problem when it does not, particularly on accounts opened years earlier with beneficiaries who no longer reflect current intentions.

Beneficiary designations also outlive relationships. Reviewing them after a marriage, a divorce, a birth, or a death is worth more than any adjustment made purely to raise an insurance limit.

The FDIC's coverage estimator handles the trust arithmetic directly, using your account titling and beneficiary count, which settles the question faster than reasoning through the formula by hand.

## Formal trust or POD: which one to use

Both fall in the same insurance category now, so coverage is not the deciding factor. The differences are legal rather than insurance-related.

A payable-on-death designation is free, takes minutes at the bank, and passes the account directly to the named beneficiaries on death, outside probate. It offers no control over what happens next. The beneficiary receives the money outright, immediately, regardless of age or circumstance, and there is no mechanism for staging distributions or protecting a beneficiary who should not receive a lump sum.

A formal revocable living trust costs money to draft and requires accounts to be retitled into it. In exchange it can hold many kinds of assets under one set of instructions, can stage distributions over time, can name a successor trustee to manage assets during incapacity rather than only after death, and keeps the arrangement out of the public probate record.

For a household whose main asset is a deposit balance going to adult children in equal shares, POD designations handle it. Where there is real estate, a business, a minor beneficiary, a beneficiary with a disability, or any wish for distributions over time, the trust is doing work the designation cannot.

## Titling mistakes that cost coverage

The FDIC insures according to the bank's records. Several common errors break the intended treatment:

- **A trust account titled in the grantor's personal name.** It is insured as a single account, capped at $250,000 regardless of what the trust says.
- **Beneficiaries named in the trust document but never given to the bank** for an informal POD account. The bank's records are what count.
- **A beneficiary named on one account and assumed to cover others.** Designations are per account.
- **Outdated beneficiaries** following a death, divorce, or birth, which affects both the coverage math and where the money actually goes.
- **A trust naming an estate or a class of people** rather than identifiable individuals, which does not multiply coverage.

The fix in every case is a request to the bank to confirm how the account is titled and who is recorded as a beneficiary. That is a five-minute call and it is the only version of the answer that matters.

## Retirement accounts are separate

Certain retirement accounts, including traditional and Roth IRAs held as deposits, sit in their own insurance category with its own $250,000 limit. They do not combine with trust accounts, single accounts, or joint accounts at the same bank.

That is frequently the simplest available headroom for a household near the limit at an institution it wants to stay with, and it requires no restructuring at all, only an awareness that the balance is already in a separate bucket.
