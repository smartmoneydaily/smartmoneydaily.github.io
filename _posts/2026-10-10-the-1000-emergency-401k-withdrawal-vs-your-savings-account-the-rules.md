![The $1,000 Emergency 401(k) Withdrawal vs Your Savings Account: The Rules](/assets/pin-images/2026-10-10-the-1000-emergency-401k-withdrawal-vs-your-savings-account-the-rules.png)

Take a hypothetical worker in her 30s: she faces a $1,000 car repair, has about $400 in savings and roughly $9,000 vested in a 401(k), and sees a line on her plan's website about a "$1,000 emergency withdrawal." The short answer: that option, called an emergency personal expense distribution, removes only the 10% early-withdrawal tax. The money is still taxable income, the cap is $1,000 per calendar year, the plan has to choose to offer it (with a limited fallback on your tax return if it doesn't), and taking it can block a second one from the same plan for the next three calendar years unless the amount is repaid or contributed back. A pension-linked emergency savings account, or PLESA, is a different tool: a small Roth side account inside some 401(k) plans, open only to workers who are not highly compensated, with a cap of $2,600 for 2026 on the part of the balance that comes from your own contributions. Neither one replaces the first dollars of a cash emergency fund in an insured savings account, which carries none of these rules. Both can work as a backstop, and the 2026 allowance for the emergency distribution ends Dec. 31, 2026.

The rules below come from five IRS documents. [Notice 2024-55](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-drop/n-24-55.pdf) is the main guidance on emergency personal expense distributions, and the IRS page [Retirement topics - Exceptions to tax on early distributions](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/plan-participant-employee/retirement-topics-exceptions-to-tax-on-early-distributions) lists them with the other exceptions to the 10% tax. [Notice 2024-22](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-drop/n-24-22.pdf) explains PLESAs, and [Notice 2025-67](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-drop/n-25-67.pdf) sets the 2026 PLESA limit. [Retirement topics - Hardship distributions](https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/plan-participant-employee/retirement-topics-hardship-distributions) covers the older option these tools are often confused with. This is general information about how the rules work, not tax, legal or investment advice for any household.

## What the $1,000 emergency withdrawal is

The SECURE 2.0 Act added a new exception to the 10% additional tax on early retirement-plan withdrawals. The IRS exceptions page states the starting point: amounts withdrawn from an IRA or retirement plan before age 59½ are "early" or "premature" distributions, and "Individuals must pay an additional 10% early withdrawal tax unless an exception applies."

Notice 2024-55 calls the new exception an emergency personal expense distribution, shortened here to EPED. It defines one as "a distribution made from an applicable eligible retirement plan to an individual for purposes of meeting unforeseeable or immediate financial needs relating to necessary personal or family emergency expenses." It applies to distributions made after Dec. 31, 2023, so 2026 is the third calendar year it has existed.

Whether an expense qualifies depends on the facts. The notice lists factors that "include, but are not limited to," whether you or a family member have expenses relating to:

- medical care
- accident or loss of property due to casualty
- imminent foreclosure or eviction from a primary residence
- burial or funeral expenses
- auto repairs
- "any other necessary emergency personal expenses"

The paperwork is light. A plan administrator "may rely on an employee's written certification that the employee satisfies the conditions for an emergency personal expense distribution," and the notice counts an IRA trustee, custodian or issuer as an administrator for this purpose. In other words, the plan can accept your written statement that you qualify.

Which accounts can pay it? Notice 2024-55 says that "generally, a section 401(a) qualified defined contribution plan (such as a section 401(k) plan), a section 403(a) annuity plan, a section 403(b) annuity contract, a governmental section 457(b) plan, or an IRA is eligible to permit an emergency personal expense distribution." Defined benefit pensions are excluded.

The plan has to opt in. The notice says: "It is optional for an applicable eligible retirement plan to permit emergency personal expense distributions." Your employer's 401(k) may not offer them, and an IRA provider's withdrawal form may not list them. The notice does give individuals a fallback, covered in the hardship section below.

## How much you can take: the lesser-of rule

Notice 2024-55 sets three limits: one EPED per calendar year, no more than $1,000 in a calendar year, and a rule restricting later EPEDs, covered in the lockout section.

The $1,000 is fixed. A footnote in the notice says: "This $1,000 amount is not indexed for inflation." Unlike the PLESA cap and the other limits Notice 2025-67 adjusts, it does not rise with the cost of living.

The cap has a second test built in. Under Q&A A-5, the amount can't exceed "the lesser of $1,000 or an amount equal to the excess of" your total vested ("nonforfeitable") balance in the plan on the date of the distribution, over $1,000. For an IRA, the test uses your total interest in the IRA. The IRS exceptions page puts it in one line: "up to the lesser of $1,000 or vested account balance over $1,000." In practice, the EPED can't reach into the first $1,000 of the vested balance, so small balances get a smaller EPED or none at all.

**Worked example 1: the vested-balance test.** These balances are hypothetical except where noted.

- Vested balance of $1,600: $1,600 − $1,000 = $600. The lesser of $1,000 and $600 is $600.
- Vested balance of $9,000: $9,000 − $1,000 = $8,000. The lesser of $1,000 and $8,000 is $1,000.
- Vested balance of $900: there is no excess over $1,000, so nothing qualifies as an EPED from that account.
- The notice's own example: a vested balance of $1,500 gives $1,500 − $1,000 = $500, so a $500 request qualifies in full.

| Vested balance on the distribution date | Balance minus $1,000 | Maximum EPED |
|---|---|---|
| $900 | $0 (no excess) | $0 |
| $1,500 | $500 | $500 |
| $1,600 | $600 | $600 |
| $2,000 | $1,000 | $1,000 |
| $9,000 | $8,000 | $1,000 |

Source: Notice 2024-55, Q&A A-5. The $1,500 row is the notice's example; the others are hypothetical.

For 401(k) participants, the test counts only the vested part. Employer money that has not vested yet is left out, so check the vested figure on your statement rather than the total balance.

## The tax bill that still comes with it

The rule is easy to misread as a tax-free $1,000. It is not. Notice 2024-55 says: "An emergency personal expense distribution is includible in gross income, but it is not subject to the 10 percent additional tax under section 72(t)(1)." Regular income tax still applies to the taxable part. The notice also says the 10% additional tax applies only to the portion of a distribution included in gross income, so accounts holding Roth or after-tax money can work differently; the plan or IRA provider can explain its tax reporting.

Withholding also differs from most 401(k) payouts. Under Q&A A-11, an EPED is not treated as an eligible rollover distribution for the direct rollover, rollover-notice and mandatory withholding rules, and "the administrator or payor of the emergency personal expense distribution is not required to withhold an amount equal to 20 percent of the distribution, as generally is required in section 3405(c)(1)." The EPED "is subject to the withholding requirements of section 3405(b)" instead. The plan's distribution paperwork shows how much it will withhold, and that amount may not match the tax you owe for the year.

The distribution is reported on Form 1099-R. The IRS exceptions page covers the case where the form doesn't reflect the exception: "If you received a distribution that meets an exception, but box 7 on Form 1099-R doesn't show an exception, use Form 5329 to indicate the correct exception."

**Worked example 2: what $1,000 costs.** This example uses a hypothetical 20% combined income tax rate to keep the arithmetic clear. Actual rates depend on income, filing status and state.

*Option A, an EPED of $1,000:*

- Income tax: $1,000 × 0.20 = $200
- 10% additional tax: $0
- Net after tax: $1,000 − $200 = $800

*Option B, the same $1,000 as an early distribution with no exception:*

- Income tax: $1,000 × 0.20 = $200
- 10% additional tax: $1,000 × 0.10 = $100
- Net after tax: $1,000 − $200 − $100 = $700

The exception saves $100. It does not save the $200.

*Option C, trying to net $1,000:* At a 20% rate, $1,000 ÷ 0.80 = $1,250 would net $1,000 if no 10% tax applied. But only $1,000 can be an EPED, so the other $250 faces the 10% tax unless another exception applies.

- Income tax: $1,250 × 0.20 = $250
- 10% additional tax: $250 × 0.10 = $25
- Net after tax: $1,250 − $250 − $25 = $975

That falls $25 short. The first $1,000 nets $800, so the remaining $200 has to come from dollars taxed at 20% + 10% = 30%, which keep 70 cents each: $200 ÷ 0.70 ≈ $285.71. The total distribution is about $1,000 + $285.71 = $1,285.71.

- Income tax: $1,285.71 × 0.20 ≈ $257.14
- 10% additional tax: $285.71 × 0.10 ≈ $28.57
- Net after tax: $1,285.71 − $257.14 − $28.57 = $1,000.00

The cap applies to the distribution, not to what you keep. In this hypothetical, the most that can come out free of the 10% tax leaves $800.

Spending $1,000 of deposits from a savings account is a different kind of transaction. It is not a retirement-plan distribution, so none of these rules apply: no certification, no IRS dollar cap and no IRS limit on how often, though the bank's own account terms can still limit transfers.

## Repaying it and the three-year lockout

The EPED rules run two clocks, and they measure time differently.

**Repayment: three years from the day after you receive the money.** Q&A A-7 says you may, "at any time during the 3-year period beginning on the day after the date on which the distribution was received, repay any portion of an emergency personal expense distribution (up to the entire amount of the emergency personal expense distribution)" to a plan that can accept a rollover from you. Repayment is optional. Q&As A-13 and A-14 treat it like a rollover made in a direct trustee-to-trustee transfer within 60 days of the distribution, and Q&A A-12 says a plan that offers EPEDs must accept your repayment if you got the EPED from that plan and can make a rollover contribution to it at the time. Those answers don't spell out how a repayment affects tax already reported, so ask a tax preparer how to report one.

**Lockout: the next three calendar years.** Q&A A-6 says that after an EPED from a plan, "no amount of any subsequent distribution can be treated as an emergency personal expense distribution during the immediately following 3 calendar years with respect to that plan" unless one of two things has happened:

1. "the previous emergency personal expense distribution is fully repaid to the plan," or
2. your elective deferrals and employee contributions to the plan after the earlier EPED (for an IRA, your IRA contributions) add up to at least the part you haven't repaid.

The second path doesn't require writing a check to the plan. Ordinary payroll contributions after the EPED count. The notice's own example: an employee takes a $500 EPED on July 1, 2025, repays nothing, defers $3,500 by Aug. 1, 2027, and can then take a new $1,000 EPED from the same plan.

**Worked example 3: the two clocks.** The dates and amounts are hypothetical.

A worker receives a $1,000 EPED from her 401(k) on Monday, Nov. 2, 2026.

- Repayment window: begins the day after receipt, Nov. 3, 2026, and lasts three years, through Nov. 2, 2029.
- Lockout years for that plan: 2027, 2028 and 2029.

She repays $400 in 2027, leaving $1,000 − $400 = $600 unrepaid. Her deferrals since the EPED need to reach at least $600. At $150 a month, that's $600 ÷ $150 = 4 months. With no repayment, she would need $1,000 of deferrals: $1,000 ÷ $150 = 6.67, so 7 months ($150 × 7 = $1,050).

| Year | One-per-year rule | Same-plan lockout after a 2026 EPED |
|---|---|---|
| 2026 | EPED used | Not applicable |
| 2027 | One EPED allowed | Blocked unless fully repaid or contributions since the EPED at least equal the unrepaid amount |
| 2028 | One EPED allowed | Same condition |
| 2029 | One EPED allowed | Same condition |
| 2030 | One EPED allowed | Lockout over |

Source: Notice 2024-55, Q&As A-4 and A-6.

Note the wording. The one-per-year limit applies to the individual: "An individual is permitted to treat only one distribution per calendar year as an emergency personal expense distribution." The lockout is written "with respect to that plan." Anyone with several plans or IRAs should confirm how the two combine with the plan or a tax professional.

## How it compares with a hardship distribution

Hardship distributions are the older option, and the two are easy to confuse.

The IRS hardship page says a plan may allow them, "although not required," and that a distribution from an elective deferral account must be "Due to an immediate and heavy financial need" and "Limited to the amount necessary to satisfy that financial need." The employer decides whether the need qualifies under the plan terms. "Consumer purchases (such as a boat or television) are generally not considered an immediate and heavy financial need."

Tax treatment is the sharpest difference. The page states: "Hardship distributions are subject to income taxes (unless they consist of Roth contributions). They may also be subject to a 10% additional tax on early distributions." And: "Employees who take a hardship distribution can't: repay it to the plan, or roll it over to another plan or an IRA." An EPED avoids the 10% tax within its limits and can be repaid within three years.

Notice 2024-55 links the two in Q&A A-15. If your plan does not offer EPEDs and you receive "an otherwise permissible distribution that meets the requirements of an emergency personal expense distribution," you "may treat the distribution on the individual's federal income tax return as an emergency personal expense distribution to the extent the distribution meets the various limitations," claiming it on Form 5329. A footnote names a hardship distribution as one example. If you then want to repay, the same three-year window applies, and you may "repay the amount to an IRA."

So a hypothetical $3,000 hardship withdrawal from a plan with no EPED feature could have up to $1,000 treated as an EPED on the return, if it meets the definition and limits, while the other $2,000 follows the normal hardship rules. That is the general framework; a tax preparer can confirm how it applies to a specific withdrawal.

## Pension-linked emergency savings accounts (PLESAs)

The second SECURE 2.0 tool builds an emergency balance next to retirement savings instead of carving one out of it. Notice 2024-22 says: "In general, PLESAs are short-term savings accounts established and maintained in connection with a defined contribution plan and are treated as a type of designated Roth account." The plan keeps PLESA contributions and earnings in a separate account, and the rules apply to plan years beginning after Dec. 31, 2023.

**Who can join.** An eligible participant meets the plan's age, service and other requirements "and is not a highly compensated employee (as defined in section 414(q))." That holds "without regard to whether the individual otherwise participates in the plan," so a worker who doesn't contribute to the 401(k) itself can still be eligible. Someone who later becomes highly compensated can't add more but keeps the right to withdraw the balance.

**How you get in.** A plan may either "offer to enroll an eligible participant in a PLESA" or "automatically enroll an eligible participant in a PLESA pursuant to an automatic contribution arrangement." Under an automatic arrangement, a worker could be enrolled without signing up. If your employer holds a benefits enrollment period this fall, the plan materials should say whether a PLESA exists and whether enrollment is automatic.

**How you get money out.** A PLESA "generally must allow for withdrawal by the participant on whose behalf the account is established of the account balance, in whole or in part, at the participant's direction, at least once per calendar month," paid "as soon as practicable." A PLESA withdrawal is treated as a qualified distribution under the designated Roth rules, and the IRS exceptions page lists "distributions from a pension-linked emergency savings account (made after 12/31/2023)" as an exception to the 10% tax. Withdrawals are also allowed when you leave the employer or the plan ends.

Notice 2024-22 describes no certification, no list of qualifying expenses, no one-per-year rule and no lockout for PLESA withdrawals; they are made at the participant's direction, though the notice notes a plan is not required to allow more than one withdrawal a month. In that sense a PLESA behaves more like a savings account than an EPED does. But the sponsor controls its existence: "a plan which includes a PLESA may cease to offer such accounts at any time." Notice 2024-22 also limits how a plan can police frequent withdrawals. It lists as unreasonable any anti-abuse procedure that forfeits matches already made because of a PLESA withdrawal, suspends PLESA contributions after a withdrawal, or suspends matching on regular 401(k) deferrals.

## How the $2,600 cap and the match work

The PLESA limit is not an annual contribution limit. As quoted in Notice 2024-22, no contribution may be accepted "to the extent such contribution would cause the portion of the account balance attributable to participant contributions to exceed the lesser of (i) $2,500 or (ii) an amount determined by the plan sponsor of the PLESA." Notice 2025-67 updates the dollar figure: the limit "is increased from $2,500 to $2,600." For 2026 the cap is $2,600, or less if the sponsor sets a lower one.

Three things follow:

- It caps a balance, not a year's deposits. Once the contribution portion reaches the cap, new contributions stop being accepted.
- It counts only the part of the balance attributable to your contributions.
- A withdrawal can open room again. Notice 2024-22 says a sponsor may consider a participant as not manipulating the rules if the participant "made a $2,500 contribution in one year, received the matching contribution on such amount, and then took $2,500 in distributions that year and repeated that pattern in subsequent years."

**The match goes to the regular account.** If the employer matches contributions, it must match PLESA contributions "at the same rate as any other matching contribution on account of an elective contribution by the participant." The match does not land in the PLESA: it "will be made to the participant's account under the defined contribution plan which is not the PLESA." Matching on PLESA contributions can't exceed the maximum account balance for the plan year, and for any limit on matching, the match is "treated first as attributable to the elective deferrals of the participant other than contributions to a PLESA."

**Worked example 4: one hypothetical formula, two workers.** Real formulas vary and are often a percentage of pay. Suppose a plan matches 50 cents per dollar on the first $3,000 a participant contributes each year, with the full $2,600 PLESA cap.

*Worker A* puts nothing in the regular 401(k) and $100 per paycheck into the PLESA for 26 paychecks.

- PLESA contributions: $100 × 26 = $2,600, reaching the cap with the last paycheck
- Match: $2,600 × 0.50 = $1,300, deposited in the regular 401(k) account

*Worker B* already contributes $3,000 a year to the regular 401(k) and adds $2,600 to a PLESA.

- Regular deferrals use the formula first: $3,000 × 0.50 = $1,500
- The $3,000 ceiling is used up, so the PLESA adds $0 in match

Worker A builds an emergency balance and collects a match toward retirement. Worker B gets a liquid Roth balance but no extra employer money. The plan's actual formula decides which case applies.

Notice 2024-22 also says it "does not provide guidance on elements of section 127 of the SECURE 2.0 Act over which the Department of Labor has interpretive authority." For details beyond the tax rules above, the plan's own disclosures are the place to look.

## Savings account vs. the retirement-plan tools

The table sets the rules side by side. The savings-account column describes ordinary deposit-account features, not an IRS rule.

| Feature | Cash in a savings account | EPED from a 401(k), 403(b), governmental 457(b) or IRA | PLESA inside a 401(k)-type plan |
|---|---|---|---|
| Who decides if it's available | You | The plan or IRA provider (optional) | The plan sponsor (optional, can stop anytime) |
| Who can use it | Anyone with the account | Participants with a qualifying emergency expense, in a plan that offers it (or through the A-15 tax-return fallback) | Non-highly compensated employees can contribute; a worker who later becomes highly compensated can still withdraw |
| Amount | Whatever you've saved | Lesser of $1,000 or vested balance over $1,000, per calendar year | Contribution balance capped at $2,600 for 2026 (or lower plan limit) |
| 10% additional tax | Not applicable | Waived | Waived |
| Income tax on withdrawal | Not on your own deposits | Yes, on the taxable part | Treated as a qualified Roth distribution |
| Paperwork | No IRS paperwork; the bank's own procedures apply | Written self-certification | Withdrawal request, available at least monthly |
| Repay or refill | Any time | Optional, within 3 years | Contribute again up to the cap |
| Limit on repeat use | No IRS limit; account terms may limit transfers | Same plan blocked for 3 calendar years unless repaid or recontributed | No lockout; the plan may limit withdrawals to one a month |
| Employer match | No | No | At the plan's rate, paid to the regular 401(k) account |

Sources: Notice 2024-55; Notice 2024-22; Notice 2025-67; IRS exceptions page. One caveat on the 10% row: the IRS exceptions page says "Distributions from a governmental 457(b) plan are not subject to the 10% additional tax except for distributions attributable to rollovers from another type of plan or IRA," so for most governmental 457(b) money the EPED exception adds little on that front.

Only the cash column has no IRS eligibility test, no IRS cap and no plan rule between you and the money. That is the case for keeping the first dollars of an emergency fund in an ordinary deposit account. How large the fund should be is covered in [this site's guide to sizing an emergency fund](/2026/05/09/emergency-fund-basics-how-much-should-you-really-save/), where to hold it in [Where to Keep an Emergency Fund: HYSA, MMA, CD, or Cash](/2026/07/20/how-to-effectively-use-high-yield-savings-for-emergency-funds/), and how to confirm coverage before moving cash in [How to Verify a Bank Is Actually FDIC-Insured Before You Transfer](/2026/09/09/how-to-verify-a-bank-is-actually-fdic-insured-before-you-transfer/).

The plan tools can supplement that cash. An EPED reaches a small amount of retirement money more cheaply than a withdrawal that triggers the 10% tax, but it shrinks the retirement balance, adds taxable income and starts a three-year clock for that plan. A PLESA is closer to a savings account inside the plan, with a modest cap, Roth treatment and possibly a match, but only where the employer adds it and only for workers who are not highly compensated.

## Questions to ask before Dec. 31

Because the EPED limit runs on the calendar year, someone who has not used a 2026 EPED may still have one available through Dec. 31, 2026, if a plan offers it (or the A-15 fallback applies), the expense qualifies, the vested-balance test is met and no lockout from a 2024 or 2025 EPED from the same plan is still running. An unused 2026 allowance does not carry into 2027.

Questions for the plan administrator or IRA provider:

1. **Does the plan permit emergency personal expense distributions?** They are optional. If an employer plan doesn't offer them, ask whether hardship distributions are available, since Q&A A-15 lets an otherwise permissible distribution be treated as an EPED on your return.
2. **What is my vested balance?** The lesser-of rule uses the nonforfeitable balance on the distribution date.
3. **How much will be withheld?** The mandatory 20% does not apply to an EPED, but the section 3405(b) rules do.
4. **How do repayments work?** The window is three years from the day after you receive the money.
5. **Does the plan include a PLESA, and is enrollment automatic?** If so, ask about the plan's cap, the match formula and how often withdrawals are allowed.

At tax time, compare box 7 of the Form 1099-R with the exception you are claiming, and use Form 5329 if the code doesn't show it. A tax preparer can check how the distribution, any repayment and any Roth amounts fit together.

A few limits are fixed for planning purposes. The EPED cap is $1,000 and not indexed. The 2026 PLESA cap is $2,600. The IRS had not published the 2027 figure as of Oct. 4, 2026, so check the IRS's next cost-of-living notice before planning for 2027. A PLESA exists only if the employer adds one. An EPED feature is also optional for each plan or provider, although Q&A A-15 lets an otherwise permissible distribution be treated as an EPED on your tax return.

## Common questions

**Is the $1,000 emergency withdrawal tax-free?**

No. Notice 2024-55 says an emergency personal expense distribution "is includible in gross income, but it is not subject to the 10 percent additional tax." Only the additional 10% is waived. Regular income tax still applies to the taxable part, and the amount withheld may differ from what you owe.

**Can I take $1,000 in December 2026 and another $1,000 in January 2027?**

The one-per-year rule alone would allow one EPED in each calendar year. The lockout in Q&A A-6 adds a second test: after an EPED from a plan, no later distribution from that plan can be an EPED during the next three calendar years unless the first is fully repaid or your contributions since then at least equal the unrepaid amount. A January EPED from the same plan would depend on one of those. How the rules apply across different plans is worth confirming with the administrator or a tax professional.

**Does my employer have to offer the emergency withdrawal or a PLESA?**

No. Notice 2024-55 says it is "optional" for a plan to permit emergency personal expense distributions, and Notice 2024-22 says a plan with a PLESA "may cease to offer such accounts at any time." The IRS exceptions page marks the PLESA exception as not applicable to IRAs. Your plan administrator can say which features are included.

**Is a PLESA the same as an emergency fund in the bank?**

Not quite. It allows withdrawals at least monthly without the 10% tax, which brings it close to a savings account in daily use. But it is capped at $2,600 of your own contributions for 2026, or less under a lower plan limit, and only non-highly compensated employees can contribute. The employer can also stop offering it. It can serve as one layer of an emergency fund rather than the whole fund.

**Why would anyone use a hardship distribution instead of an EPED?**

A hardship distribution can be larger. An EPED is capped at $1,000 a year, while a hardship distribution is limited to "the amount necessary to satisfy that financial need," in the IRS hardship page's words. The trade-off is that hardship money may face the 10% tax and can't be repaid to the plan or rolled over. Under Q&A A-15, up to $1,000 of a qualifying distribution may still be treated as an EPED on your return if your plan doesn't offer EPEDs.

The retirement-plan tools are narrower and carry more rules than cash in a bank account. They still work as a second line, provided the plan offers them and you know the limits before the money is needed.

Run your own numbers instead: your balance, the APY on offer, and the period you plan to hold.
